VII Emblematic modes of expression
in the school drama
The close relationship between emblematics and Renaissance and Baroque
drama is well known, but the rhetoric of Baroque school drama and the role
of emblematics within it are, with a few honourable exceptions, less well
explored. When we consider that theatrical culture in Hungary during the
seventeenth and eighteenth centuries consisted almost exclusively of school
drama and that this was a field in which symbolic and emblematic forms
were absolutely central, it is clear that here is a topic that deserves a chapter
of its own. The scattered information that has come down to us in the form
of published texts and records of stage settings all suggests that emblematic
and related symbolic modes were vital to performances of plays such as
these, and that informed scrutiny of these same modes of expression will
make a significant contribute to our knowledge both of emblems and of
school dramas themselves.
For some time now, we have been aware that emblematics made a signifi-
cant contribution to the early modern drama and, more specifically, that Jes-
uit rhetoric, with its emphasis on argutia and its employment of pictorial
modes of thought and expression, was extremely influential right across the
board when it came to literature, arts, and the theatre. Acting in school plays
was part and parcel of training in eloquence and pupils were targets of peda-
gogical attention as well as mouthpieces of their teachers. Emblems often
accompanied performances, where they functioned as educational, didactic
tools. From the mid-seventeenth century, one can see how the school drama
turned biblical narrative and historical subject-matter into exempta with a
political significance that was conveyed through topical allegorical interpre-
tations; as the world of late humanism and the dramatic plots associated with
that world receded, so neo-Stoic, allegorical, and emblematic interpretations
moved in to fill the void. The emergence of works of this type was triggered
by collections of sample texts and the compilation of anthologies and works
of reference containing well-known quotations from the authorities. In order
to achieve the objective of the exercise, which was to win over the audience
to a particular point of view, the whole theatrical effort concentrated on
directing their emotional reactions; as a consequence, tropes assumed a par-
ticular significance because of their effectiveness at communicating pictures.
By the mid-eighteenth century, rhetoric was becoming less and less important

We know today that Schöne's insistence on a simple, all-embracing defini-
tion of what was and what was not emblematic begged a number of questions
about the earlier period and about the influences that led to emblematic
stage effects. Not all recent scholars have been happy with his view that
Baroque drama is fundamentally emblematic in structure, that it is a thea-
trum emblematicum in which >history became emblematized<. But, even so,
most research into emblematic influences in the drama follows the lines laid
down by Schöne. Dieter Mehl's work, for example, on three areas with pos-
sible links to the emblematic tradition and the drama (direct borrowings,
allegorical scenes, and symbolic props),6 and Peter M. Daly's research into
four such areas (props, rhetorical figures, actors and acting, and the stage),
partially overlap and are both heavily influenced by Schöne.7 The lesson we
can draw from research projects like these is that, rather than looking for
specific borrowings and direct influences, we would do far better to examine
the forms and functions of the emblematic method employed by dramatists
and to treat emblem books not as sources but as manifestations of habits of
mind that we can also see at work in the dramatic literature of the time.8
Some of the most recent work to be published includes warnings about the
one-sidedness of the emblematic approach to the analysis of dramatic genre.9
It is now quite clear that emblems do not furnish us with a master key that
will unlock the meaning of every play from the period; generally speaking,
they are not direct sources. What they did was to provide the dramatist with
certain parallels, analogies, and possibilities, and so, for us, emblem books
constitute a sort of repository or dictionary in which we can search for vari-
ants in meaning and usage. This more circumspect approach to Baroque
school drama has been shown to be effective in a number of recent studies
of the French and Polish traditions.10

The turn of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries saw the emergence
of a whole warren of connections between symbolic-emblematic motifs on
the one hand and plays written for stage performance on the other. Emblem-
atic set designs helped the audience understand what took place on stage,
while the action of the play itself, in turn, made the meaning of the stage set
that much more comprehensible. A good illustration of the early stages in
this process is Jan David's collection of meditations, entitled Occasio arrepta,
neglecta, huius commoda: illius incommoda, a work known in Hungary and
which gives us a Christianized version of Occasio with twelve engravings and
prose texts explaining them. But that is not all: the last of the emblems is
then followed by a play-text, also entitled Occasio, in which the twelve em-
blem pictures function as stage sets for the twelve scenes (schemata) of the
drama. The figures in the engravings are joined by personifications of Tem-
pus and Occasio and become actors on stage, their vestments are now cos-
tumes, and the inscriptions to the emblems become the titles of the scenes.11
This play is purely and simply a series of emblem explications rewritten in
dialogue form with the text distributed among the actors.
As we now know, seventeenth and eighteenth-century school theatre
made frequent use of symbolic and allegorical motifs and modes of expres-
sion. Symbolic expressions of this kind, now freed from the shackles of hu-
manist scholarship, not only became, to some degree, autonomous but also
gained a wider understanding; they proved a great fillip to the presentation
on stage of the heroic ideal in treatments of historical-political, moral, and
mythological themes. The development on stage of emblematic motifs was
also helped by the breaking of the stranglehold exerted by the rules of classi-
cal drama, the broadening of the range of possible subject-matter for the
stage, and the increasing complexity of the action itself. The very concept of
drama seemed to have undergone a change.12 That change affected not only
texts intended for performance before an audience but also declamations,
monologues, and tableaux vivants that had no text at all. As a result, more
attention began to be paid to the >hidden< motivations of the characters, to
representational argutia (or wit)< linked with the subject-matter of the play
or the occasion on which it was performed, and to the themes and moral
questions raised by the action on stage. All these shifts in emphasis paved
the way for a growth in emblematic modes of expression.13

Several factors make it a problem to assess the emblematic influences on
seventeenth and eighteenth-century school theatre in Hungary. A relatively
high proportion of the plays that were performed are known to us only from
their titles or from a surviving abstract (or perioche), and it is not always
possible, even where we do have a text, to reconstruct the performance or
the appearance of the stage from that text.15 Programmes and playbills are
much less useful to us than complete texts and, for declamations, a quite
different approach is needed. Most of the emblematic elements in school
drama are connected with the presentation of an allegory, or allegorical ele-
ments; to divorce these from their context is often impossible. Further diffi-
culties are posed by the nature of the occasion on which a piece was played,
the other paradramatic elements on display on that occasion, the lack of
consensus concerning the wide variety of technical terms employed, our lack
of knowledge concerning the intentions of the playwright/director and the
audience, and our incomplete understanding of the concept of the emblem-

Emblematics and dramatic theory
Most seventeenth-century literary theories, as set out in handbooks on poet-
ics, have a separate section on the drama. The standard starting-point for the
discussion would be Cicero's famous definition >Drama est imitatio vitae,
speculum consuetudinis, imago ventatisi This would then be followed by
references to other authorities, such as Aristotle, Julius Caesar Scaliger, Do-
natus, Martinus Antonius Delrío, and by a résumé and interpretation of clas-
sical theories on the drama.16 But the picture had begun to change even as
early as the end of the sixteenth century. This standard approach, founded
on classical and humanist precedents, had begun to give way to other ways
of looking at drama. In his poetics, Jacobus Pontanus (Spanmüller), for ex-
ample, suggested, in a passage in which he is discussing stage apparatus and
ornatus, that carefully chosen pictures might usefully be included as part of
the stage set to reinforce the action of the play, entertain the audience, and
contribute to the success of the performance.17
Those who came after Pontanus tended - whether or not they were con-
sciously following in the humanist tradition - to countenance an even more
marked departure from classical tradition: new questions were asked which
arose out of seventeenth-century stage practice, and among these was how
(and how far) a drama might make use of symbolic and emblematic elements.
The approach adopted by Martin Du Cygne is instructive. His ideas about
drama come hard upon the chapter of his Poetics in which he discusses the
emblem.18 He starts by quoting Cicero, Scaliger, and Donatus, and then
plunges into specifics, such as the prologue and the chorus, before devoting
a separate chapter to the methodology of playwriting. He fashions this novel
approach on a definition of drama that was quite different from Cicero's:
>Est plurium personarum collocutio cum gestu et actionem For Du Cygne,
mythological figures - the personifications of ideas and genres, the so-called
>mirabiles res< - are essentially pictures or series of pictures that help an
audience understand a play. Their place is, above all, in the choruses because
these deal not with plot or action but with the central theme of the play (>ex
idea argumenti vel totius fabulae<). The link between the >res mirabiles< and
what happens on stage comes from the fact that word and picture only make
sense when they are interpreted together, and he advocates the use of what
were known as the scenae mutae: scenes which, just like an emblem picture,
the audience had to decode and interpret. Every act (actus), he says, may
have its own scena muta, but these may most usefully be placed at the begin-

dat<, >favet sors<, or >fovet sors< point to abstract ideas we commonly en-
counter in emblematics and, in doing so, they conjure up pictures. Combina-

(>rota Fortunae<, >virtutis ardor<), are open to emblematic interpretation and
also suggest pictorial associations.55 Paratactical word order, such as we find
in expressions like >lauri et olivae conjunction6 stimulate the visual imagina-
tion of the audience via the use of symbols.
Emblematic roles, characters, and personifications
Emblematic influences most easily made their presence felt when a stage
action had an abstract significance and the need to play a character in a way
commensurate with that character's traditional image made for symbolic,
rather than >realistic< acting. Most allegorical roles in a fictitious drama sym-
bolicum are representations of abstract ideas; as such, they are perfectly in
keeping with the nature of the performance itself and have no meaning be-
yond themselves. Roles such as these become symbolic not because of what
the players do and say but primarily because of what they are and what they
look like. In the Religio periclitans, for example, Fides enters bedecked with
badges (insignia) and, in the course of the scene, these are taken from her,
as she is seized and bound, a crown is placed on her head bearing the motto

In dramas performed >sub symbolis<, the lead often becomes a symbol for
something above and beyond the character we see on stage, with the result
that he or she, while still playing the role demanded by the action on stage,

emblematic sense of the play. In the Judith drama performed in 1649 at Na-

scenes (sc. 3 and sc. 16) all suggest an emblematic interpretation of the lead-

comes to symbolize not the biblical heroine but the Virgin Mary, patron saint
of Hungary, who is seen ascending into Heaven. This allegorical interpreta-

so-called Gualbertus drama can be taken simply as a play about Joannes
Gualbertus: when the performance is structured around the representation
of the Passion, then the play is clearly about the Saviour and the main role
represents Christ's suffering on the Cross.59
Characters can also be considered emblematic where the cast-list indi-
cates that actors effectively played two roles at once. In such > double cast<
plays, the character on stage behaves, so to speak, as the imago of the other,
>hidden< role, and plays that alongside his own. In 1708, for example, at
Kézdivásárhely-Kanta (Tîrgu Secuiesc), the Minorites staged a play about
the Spanish War of Succession. In it, both Hircanus and Silvanus represented
France but also symbolized ambitio and dolus respectively; the other charac-
ters, all named after syntactical concepts, also represented various virtues
and sins.60

sky with a telescope, is very familiar to us from emblematics; and the first
scene of the play, in which the disaster foretold by the astrologer takes place,

sumed an emblematic meaning thanks to the scenery on display. Not only is
the proscenium and the entire action of the Johannes Schwartz play we just
referred to emblematic in character but so also are several of its sets. In the
second act, for example, the main character, Constantius, is shown asleep, on
the night before his victory, beneath a palm tree on which two singing angels

symbolizes the slumbering hero, and the wreath placed upon it gives us an
indication that Constantius is about to win a great victory. In this scene, the
stage set and the character are blended together into a single emblematic
picture, and the song the angels sing helps the audience to appreciate what
is going on.
