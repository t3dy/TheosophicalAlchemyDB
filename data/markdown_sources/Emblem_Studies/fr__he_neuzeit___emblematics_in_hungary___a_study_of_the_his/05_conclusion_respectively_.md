The thematic range of the emblems Whitney borrowed from Zsámboky
is consistent both with his general selection criteria and his personal interests.
There are a number of emblems on various vices (e.g. hatred, enmity, gullibil-
ity, lust for glory) and virtues (dutifulness, trust, love of children, patriotism),
and several pieces on the absurdities and dangers of life and the need to be
prepared for them (e.g. the power of Chance, the turns of Fortune). Other
recurrent topics include the need for leisure and a critique of avarice, guile,
intemperance, and self-destructive passion. Several of the borrowings treat
commonplaces and topoi (>there is a time for everything<; transience; appro-
priate use of earthly goods), but Whitney also adapted some pieces on
themes that are relatively rare in emblematic literature, such as a complaint
about the lack of patronage, the advantage of distance when building a repu-
tation, or the idea of beauty as a weapon.
It has recently been suggested that Whitney's collection is based on the
concept of human life as a pilgrimage, a journey to reunion with God, a
sort of spiritual voyage.38 The prevalence of >ethereal ideology< in Whitney's
treatment of themes seems to justify this. Certain similarities with the compo-
sition of de Montenay's book, and the number of emblems about pilgrimage
and related ideas and values that Whitney took from de Montenay, may also
point in this direction. Yet considered as a whole, Whitney's collection is a
complex example of thematic and structural stratification, with polysemantics
as a fundamental feature, involving various attempts in emblematic pattern-
ing.
A further important feature is that there are numerous comments and
quotations in the margins. These support, reaffirm, and explain the epigrams
to an extent that is unusual in emblem-books. In line with humanistic stand-
ards, this running commentary displays the author's erudition, and its length
bears witness to the impact of earlier and contemporary annotated emblem
books. Some of them are important for today's researchers, because they
refer to otherwise unknown sources used by emblematists, including Zsám-
boky, whose works Whitney exploited; they can thus help us to identify the
sources of his so-called >newly devised< emblems. More than five-sixths of the
414 annotations are, however, direct quotations from classical and medieval
authors; the rest come from comments and marginalia in other emblem
books.39

of the Emblemata. The Choice was neither reprinted nor translated in the
sixteenth century. Some of the surviving copies show traces of intensive use,40

Whitney's practice of imitation and adaptation
It is difficult to establish which of the expanded Emblemata editions Whitney
used. Theoretically, it could have been any of the four editions published
between 1566 and 1584, or the French translation or even a combination of
one or more of these. The differences between the various editions of Zsám-
boky have yet to be studied. Another problem is the complex structure of
Whitney's book: sometimes he treats the individual emblem as a basic unit
which can be placed anywhere more or less at random, but there are also
some subordinate series (as happened in the later Jesuit collections) which
had to be kept together. However, as the number of editions of Zsámboky
that need to be examined is much smaller than the number of pre-1586
Alciati editions, for example, there is some hope that this question will be
answered eventually.
In Whitney's manuscript, 155 of the 184 emblems that later reappear in
the printed version have the same mottoes as those of their sources. In the
printed edition, only 79 mottoes differ from their source emblems.42 Eleven
of the changes are minor: for example, Ζ 14, Conscientia integra, laurus (Lau-
rel means a clear conscience) becomes W 67, Murus aeneus, sana conscientia
(A wall of brass is a clear conscience); Ζ 204, Caelum, non animum mutant
(They change the climate, not the mind) results in W 178, Caelum, non ani-
mum (The climate, not the mind). Such changes are mainly designed to avoid
inconsistencies between Zsámboky's mottoes and the texts of Whitney's epi-
grams. Synonyms are found, moral messages are given added emphasis, and
motto and epigram are more closely linked.

in the manuscript but changes it in the printed version. In the following ex-
ample, a poem adapted from Paradin (Symbola heroica, Antwerp 1567) ap-

woodcut. Instead it is a repetition of the closing words of the quotation from
Horace (Carm. 2.10) which is appended to Whitney's epigram on page 59.
In order to bring this new motto into line with the epigram, Whitney's poem
has an added third verse that has no equivalent in Zsámboky.
Thanks to Whitney's draughtsman, most of the pictures and epigrams in
the manuscript version are linked with each other. Forty-four of his drawings
are based on woodcuts in Zsámboky's Emblemata. Most of them are almost
exact copies, but three differ markedly because they take account of Whit-
ney's textual divergences from the Zsámboky original in the epigram. In the
printed book, on the other hand, the relationship of text and image is occa-
sionally strained, because Whitney (as already indicated) substituted prints
of the original woodcuts for all drawings, even for those drawings which
departed from the original in order better to match his texts.44 In his hurry
to have his work published, Whitney did not take the time to remedy this
situation by rewriting the relevant texts accordingly.

in his epigram remain unconnected with the reprinted image. Tensions such
as these between Zsámboky's woodcuts and Whitney's epigrams must have
puzzled contemporary readers of Whitney's book.
The changes Whitney made from the manuscript to the printed book con-
firm the observation that he handled his sources in a highly independent
fashion. He evidently did not feel bound by his own first solutions either. A
direct statement in the manuscript may become indirect in the printed book,
and a literal translation may be transformed into a paraphrase. Elsewhere,
he recasts his original distichon as a marginal annotation, while rewriting the
main text. Many such changes improve on the manuscript version: messages
are easier to understand, causal relationships are emphasized, the poetic lan-
guage is condensed and more economical, the rhyme pattern made more
attractive, and the relationship between text and picture is closer.
Whitney uses several techniques to adapt the epigrams. In about half of
them, he stays fairly close to the source. For the rest, his preferred method
by far is expansion; he expands three times more often than he shortens. The
most extreme instances of both expansion and shortening can be found in
the texts adapted from Alciati and Zsámboky. This is where we see the great-
est variety of length. Whether lengthening or shortening, Whitney tends to
leave the moral as it is. In the shortened texts, he omits or simplifies details
and references, while expansion mainly serves to give him more detail and
allow him to apply the moral more explicitly. Expanded texts also present
an opportunity to exhibit his culture and his personal interests, particularly
in the context of topoi and commonplaces. Adaptation of the original content
is always an option: in one instance an eight-line stanza is expanded into
twenty-five couplets and, in another, eight sextets grow out of a four-line
stanza.

When Whitney translates and renders the full content of the originals, he
refrains from radical shortenings and omits only the mythological references,
the names of places and the occasional auxiliary motif, while summarizing
the moral more concisely.45 A good example of a translation and interpreta-
tion this kind which sticks close to the original is epigram Ζ 98-W 22, enti-
tled Nullus dolus contra casum (No cunning against chance; see Fig. 46). The
warning of unexpected danger is conveyed by an >exemplum jocosum< with
a precise geographic location: the story of the fox trapped on an ice-floe in
the Danube.46 Zsámboky's dactylic epode of nine Alcmanic couplets, well-
suited to the epic form, is rendered by Whitney into four stanzas of iambic
pentameters. The adaptation is as brisk as the original. In both versions, the
tale is told by someone close to the event. Zsámboky opens with a poetic
question: >Who would not be amazed by the curious spectacle that could
recently be seen on the melting Danube?<, whereas Whitney gets right down
to the story. In the second and third pairs of lines, Zsámboky captures the
reader's attention by describing the circumstances in great detail. Then he
halts the narration and evokes an ancient visual topos·. Phoebus's chariot.
Whitney retains the classical reference, but his description of the scene is less
precise. The concluding moral (>one should always be cautious<) is hidden
by Zsámboky inside another question addressed to the reader: >Now tell me,
what precaution can protect from chance, what ruse could prevent such an
event from happening? < Whitney does without the question and lets the same
message be spoken by the people who catch the fox. He attaches to this a
quotation from Seneca. Unlike Holger Homann, who considers the piece an
illustrated anecdote rather than an emblem in the strict sense (because the
real story can hardly be told in a single image, and because other animals
can be imagined in the place of the fox), we think this piece is a good illustra-
tion of the close connection between exemplum and emblem.47 The use of
the fox is of course deliberate: his proverbial cunning, implied by Zsámboky
and made explicit by Whitney, underscores the lesson in ironical fashion.
Substantial structural transformation of epigrams can be found where
Whitney turns a text into dialogue or opens with a moral that Zsámboky
only offered at the end of the tale. In other instances, ancient examples which
Zsámboky gave in his introduction are appended to general considerations,
additional examples are omitted, or the two parts of an explicatio are
switched.48 The theme of Zsámboky's emblem on the danger of false friend-
ship had been popular with emblematists since it first appeared in Corrozet's

enemy, because a fox, apart from openly offering his hand, also wears his

strikes unexpectedly. Whitney either completely misunderstands the topos of
the fox as it is used by Zsámboky, or he deliberately ignores it (possibly an

consist of a paraphrase of a saying by Bias (of the Seven Sages), and the line

Another of his favourite ploys for expansion is to single out and then
interpret details in an emblem picture which Zsámboky left unmentioned.
The details may be ancient gods like Kronos, Mercury, or Bacchus,50 they
may be symbolic figures such as a court jester,51 or even symbolic motifs
taken from nature, such as a crocodile, a river, or a spruce.52
Brevity is most frequently achieved by omitting Zsámboky's mythological
references. Thus, in the epigram about a clear conscience,53 Whitney leaves

Before concluding, it is worth taking a brief comparative look at the
French translation of Zsámboky's work by Jacques Grévin, as well as at
Whitney's adaptations from other works, namely Alciati and the first and
perhaps most influential French emblem book, Le théâtre des bons engins
(Paris 1539) by La Perrière. As Alison Adams has shown, Grévin, who was
personally acquainted with Zsámboky (they met during the latter's second
stay in Paris), made a conscious effort to make Zsámboky more easily under-
standable, while retaining his stylistic effectiveness.64 He resembles Whitney
in this. Moreover, as in his translations of Junius's work,65 Grévin also simpli-
fied and omitted scholarly references, while adding emphasis to the morals
and to the emotional elements. He also tried to achieve a great variety of
rhythmic patterns in his poetry. His translation shows that the woodcuts in-
fluenced him to some extent. However, as Grévin was commissioned by
Plantin, his task was essentially unlike Whitney's, and he was obliged to stay
much closer to the source. But like Whitney, he used stanzas, and attempted
to convey some of the patterns of the original by playing with the word
order. He also made an effort to replicate the pace and rhythm of Zsámbo-
ky's poems, though some of his solutions are rather weak and others are
extremely brief.
In her analysis of Whitney's Alciati adaptations, Mary Silcox has pointed
out some changes in content (for example, Christian references, occasionally
Protestant, appear; there are topical patriotic elements; emphasis is given to
social cooperation: and Whitney sees women in a more positive light), as well
as a number of typical formal modifications.66 According to Silcox, Whitney
handled both Alciati's texts and Mignault's comments (first published at
Paris in 1571) with a great deal of freedom and originality. His expansions
tend to reinforce the moral messages and to address the reader directly, ex-
horting him to action. Scholarly references are omitted, remarks aimed at a
specific readership are left out, and the scope of both subject matter and
interpretation becomes wider and more inclusive. All this suggests that Whit-
ney was aiming at a wider audience than Alciati. The wit, brevity, and enig-
matic quality of the originals are sometimes victims of the heavy emphasis

Whitney's seven (or some would say eight) adaptations of La Perrière
were made along the same lines as those of Zsámboky.67 Alison Saunders

that there is no significant difference in his methods when adapting from
Latin or the vernacular. He frequently expands the originals by the addition
of poetic images and descriptive or narrative details, primarily to make his
verse more worked and ornate. Again, he uses the source as a starting-point.
A new moral interpretation evokes new associations, and may shift the bal-

mottoes, occasionally transforms the structure, and adds new information.
While his adaptations are no less didactic than the source material, his didac-

of the literary importance of his source and adds Latin mottoes and anno-
