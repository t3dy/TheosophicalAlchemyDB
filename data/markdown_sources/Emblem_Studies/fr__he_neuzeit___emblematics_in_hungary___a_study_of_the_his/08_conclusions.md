## Section: Conclusions

Conclusions
The main incentive as well as the principal source for emblematic elements
on stage comes in the révélant passages of works of a theoretical stamp and
in the many anthologies of symbolic and emblematic material and the speci-
men plays these include. There seem to be no denominational barriers of
any significance to the introduction of emblematic material nor is it associ-
ated with any particular religious order. Lutherans and Catholics alike had
a taste for the various symbolic modes of expression at their disposal. In
Calvinist and Unitarian performances, symbolic material is possibly less in
evidence, but this is probably because of their own theological views about
the role and function of the visual arts. It is true that we still not have the
definitive picture when it comes to school drama in Hungary and still do not
know the exact ratio of Latin plays to those in the vernacular, but, as far as
we can tell, there is more symbolic material in the Latin dramas, while the
vernacular pieces employed a greater range of visual effects.
The extent of the influence of emblematic modes of expression and hid-
den symbolic material, as well as the precise forms these take and the extent
to which they affect school drama are still, to some degree, uncharted waters.
The drama symbolicum and the purely emblematic declamation are rela-
tively rare, while plays which are effectively generic hybrids in this respect
(the so-called drama mixtum) are relatively common as late as the second
half of the eighteenth century. The greatest percentage of drama fictum was
staged in the second half of the seventeenth century, the so-called Golden
Age of emblematics in Hungary. Symbolic elements affecting the whole of
a play are best seen in the plot-summaries and the action on stage, while
performances tended to acquire a symbolic aura when the scenery had a
direct bearing on the way the play was interpreted.
From the second half of the seventeenth century until the middle of the
eighteenth, there seems to have been a predilection for the staging of histori-
cal subjects, accompanied by a series of >framing< scenes presenting abstract
ideas. These last stressed the symbolic-allegorical purpose of the piece and
so facilitated its moral-cum-didactic interpretation. Such performances were
not based on two separate >stories<; what they did was stage the same story
and then invite reaction and interpretation on two levels and of two separate
kinds. Allegorical presentations of personified ideas and mythological char-
acters served to intensify the spectacle and drive home the moral lesson to
be drawn from the play. The subject-matter of plays like these would not be
affected even were one to jettison the >framing< scenes.
From the 1730s onward, the proportion of symbolic material begins to
lessen, and abstraction features less and less in the stage action. Even leaving
to one side for the moment the question of allegories affecting complete
performances, there is no doubting, throughout the period under consider-

Symbolic and emblematic elements were fashionable in Hungarian school
theatres for close on two hundred years. The pictorial tradition was linked,
often directly but sometimes only indirectly, with various facets of the drama
and affected in equal measure action, text, characters, scenery, and audience
reaction. It furnished the audience with some idea of what they were about
to see, provided them with background information on the events related,
helped them to relate to what was going on, and established a bond between
the characters (or actors) and their audience.

VIII The emblematic mode and the sermon
The main role of emblematic material in early modern rhetoric is as a store-
house of subjects, associated traditions, and pictorial motifs on which a writer
may draw to enhance the ornatus of his text. Most of the time it is looked
upon as an integral part of the discipline of inventio and elocutio·, yet, at the
same time, the emblem is not seen as an independent figura in the full sense
of the term.1 In trope theory and in those sections in manuals of rhetoric
that deal with metaphor and allegory, there is, by and large, no reference to
the emblem as a related form or a special variety of allegory. In topos theory,
the emblem serves as a likely source of topoi, alongside dicta, devices, sym-
bols, hieroglyphics, and coats of arms. The boundaries between all these re-
lated forms often become blurred, since their function in oratory is identical:
they are at the same time the subject of imitatio and an example of it. Manu-
als of rhetoric often refer to the role of the emblem in arousing attention
and emotions, as well as in fixing things in the memory; and they cite, as the
main sources for such material, emblem books and emblem compendia.2
In sermon theory and homiletic practice during the first half of the seven-
teenth century, the hermeneutical approach, interpreting the Scriptures and
nature allegorically, was transformed into a specifically emblematic method.
Its basic principle was the spiritual interpretation of images and subjects
taken from the Bible and/or from nature, of references to God and the his-
tory of Man's redemption, and of the events and people involved in that
history. Its practical expression is the sermon adorned with clear emblematic
elements, and the so-called >emblematic sermon< structured around an un-
swerving concentration on a single picture, object, or creature and its spiri-
tual interpretation. In the Altdorf academy, emblematic laudatory orations
were regularly held as early as the second half of the 1570s. The texts of
these orations - together with the drawings of the medals showing the motifs
from which they are derived - were regularly published. In Lutheran circles,

Our opening survey of the sources of both the theory and the teaching of
emblematics indicated that, in Hungary, the Golden Age of emblematics
lasted from the second half of the seventeenth century through to the 1730s
and that this mode of thought and expression was particularly prominent in
the Jesuit curriculum. Further evidence for a close connection between ser-
mon literature and emblematics is provided by the fact that Jesuit library
catalogues normally classified emblematic printed material alongside ser-

In this chapter we shall focus on the emblematic influences on sermon
literature that emerged during this Golden Age of emblematics in Hungary.
We shall consider in turn the variants, characteristics, functions, uses, and
sources of rhetorical forms of expression that were held to be emblematic,
in accordance with the theory and practice of the time. The authors examined
are not particularly well known but they are representative of Catholic ser-
mon writing and, more specifically, of the Jesuit sermon. We shall have occa-
sion from time to time to refer to sermons that predate this Golden Age, as
an intensive use of pictorial expressions can be detected even in the earlier
period.

It would seem, however, that Baranyi's emblematic practice is based on
foundations that go back even earlier than humanist and late humanist theo-
ries. Evidence for this comes in the very many protoemblematic, mnemotech-
nical devices he employs, such as heraldic interpretations, similes, and sym-
bolic and allegorical interpretations of pictures. This use of pictorial
references in Baranyi's sermons accords with an overall Aristotelian concept
of inventio-imitatio such as we find in summary form in Joannes a Sancto
Geminiano.
According to this theory, images and pictures drawn from nature can be
usefully employed in demonstrations of moral actions and human behaviour.
External things (res extrinsecus) lend themselves to the interpretation of hu-
man actions. They can be both exempta and pictorial similes. The preacher
can safely use them, because they give pleasure and, at the same time, can
act as vehicles for a sensory representation of spiritual interpretations and
>hidden matters< (subtilia). The most important consideration is choosing the
correct similitudo and fitting it properly into the sermon.18
Joannes a Sancto Geminiano based his ideas on the three key Aristotelian
concepts of sensus, imaginatio, and ratio. He states that it is the imagination
that must supply the evidence for the things that are being linked or com-
pared actually belonging together by dint of some pictorial similarity. In this
respect, the most important rules - and Baranyi endeavoured to obey
them - are: (i) the pictures involved in the similitudo may be (a) natural or
artificial, (b) actual pictures, or (c) visions and phantasms; (ii) the correlation
between the picture and the idea or object to which it is compared must be
persuasive; (iii) the comparison should help move the sermon forward; and
(iv) it must seize and hold the attention of the congregation. Anticipating
the systematized indexes one finds later in the emblem encyclopaedias, the
Liber de exemplis et similitudinibus rerum came complete with two indexes
to make it easier to use: one ordering the contents by pictures and objects,
and the other doing so by groups of possible meanings and interpretations.
The introduction to the sermons of István Csete was, in all probability as
we say, the work of János Gyalogi. In it, he develops the theory of preaching
above and beyond the triple function of the sermon: docere, delectare, mo-
vere. Part III of the introduction considers the role of pleasure in all this and,
in one section of it, we are given a résumé of the received wisdom concerning
those pictorial forms that may best be built into a sermon. The central idea
of Part III is that comparatio is a important catalyst of pleasure. Gyalogi
advances, as his fourth source of delectatio, the use of pictorial comparisons.

other Hungarian orators to use emblematic material) refers to one of the
most popular and influential seventeenth-century authors of emblem ser-

are recorded in many Hungarian libraries at this period (see Fig. 57). For
Engelgrave, usefulness is more important than pleasure; and a useful sermon
is one whose meaning is clear (>Oratio sit talis, ut res potius, quam se osten-

(>nos humili stilo, et tenui modulamur avena<) in his comments on Chrysolo-

>whose meaning is clear< as being shot through with colourful and attractive
pictures. The key component in achieving this is, he suggests, the emblem, as
it makes the sermon almost visible to the eye, more easily apprehended by
the senses, and accordingly more >useful<.
For Engelgrave, in an emblematic sermon the emblem introduces the ser-
mon and is part of the argument (propostilo). The motto above the emblem-
atic picture is identical with the topic of the sermon, and the sentence be-
neath the picture, naming and giving a brief interpretation of it, anticipates,
so to speak, the substance of the sermon and also furnishes a transition to
the textual part of the propositio. The moral of the emblem presented at the
beginning of the oration is identical with the central theme of the sermon.

symbols, or enigmas, may also appear, their function normally being subordi-
nate or complementary to that of the main picture. In this fashion, according

Types of emblematic expression
In the whole welter of emblematic modes of expression, there are two chief
types, and they are easily distinguishable from each other, even in strictly
structural terms. The first is the symbol consisting of picture description and
motto, and the other the emblem consisting of motto and explanation, or -
to put it another way - symbol and description. In practice, however, there
is a whole variety of different types and forms.
These differences in nomenclature are largely the product of the idiolect
of the individual author involved. It is clear that, at the time, a host of terms
for emblematic expressions were in current use and different writers attached
different meanings to them. It is no easy matter to distinguish clearly be-
tween one emblematic form and the next: all had their origins in protoem-
blematic forms or in the variants ushered in by Jesuit emblematists.
I. Protoemblematic expressions are characterized by a powerful pictorial el-
ement with figurative meaning. Their common characteristic is that they do
not assume the forms we find prescribed in the rules on emblematics and for
this reason, even though they are very similar to forms we term emblematic,
they cannot be called emblematic expressions in the strict sense of the term.
There are two main types of protoemblematic expression:
(i) Thinking in pictures, or employing pictorial parallels and associations.
There are plenty of examples in the sermons of Péter Pázmány and György
Káldi. These pictures differ from the literary pictures we find in sermons,
principally in that they are intended to convey more than their surface mean-
ing and do so in a different fashion. The motif of the compass,28 common
enough in emblematic material, was used, for instance, by Pázmány as a
pictorial parallel: >The little piece of iron, one end of which was forged on a
magnet, will not come to rest until it points directly North, as we see in the
compass. Our soul, too, was forged by God: God is its ultimate goal, and it

Use and functions of the emblematic mode
The greatest variety of terminology in Hungarian to describe emblematic
compositions comes in István Illyés's two-volume Sertum sanctorum (1708)
and his Fasciculus miscellaneus (1710).52 In his sermons, Illyés continually
employed the emblematic mode, doing so on the grand scale and with great
confidence. His favourite form was the symbol. He would start with a symbol,
invent a series of variations on it - each of which would unlock the door to
another, related form - and also employed transitional figures. His use of
concepts is more consistent than that of any other writer of sermons, and
only relatively rarely did his practice run counter to his own theory. There is
not a single emblematic form or function in any sermon of the period that
cannot be also found in Illyés.
We have already indicated that, for the sermon writer, there are two basic
forms of emblematic material: the sermon structured around a single em-
blematic picture (the emblematic sermon) and the use of emblematic motifs.
In the second option, the use of motifs, the emblematic element - whether
it is invoked as an example because of its sensory appeal or whether what is
being harnessed by the writer is its intellectual capacity for making a subject
comprehensible - is bound in with one particular idea in the sermon and its
significance does not go beyond that particular context. Both forms were
common in sermons at the time right throughout Europe.
(i) Of the emblematic sermon we can find examples in István Illyés, as we
say, but also in the work of István Csete and Pài Baranyi. In such cases,
entire sermons are dominated by a single emblematic picture or series of
pictures which both carries the message and supplies the structure for the
sermon. In his study of the structural characteristics of István Landovics's
sermons, Sándor Lukácsy distinguished between four component parts: the
passage from the scriptures which provided the theme, the propositio, the
way the sermon was developed (applicatio), and the epilogue (conclusio).53

(iii) More frequent than the two forms mentioned so far is the indirect indica-
tion. Here, the text of the sermon contains only a paraphrase of the source
material, and thus gives us only an indirect indication of the work or author
used. When, for example, we want to trace the source of a picture with a
figurative sense that is introduced by an expression such as >the Egyptians,
wanting to depict.. .<, then we should look for it, first of all, in Valeriano.70

find the original. Vargyassi, for example, describes how he adapted for the

it must have been one of the symbols of the Spanish king from Jacobus
Typotius's Symbola divina et humana Pontificum Imperatorum Regum.71

In addition to emblematic publications with a recognizably original take
on emblematic material, other possible sources suggest themselves. Jesuit
teaching of emblematics probably accounts for an acquaintance with the
works of writers such as Diego de Saavedra Fajardo, Julius Wilhelm Zincgref,
Abraham a Sancta Clara, and Henricus Engelgrave.74 Although some of his
examples are identical to what we find in certain possible sources, it seems
unlikely, for example, that István Csete knew the Picta poesis of Barthélémy
Aneau, or János Sebacher the Symbola et emblemata of Joachim Camerarius,
or Vargyassi the Imprese of Labia, or István Illyés Girolamo Ruscelli's Le
imprese illustri.75
However, an examination of the sources available to them helps us to
recognize whether Hungarian preachers were capable of producing original
emblematic structures. There are several signs that István Csete and János
Sebacher, to take just two such preachers, made a conscious effort to fashion
individual versions from the material they inherited. One indication of this
would be the repeated use of the first-person singular. When Csete, for exam-
ple, wants to express the idea that Hungary has been saved from ruin by
images of the Holy Virgin that wept real tears, he uses the motto >Post lacry-
mas matris<, associates it with the motif of a bunch of grapes hanging from
a vine, and begins the introduction to his sermon: >If I wanted to give a living
picture of Hungary, I should write on a tablet before you .. .>76 It may be that
Csete's basic idea came from Picinelli for whom the motif of sap dripping
from a cut vine, and accompanied by the motto >In lachrymis feracior<, sym-
bolized tears.77 But the use of a different motto and interpretation, as well
the transformation of symbol into sermon, clearly indicate that Csete trans-
formed and further developed that idea in an original manner.
These sermons also demonstrate that preachers in Hungary derived their
emblematic forms from a rather narrow range of sources. An author will
repeatedly use the same devices: the motif of the unicorn, for example, in
Landovics78 or those of the falcon and the mill-wheel in Csete;79 in similar
contexts, different preachers will often use similar or even identical material.
