## Section: Conclusion

Conclusion
The point we have been seeking to make is that more investigation is needed
into the relationship between Latin emblematic poetry and vernacular poetry
in the sixteenth century. It is well known that the themes and ideas of Renais-
sance Latin poetry were also treated in the vernacular. However, we still do
not know enough about how this came about. How are the themes and ideas
modified as they pass from the learned language into the vernacular? What
is their significance, and their relation to the vernacular works? Can we inter-
pret English poems in the light of their Latin sources? How is our judgement
of a poet altered by our awareness of his Latin sources? It seems to us that
the study of sixteenth- and seventeenth-century literature could profit from
more research along these lines.
The examination of the origin and content, the structural and stylistic
characteristics of the collections of Zsámboky and Whitney, as well as a com-
parison of some of their texts, points to a shift in late humanist emblematics.
There are changes in the concept of poetic imitation and adaptation, and the
emblem form becomes increasingly flexible during the last third of the six-
teenth century. When assessing the results of such textual comparisons, one
has to bear in mind that the intellectual environments in which these two

Imitation for Zsámboky is closely related to the rhetorical concept of aptum
and to the idea of poetic inspiration as divine.

As Zsámboky's epigrams are of an uneven standard, the quality of Whit-

Zsámboky's originals. In some of them, he simultaneously summarizes and

and adding a number of classical references. Whitney employs the stanza
structure in order to make his translations transparent and easily comprehen-
sible. Yet, on the other hand, he often loses the concision, tension, and wit
of the original. An important difference between Zsámboky and Whitney is
that Zsámboky thought predominantly in terms of poetic images which were
only afterwards visually expressed, while Whitney worked with existing vis-
ual representations in addition to the source texts. But, by and large, he was
meticulous; there appear to be no instances of mistranslation, nor interpreta-
tions which completely contradict the original.
Both Zsámboky collection and Whitney's are characterized by their high
intellectual standards, and they share an aristocratic spiritual awareness
which is essentially concerned with the inner being. The key element of this
awareness is a syncretism of viewpoints and attitudes. The features shared
by both works include a reliance on classical authors for inspiration, the use
of pseudo-classical mythographic literature, humanist collections of common-
places, and moralizing animal symbolism in the Aesopian tradition.76 In addi-
tion, both works consistently combine deep learning and moral appeal. That
said, Zsámboky's epigrams are more complex and less direct than Whitney's,
and while the former is concerned with subtly illuminating the obscure (with
argutia, that is), Whitney's prime concern is that he should be understood.
The esotericism of Zsámboky's emblems is largely missing from Whitney's
adaptations.
The mutually reinforcing interplay of various rhetorical forms is another
feature the two writers share, though it is much less significant in Whitney.
Zsámboky's preferred rhetorical strategy builds up to a moral with meta-
phors, poetic questions and paradoxes, and figurative speech. Visual and ver-
bal symbolism both play an important part, as do ecphrastic traditions. Whit-
ney employs some of Zsámboky's forms, yet he does not attempt to recreate
the rhetoric and stylistic variety of Zsámboky's poetry.

Although the cultural attitudes of the two authors are largely the same,
their collections are essentially different. Mainly, this is because one of them
is considerably more learned than the other, but they are not equally inven-
tive, either, nor do they both have the same capacity for absorbing and com-
municating humanist learning. In addition, their concepts of imitation, adap-
tation, and originality are different. Zsámboky, with his extensive and refined
philological learning gleaned from fifteen years of editing texts,77 aimed his
work at a broad educated readership.
Zsámboky handles his topics with confidence, sometimes even playfully,
and effortlessly combines the principles of learned imitation with the poetic
generalization of personal experience. Whitney was evidently attempting to
satisfy the demands of a far narrower readership. His marginalia and the
quotations he selects from the classics show that he regarded these writers
as little more than a convenient quarry for material. He was not capable of
handling the breadth of learning we find in Zsámboky not did he think of
that learning as part of a unified, living tradition. His mannerist excesses of
quotation and reference, along with an increased emphasis on didacticism
and learned moralization, betray a concept of poetry which is both more
popular and less wide than Zsámboky's. Increasingly alienated from the hu-
manist literary tradition, Whitney's work is an indication of that tradition
in decline.

VI Mannerist emblematic poetry? The layers of literary
tradition in János Rimay's poem Fortuna/Occasio
An explosion of interest in the classics and in the critical interpretation of
ancient writers, and the positive and dynamic influence of the classical liter-
ary heritage on contemporary writers produced the wealth of subject-matter,
forms, and styles we now call humanist and mannerist literature. Philological
study of the heritage of Greece and Rome not only encouraged an increas-
ingly complex and imaginative imitation of the art of antiquity but also made
such activity fashionable. Different techniques and approaches to the key
concept of imitatio spawned fresh genres and new kinds of literature, many
of them of great artistic value, and the boundaries between the different
literary genres gradually became blurred.1 >Pure< genres were pushed into
the background and new hybrid forms assumed centre stage. Among these
were the epigram and the emblem, which grew in popularity until they came
to constitute a separate category of their own. One specialized form of the
epigram, the ecphrastic epigram, became, along with the emblem, especially
popular in the sixteenth century, because of its twin appeal as a vehicle for
visualization and as the sensual depiction of abstract thought. Mannerist sty-
listic ideals made great play with the visual aspects of hidden meanings, since
visualization made it possible to express complex ideas in a quite fresh artis-
tic form. The symbiosis and coalescence of discrete literary forms is a feature
of life in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Mannerist taste, sumptuous
decoration, and the taste for occult and obscure knowledge given encoded
literary expression all made for an art that was both compressed and laden
with meaning.
These are the principal features of the poetry of János Rimay, the most
important Hungarian poet of the first third of the seventeenth century and
a man much influenced by Bálint Baiassi and the neo-Stoicism of Lipsius.2

The negative evaluation of emblematics we talked of earlier and the par-
allels drawn with the decline of Renaissance art both acted as impediments
to research into the history of literary emblematics in Hungary in the early
1960s. From the outset, limits were imposed on research into texts with em-
blematic associations by statements as extreme as >the emblematic picture
and poem mean the death of true poetry<. At this period, scholars dismissed
János Rimay's so-called >emblematic explanations of fortune, virtue, and reli-
gion as unsuccessful, since their alleged >aesthetic formlessness< indicated
>the bankruptcy of a passing fad<.8
Given this background, it is perhaps unsurprising that, when historians
and critics began once again in the early 1980s to interest themselves in em-
blematics, interdisciplinary attempts to approach works of this kind using the
methodology of the art historian did not proved particularly fruitful9 and
that the term >emblematic< was employed, in interpretations of mannerist
and Baroque texts with visual elements and pictorial associations, with insuf-
ficient care. Ferenc Zemplényi's assertion, for example, that Rimay was in-
capable of constructing a complete poem around a single picture (and the
idea expressed by it)< went largely unchallenged, as did his claim that, in
Rimay's works, >the effect of the pictures becomes atomized and only influ-
ences the line or, even simply the phrase.<10 It is now clear that what we
need is a multilevel interpretation of those kinds of epigrammatic poetry that
most closely aspire to emblematics: one which pays equal attention to both
the physical features of the genre and the theoretical thinking that lay behind
it. Our aim is to take János Rimay's poem Fortuna/Occasio and attempt to
demonstrate a fresh way of looking critically at a mannerist poem with neo-
classical, mythographical pretensions, while simultaneously revealing new in-
formation about the poet's background and education and offering a glimpse
into how he operated as a poet.

The theme of Fortuna in European literature
The history of the theme of Fortuna in European literature and art is an
extremely complex one and has many ramifications; it is no coincidence that,
even today, we have no comprehensive study of the subject. Even when For-
tuna first appears in classical antiquity hers is an ambiguous figure, and the
concept of Fortuna was, even at that time, a shifting one. She was an amalgam
of several mythological figures, her physical attributes an admixture of exter-
nal and internal qualities. Not only in ancient times but ever since, hers has

[Page 124]
116
been a figure constructed from metaphors that have been transmuted into
fixed images.15
If we are to place Rimay's image of Fortuna in a broader context, we
must first turn to some of the key stages in the development of the figure of
Fortuna. When we do this, we will be struck by the many changes the figure
of Fortune has undergone. Also fundamental are the interaction of portrayals
of Fortuna in literature, the visual arts, mythology, emblematics, and so on,
and the ways between these various symbolic forms of expression have be-

Remarks on genre history
It is common knowledge that one of the important thematic groups in classi-
cal epigrammatic poetry consists of epigrams written about works of art and
that these so-called imago (or picture) epigrams were one of the most effec-
tive stimulants in the whole history of epigrammatic poetry.54 The key ele-
ments of such classical picture epigrams are the explanatory inscriptio and
the artist's signature; the principal components are, however, the introduc-
tion (descriptio) and the commentary (interpretatio). The relationship be-
tween >picture< and text in the picture epigram will vary from example to
example, and the text will not in every case be tied in tightly to what is
actually depicted. It often happens that the poet will dwell on only a part or
parts of the object pictured, or go well beyond the picture itself. By dint of
operating ecphrastically, the classical picture epigram alludes in a number of
ways to the kinds of epigram we find associated with the allegorical figures
of mythology, history, and literature.55
As we have already had cause to mention, one of the best-known exam-
ples of a picture epigram written on a symbolic, allegorical work of art is the
epigram by Posidippus on Lysippus's statue of Kairos; in it, the figure de-
picted answers the spectator's questions about the significance of the various
attributes on display. Posidippus's poem, together with other picture epi-
grams from the Anthologia Graeca, inspired Latin writers. Most of the adap-
tations - one of the earliest of them being by Ausonius - make use of the
original simply as a point of departure; any links they may have with the
actual work of art soon disappear and, in their place, centre stage is now
occupied by interpretations and the skilful wording of rhetorical maxims.56
The first Christian epigrammatists modified the genre even further: the pic-
tures discussed (usually scenes from the Bible) were no longer actual works
of art but rather symbols for something above and beyond that can be used
as the basis for a meditation or a typological interpretation.57
The mannerist poetry used to describe pictures in the Renaissance, both
straightforwardly and in more symbolic ways, is also amenable to a variety
of approaches. The fundamental difficulty facing the typologist here arises

Structural and stylistic features of the poem
The poem is cyclical and falls into five clear sections, each building on the
last and advancing the argument further (RÖM, no. 65). Starting out with a
gentle introduction to the problem, each section looks at a separate external
attribute of the figure, taking us another step forward until, when we get to
the quasi-psalmodic tone of the final strophe, we have the complete argu-
ment, the poet's case against Fortuna. The overall structure is highly reminis-
cent of the explicatio technique we find in the symbolic poetry penned by
Jesuits like Jan David, Nicolaus Caussin, and Charles de La Rue at the turn
of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the purpose of which was primar-

schoolteacher will with his pupils. With the remark >painters paint<, Rimay
locates his description of Fortune within a particular tradition, and the ex-
pression >here you can learn< makes it clear that his primary purpose is to
teach. The prose introduction explicitly calls to mind the familiar figure of

repetitions of external attributes and internal qualities, the hidden references,
and the questions and answers, Rimay's value judgement, his taking of sides,
is put on hold.
The fourth of our sections, like the second, runs to three stanzas (lines
31-39). None of the external attributes of Fortune are repeated here, but
we are afforded an extra one (the tenth): the turning wheel. When it comes
to internal qualities, the fleeting nature of the benefits Fortuna bestows is
reiterated, and the wording emphasizes her mutability. This section has no
questions and explanations, and Rimay's target remains Fortuna's fickleness.
Formulaic references to source material are once again centre stage: >Thus
did in old times Sapientia paint you, | Taking in every aspect of your appear-
ance< (lines 31-32). This section ends with a new twist: a prayer to God,
which also acts as a transition into the concluding part of the poem.
In the last three stanzas (lines 40-48) one final external attribute makes
its first appearance: Fortune's banner. At the beginning of this section Rimay
makes a personal remark which smacks of the confessional (>I, too, who
frequently have dreaded you<, line 40); he then prays again to God to help
him avert the negative influences of Fortuna/Occasio.
It is clear that this is a very conscientiously worked composition, with a
structure that is almost geometric in its regularity. The thought and structure
are martialled precisely, and great care has been exercised over the choice
of language. The longest section, which does so much to determine the tone
and theme of the composition, is the third, with its twenty-one lines of schol-
arly gloss. Yet what binds the whole poem together is not this but rather the
sequence of features the poem reveals that are associated with the symbolic
figure addressed. The recurring attributes of the first three parts are grad-

Working methods and the strands of tradition
In a letter to György Rákóczi I, dated 25 May 1629, Rimay refers to the
meticulous care he took when writing: >1 am now writing and pasting to-
gether ... a ... piece of work.< This glimpse of his working method chimes in
with one of the key features of the mannerist style. Rimay very often fitted
together the elements he had selected rather like pieces in a mosaic, moving
them around, trying them in conjunction with other, slotting them in, and
making an artistic whole out of them. All the time, the moral, didactic, and
interpretative purpose of the work was uppermost in his mind. He admits as
much in the letter from which we just quoted, stating: >In this work, I am
explaining the poems,< >Using little rhythmic poems, I have explained these
... in this way.<87
In his account of the mannerist stylistic elements in the Rimay poem,
Tibor Komlovszki talks at length about the influence of other genres. He
stresses in this respect the way overall harmonious effect has been sacrificed
in the interests of particular concerns. In his opinion, the key question in the
Rimay poem is the poetic image itself and the way that image is permitted
to spawn fresh ideas. Komlovszki's suggestion that it was Rimay's poetic pic-

starting-point also diverted attention away from proper research into the
literary traditions that helped to create Fickle Fortune.
Before we get involved in the search for the different strands of tradition
at work in the poem, we need to answer the question of what Rimay himself
had in mind. Firstly, of course, this poem on a humanist theme gave him a
chance to display his learning. To do this, he needed a thorough grounding
in the ways the image of Fortuna was treated in classical antiquity, an aware-
ness of humanist scholarship, familiarity with the literature of the fifteenth
and sixteenth centuries, and, last but not least, knowledge of the emblematic
techniques that were enjoying a vogue in his own day. At the same time,
composing a poem of this kind offered him an opportunity to present a man-
nerist interpretation of Fortuna and also to express his own opinions on the
subject. Rimay clearly kept all these considerations in mind as he wrote, as
he gathered his source material together and then began weaving it into a
satisfactory artistic whole. For us today, the greatest difficulty is teasing out
the various strands involved.
