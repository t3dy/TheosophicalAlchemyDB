Foreword
This monograph attempts, for the first time, to give a comprehensive picture
of one of the most popular didactic and symbolic forms of literary expression
in Renaissance and Baroque Hungary: emblematics. It will look in turn at
the effect of emblematics upon literature, upon genre, and upon the different
kinds of material printed. We shall be considering both the content and the
appearance of that material as well as the changing structures and functions
of literature, and historical and international influences. Emblematics was
not merely a Europe-wide vogue; it was a >universal language^ and under-
standing how that language operated helps us to define and understand liter-
ary processes as well as epistemological and notational conventions which
seem to modern minds impossibly obscure.

Towards a definition
We shall take the term emblematics to mean: a form of expression found in
a literary context, defined by a combination of picture and text, and consti-
tuting one of the modes of symbolic thought connecting visual motif and
abstract meaning. From the sixteenth century to the eighteenth, it played an
important role in organizing, mediating, and recording knowledge about life,
man, and the world. Beyond this general definition, which is one accepted
by more or less all emblem researchers, there lies the vast territory of the
many literary and art-historical concepts, typologies, clarifications, and defi-
nitions of the emblem and the emblem book.32 Debates still rage about the

Emblematics is, then, characterized by a high degree of diversity, both in
terms of its links with literary traditions, and also in its content, form, and
function, the processes of interpretation it required, and its intended reader-

label of emblem. The typologies they designed in order to do that were
mostly based on the supposed formal or functional attributes of the emblem,
yet it is now clear that practically any aspect of life or of the world can be
described in some sense as emblematic, and not even the most carefully nu-
anced typology is capable of capturing the truly protean nature of this mode
of artistic expression.45
So there is no standard, generally accepted definition of the emblem, even
today. Some call it an >independent< genre, others a >special form< of expres-
sion, and researchers interpret the meaning of the emblem and the relation-
ship between its parts in different ways. If, following Richard Alewyn, we
understand a literary genre to be a clearly delineated model that not only
consists of a specific collection of objects, motifs, and characters, but also
connects a given Weltanschauung and conceptual content with what has gone
before and does so in such a manner that none of the components can be
moved or exchanged,46 then the emblem does not constitute a genre, either
in fact or in theory. But, to take Bernhard Scholz's definition, now more
and more widely accepted, the emblem that expresses abstract thoughts by
interpreting the objects of the world of the senses in a manner that it points
beyond them is a form open to continual change, rebirth, and reorganization,
and one with three defining features: (a) the archetypal typographical ar-
rangement we find in the first two editions of the Alciati compilation; (b)
the remarks found in emblem books and emblem theory concerning subject-
matter and the use it is put to; and (c) the rules of poetic imitation governing
similarity and dissimilarity.47

II Emblematics in Hungarian literary theory
From the research that has been done so far on literary emblematics in Hun-
gary it is clear that progress is being hampered not only by the inherent com-
plexity of emblematic forms of expression and their protean nature but also
by a persistent haziness about the theoretical basis involved.1 In the sixteenth
century there was little or no theoretical writing about the emblem as such, ex-
cept in prologues to emblem books and the occasional remark made in a text
or a volume of impresas. In poetic treatises of the time, the impresa, emblems,
and other symbol systems are treated as though there were little or no differ-
ence between them.2 These forms of expression had common roots and what
was said about any one of them could easily be adapted to apply to another,
and this may explain why, in both in theory and practice, expressions such as
hieroglyph, emblem, impresa, symbol, and imago, were used synonymously -
or, rather, as blanket terms to cover them all.
The emblematic material that does appear in manuals of rhetoric, poetics,
and works of art theory from the early modern period can be divided into
separate sub-categories using some of the more elaborate European theories;
but with the much sparser Hungarian source material this is not always pos-
sible. Instead, we are forced to take a broad, inclusive view of the theoretical
situation, embracing not only remarks that are specifically concerned with
literary theory proper but also the theoretical considerations implicit in the
emblem collections themselves as well as what we can discover from looking
at imago theories and works in the ars memorativa tradition. Such an ap-
proach is justified both in terms of the sources themselves and also by the
fact that the theoretical statements we find in Hungarian seventeenth- and
eighteenth-century works on poetics, rhetorical treatises, and emblematic
publications refer most of the time, as do all European treatises on the sub-
ject, not to the emblem as an autonomous form of expression but rather to
the sum of all the forms of expression employed to illustrate a particular
moral point or celebrate a particular occasion.

Emblem theories in Hungary - as in the rest of Europe - were not pure
theories but rather peculiar admixtures of theory and practice. Only seldom
did theory have a direct impact upon practice, and practice was always more
powerful than theory. To a significant extent, what theory there was de-

Our research covers both the poetics and books on rhetoric published in
Hungary and also the remarks about principles made in emblematic publica-

Theories of emblem authors
We have already alluded to the fact that the development of emblematics
was determined in the whole of Europe predominantly by practice. Hungary
was no exception. Emblematists in Hungary, however, despite the richness
and variety of their emblems, did not produce theories commensurate with
those by Italian, French, and German authors. The theoretical remarks they
did allow themselves can be divided into two, chronologically separate
groups:
(/) Comments directed fundamentally at the creative element of the emblem.
Two of the most important Hungarian emblematists of the sixteenth and
seventeenth centuries, János Zsámboky and Kristóf Lackner, did record their
ideas for posterity. Zsámboky summarized his in the introduction to his Em-
blemata.42 Working out just what this dense text of four and a half pages,
written in a mixture of Latin and Greek, actually means and then attempting
to see how the ideas connected up into a system has occupied both Hung-
arian and international researchers right up to the present day.43 It is known

The introduction to the Emblemata touches upon the same topics that
occupied all emblematists at the time. Such questions as the definition and
idiosyncrasies of the emblem and how it was linked to other similar literary
forms, its function, systematization, and origins, and the rules for constructing
emblems all appear here.46 Zsámboky significantly broadens Alciati's defini-
tion of the subject of the emblem: he says that the emblem may take its
theme equally well from the areas of history, Nature, morality, and mythol-
ogy. By his definition, the emblem is, above all, a means to moral education,
in the same way as were other popular minor forms of humanist compila-
tional literature, such as the gnoma, fabula, exemplum, proverbium and
apophthegma. It differs from these only in so far as it also has a covert,
hidden reference which needs to be explained, makes one think, and which,
similarly to the symbol, pictorially represents what it wants to express. An
emblem should have wit and charm, and be rich in content; it should be
founded upon pithy and serious thought, and the picture should represent a
reality which is concrete or is at least founded upon general conviction. The
esoteric character of the emblem is something we find discussed in Alciati
and Bocchi; Zsámboky's views coincide with those of Giovio: the emblem is
more obscure than the Aristotelian problemata but can be understood more
easily than either the dilemmata or the aenigmata.
