# FrÃ¼he Neuzeit : Emblematics in Hungary : A Study of the History of Symbolic Representation in Renaissance and Baroque Literature

## Section: Conclusion

Conclusion
This study has surveyed the history of research into literary emblematics,
examined the theories underpinning this phenomenon and the ways it devel-
oped and spread, and established a typology of the Hungarian source mater-
ial. The examples we have chosen illustrate the role played by the emblem
and its associated genres in lyric poetry, in works in the Mirror of Princes
tradition, in didactic and panegyrical literature, in the school drama, and in
sermons and religious prose writing. We have also attempted an interpreta-
tion of the rhetorical conception and iconographie program of an emblematic
cycle of frescos. Emblem research is at present beset by a whole welter of
different methodologies and approaches; there is no established or agreed
technical lexicon for describing and analysing emblematic material; and the
hermeneutics and semiotics of literature in the early modern period are
widely ignored by students of the emblem. It is clear that a purely descriptive
approach to material of this kind is no longer satisfactory; that the compila-
tion of bibliographies based on particular specialist interests and informed
by obscure and arbitrary criteria will not meet the case; and that interpreta-
tions that limit themselves to a single level of meaning or a single functional
approach can no longer be justified. What is needed is an entirely fresh her-
meneutical approach: one which never loses sight of the fact that emblems
and emblematic material were, in the period under consideration, not only a
mirror of cultural and literary development but also an active and creative
factor in the development of that culture and that literature.
Our main purpose in this book has been to reconstruct these emblematic
forms of expression in all their diversity, to trace their history and the ways
they developed and changed over time, and to get as close as we can to an
understanding of their semiotic and semantic dynamics and to the poetic
models that informed them. To achieve this we have eschewed the simple
descriptive approach, with its emphasis on a single, ideal stereotype; we have
also resisted both the Procrustean temptation to stretch or prune our model
to fit an existing genre system and also the urge to ascribe an abitrary central
theme or vital core to the protean material under review. Instead, we have
chosen to interpret the emblem and its allied forms within a literary context,
grouping together material from a wide variety of sources, wherever there
are clear links either of form or of function. We have tried to embrace all
the phenomena connected with emblematics and to explore the relationship

between the various ways certain common features are adapted and trans-
formed over time and to compare these with statements of intent and theo-
retical stances. The source material, most of which has been unknown until
now, extends from the Renaissance, through the periods associated with
Mannerism, the Baroque, and the Rococo, and into the Enlightenment. In-
deed, in some cases it goes even further. We are accordingly looking at a
period of almost three hundred years. The influence of shifts in artistic fash-
ion over such a long period is clearly apparent in the Hungarian material,
and such shifts have made our task a complex one. Other goals we have
borne include the need to demonstrate - without ever losing sight of the
relationship between theory and practice - just how these forms of artistic
expression were influenced by, among other factors, social, geographical, and
religious considerations, and the importance of looking both at neo-Latin
works and at those in the vernacular, in order to identify the extent and force
of the impact such forms of expression had on the ways Renaissance and
Baroque literature developed and flowered.
We took as our definition of emblematics >a symbolic mode of expression
and of syncretic thinking<. This allowed us to examine such literature both
historically and from the point of view of artistic theory, and also to make
connexions between Hungarian literary history and what was happened in
the wider context of the rest of Europe. As a result, we have been able to
explore some hitherto little-known characteristics of the literary output of
the period under review. The study of the emblem and its related forms is a
serious and fruitful area for research, yet, partly because it involves a neces-
sarily rather narrow definition of what is and what is not emblematic, it tends
to offer rather fewer possibilities for creating comprehensive theories than
do many better-known approaches to Renaissance and Baroque art. The re-
lationship between picture and text is central to our whole approach and,
consequently, our constant concern has been with the meaning of the visual,
and the role played by pictoriality in the literary theories of the time. We
have examined closely the technical terms and rhetorical techniques em-
ployed by early modern artists and writers, while also bringing to bear mod-
ern advances in literary criticism in such disciplines as text production, trans-
mission, and reception, and the cultural and transformational processes
involved in the use of symbols. There can no longer be any doubt that em-
blematics is an integral feature of Renaissance and Baroque literary culture
in Hungary, as it was elsewhere in Europe.
Without rehearsing in detail the conclusions set out in the final pages of
each of the earlier chapters, it would seem appropriate to refer briefly here
to a few salient points that have emerged during the course of our investiga-
tions and to point to possible further areas for research. As in most European
countries, emblematics in Hungary is part and parcel of a complex labyrinth
of literary modes of thought and expression and one that reflects particular

[Page 254]
246
ideological, religious, and political trends and fashions. Hungary may not
have produced any theoreticians of truly European stature, but the theoreti-
cal side to emblematic production can be seen time and again, as can the
influence of European writers and theorists. Although, in Hungary, this rela-
tive poverty of theoretical writing went hand in hand with a considerable
body of emblematic practice, no Hungarian figure can be ranked alongside
the major Europeans emblematists - apart, that is, from János Zsámboky.
Most of the Hungarian production in this field is derivative and the majority
of the practitioners are second-rate; but the cultivation of the emblem for its
own sake was of secondary significance when one compares it to the many
adaptations of it that were produced in any number of different genres. There
is little of the typological variety we find in Southern and Western Europe,
and the link between emblematics in literature and the visual arts is infre-
quent. Emblem production in Hungary has none of the distinctive character
that we find, for example, in the Netherlands; and it is a good deal less
inventive. This relative poverty is explained by the fact that, in Hungary,
emblematics did not appear as the result of an organic cultural development
of the kind that took place, for example, in Italy and in France, but instead
came about as a result of external influences and borrowings and in a host
of other, less direct ways.
Turning from differences to similarities, we can see that in Hungary, as in
the rest of Europe, the emblem did not constitute a discrete and clearly
defined literary genre. It would be more accurate to call it a particular mode
of expression and process of transmission, the two defining features of which
were the disintegration of the traditional semiotic systems inherited from
classical antiquity and the placing of conventional modes of expression in
new contexts which imbued them with fresh meaning. The driving force be-
hind this process of fragmentation, adaptation, and recontextualization was
the urge to refresh and revitalize the message being conveyed and to gener-
ate fresh associations and new readings. From a historical standpoint, the
emblem proved to be, in Hungary as elsewhere, a transitional form between
the period when signs and motifs were regarded as having specific and fixed
meanings and the modern period when we have developed a different and
shifting concept of language and meaning. It was a period when what until
then had been regarded as an essential and immutable link between signifier
and signified gradually became an accidental one. In this new artistic world,
practically anything could become an emblem of almost anything else. In this
sense, the emblem paved the way, so to speak, for the way metaphor was
used by the Romantics, with their emphasis on a subjective, personal connec-
tion between signifier and signified. The way that the interdependence of
signifier and signified was transformed into a relationship that was basically
accidental may also explain why, in Hungary as throughout the rest of
Europe, the various emblem compendia and iconographie handbooks cease

[Page 255]
247
to furnish us with an authoritative guide to the meanings involved in works
written in the emblematic mode. One significant feature of the Hungarian
experience was the way mythological commonplaces and, more generally, the
world of classical antiquity continued to play a part in emblematic producti-
ons which, in their turn, played an important role in keeping classical mythol-
ogy and an interest in classical literature alive.
From the mid-seventeenth century onwards - about the same time that
emblematic works in the vernacular first appeared in Hungary - one can see
emblematic modes of expression taking on a popular guise for the first time,
as part of a process in which didacticism becomes more important, and the
role of the pictorial component dwindles by comparison. Propaganda began
to assume central stage; the range of pictorial motifs narrowed; and one gets
the distinct impression that the imagination of emblematists was becoming
exhausted. At the same time, while emblematic modes of expression contin-
ued to enjoy great favour in courtly circles, they also began - thanks largely
to the Jesuits - to penetrate other layers of society, first the nobility and
then the bourgeoisie. They began to play an important role as a mediator
between different social classes and groups, thanks to the emergence of a
standardized educational curriculum and a shared educational experience.
Thus, one can interpret the history of emblematics in Hungary also as the
history of how representational models and the various traditional features
of the educational curriculum - among them the literary genres most closely
associated with the emblematic mode - became, in some sense, popularized.
The Hungarian material displays the same tendency we find in the rest of
Europe in favour of social mobility, artistic openness, and an affinity between
emblematic forms of expression and the demands that appeared for new
subject-matter and fresh techniques.
At the same time as emblems began to penetrate the more popular levels
of national culture, they, paradoxically, also became more specialized. We
can trace the emergence of two main categories of emblematic publication:
the first markedly moralizing and didactic, the second mainly serving reli-
gious and pious purposes. Emblematic material belonging to the first cate-
gory arises principally as a result of the teaching of poetics and rhetoric in
schools, and is structured around classical and humanist topoi, and collections
of proverbs and adages. The second group is born out of Christian meditation
and the tradition of biblical exegesis and symbolic theology, and is given a
fresh impetus by the use by Jesuits of pictorial rhetoric, or visibilitas. There
is a good deal of traffic between these two main categories and, as result, we
see a wide variety of hybrid forms and clear evidence of mutual interaction,
giving rise to thematic and structural variants. In the wake of the humanist
emblem books, which were essentially anthological in character - containing,
as they did, pieces that were largely independent one of another - we can
trace the emergence in Hungary, in the first third of the seventeenth century,

[Page 256]
248
of series of compositions composed around a single theme, with individual
emblems forming an integral part of a larger, overarching structure. Hungar-
ian emblematic material can be divided into three main types: (i) emblem
books, stricto sensu-, (ii) printed books with emblematic illustrations; and
(iii) literary texts, in which the picture element of the emblem is replaced by
a textual description of something visual (either a description or a quota-
tion). By the middle of the eighteenth century, emblematic forms have be-
come hollow and repetitive and, by the turn of the century, they begin to
disappear altogether.
The overriding purpose of employing emblematic forms of expression to
illustrate works of literature was to explain and advocate behaviour of a
particular kind. As did their fellow practitioners elsewhere in Europe, Hun-
garian emblematists used, for the most part, existing pictorial and textual
formulae, familiar combinations of pictures and text, to direct the reader's
attention and thought processes and to trasmit received wisdom. The under-
lying message of such compositions was often a complex one. The process is
one in which one can trace the influence of emblematic ways of thinking
across a wide range of genres and types of text, with form and function often
very closely interconnected. Changes in form reflect the nature of the genre
and the type of text in which emblematic modes of expression were now
being employed. They also provided the emblematist with a wide variety of
possibilities without his ever having to break entirely with tradition. The use
of emblematic modes of expression in genres and texts which had previously
been uncharted territory for expressions of this kind led to the creation of
new way of articulating personal, religious, and political concerns. Hungarian
writers employed the emblem in particular in genres and texts of the genus
demonstrativum, which referred to matters which were topical at the time.
As this web of interrelated influences and connections developed, rhetori-
cal forms of expression based on pictorial concepts grew in number, and
the imagination and invention of the authors came to play a larger role.
Contemporary theoretical statements about emblems and associated modes
of expression became more sharply defined, and it was possible to see the
emblem as part of a wide-ranging rhetorical system. Research has confirmed
our hypothesis that, beyond a certain stage, it is neither useful nor productive
to divorce the layers of meaning present in pictorial concepts from the sym-
bolic forms of expression based on pictures and images; to do so is as arbi-
trary as would be any hard and fast definition of modern critical concepts
like picture, allegory, or symbol. In the poetics of the Renaissance, symbols -
including emblems - were primarily component parts of the epigram; and in
seventeenth-century poetics they appear predominantly in poems written for
particular occasions. In such poems, there is a pronounced overlap between
the pictorial interpretation of reality and reality as the point of departure
for linguistic expression. In poems like these, the emblem and its associated

[Page 257]
249
rhetorical devices such as simile are not invoked as part of a particular inter-
pretation of the world, but rather belong to the sphere of rhetoric and orna-
tus. They furnish us with vital clues as to the purpose of the work and its
genre as well as provide information about the role of the emblem itself. In
this sense, many emblems are not unlike the similes familar to us from rhe-
torical texts: they enhance the argument, shed light on the issues involved,
enliven the composition, expand on the theme, and provide variety; they also
focus attention on the theme and direct the attention of the reader/spectator
towards the message being conveyed. In rhetoric at the end of the seven-
teenth century, symbols were beginning to disappear from the glosses and
explanations accompanying literary works. A common element of the em-
blem and the simile is the way they are used to extend meaning - a feature
often dubbed translatio and which is closely related to the ways metaphor
assumed an ever larger role systems in Renaissance and Baroque rhetoric.
In this connexion, the Jesuit rhetorical tradition clearly played a significant
role in Hungary as in the rest of Europe; from its very beginning, Jesuit
rhetoric had always emphasized the role of ecphrasis and metaphor in satisfy-
ing the twin requirements of argutia and simplicitas.
A number of unsolved questions have come to light during the research
for this book. Among these figure the printing of didactic texts (including
emblematic material) and their reception, the problem of authorial intention,
target readership, and the methodology of argumentation. Even today, we
still know very little about just how much the pictorial and textual elements
of the emblem contributed to ensuring the impact and popularity of a work
and about the extent to which the inclusion in a text of emblems rich in
associations was considered an indication of literary quality. We have to take
each work on its own merits: before we can identify literary precedents and,
more specifically, the sources that have been used - and certainly before we
can assess the originality of the author(s) involved - we must examine the
emblematic influences available at the time and the actual extent to which
they make their presence felt in the work, as well as the manner in which
particular motifs are borrowed and transformed. Emblematics is one of the
major sources for the allegorical use of image and metaphor in Renaissance
and Baroque poetry: it is often the key to the structuring of a text, as well
as an immediate source of inspiration. Yet further research is needed before
we can decide whether this pictoriality is merely a superficial poetic or rhe-
torical feature or whether it is part of a broader symbolic system which is
vital to the creation of poetic expression. In this study, we have paid more
attention to the use of emblematics in prose texts and, more widely, to the
presence of emblematic modes of thought and expression. Future researchers
will have to pay more attention that we have done here to the poetic use of
such habits of expression, as well as to the emblematic activity of indivi-
dual authors.

250
The study of the pictorial elements in emblematic material is part of a far
wider area of research into metaphor and image. Yet it cannot be limited
even to that. Apart from the use of the symbols we find in emblem books
and associated material, there is the matter of the influence that emblematic
habits of mind and thought, with their emphasis on the tension between
representation and interpretation, had on the structural principles underpin-
ning Renaissance and Baroque works of literature, and primarily on poetry.
The influence of humanistic education on classical poetics is widely recog-
nized; but the new emphasis we find in the early modern period on wit in
such matters as the glossing or explanation of maxims still needs investiga-
tion, as does the relationship between what we might call the >sententious<
lyric, the ut pictura poesis tradition, and emblematics more generally. The
systematic exploration of primary works of several kinds, both literary and
pictorial, though clearly important in this regard, is still in its infancy. We
still need a systematic exploration of such phenomena as alba amicorum\
handwritten registers of births, marriages, and deaths; chapbooks, and works
of art of various kinds, as well as impresa portraits. It is to research of this
kind that we shall have to look in the future if we are to achieve a better
understanding of the history of this peculiarly hybrid form of artistic creation.
