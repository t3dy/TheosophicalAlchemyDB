## Section: Conclusions

Conclusions
The Gyor series of frescos is significant, above all, because the designer of
the programme combined several interpretative traditions while re-creating
and recombining in autonomous fashion conventional pictorial motifs and
texts and traditional topoi, giving them fresh meaning and, by setting up
reverberations between one element and the next, placing them in new
contexts. In doing so, he laid the foundation for a representative work of art
in which the co-ordinating structures carry both an immediate message for
contemporaries and a more universally valid one. The pictorial and literary
forms that emerge from the different traditions that interact and overlap
form a composite symbolic language in which they constantly refer back to
each other and to the function they have been assigned. While the content
of the cycle is firmly based on biblical and medieval textual and pictorial
elements, the structural conception reflects a characteristically mannerist
habit of mind, and one which is in striking contrast with the exuberant visual
language employed which is consistently that of the High Baroque.
The praise of the Virgin, her role in mediating divine grace and protecting
against afflictions of all sorts is closely connected in this cycle with the idea
of the successful campaign against the Turks. The series can, therefore, be
looked at as one of the many artistic representations of that struggle against
the Turk, but it also, of course, points beyond it. It is no coincidence that the
cycle was completed at the very time victories were being achieved against
the Türks: in the year, in fact, of the greatest victory of the imperial armies,
the battle of Zenta (Senta) which secured once and for all the process which
made the Turkish expulsion inevitable. Györ was a lynchpin in the system of
border fortifications designed to protect Hungary against the Turks; its recap-
ture in 1598 constituted a spectacular military success for the Christian
troops, and the twin themes of the struggle against the Turks and the Virgin's
role as protectress of Christendom were constantly present in the literary

and artistic life of the town in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.36 It
is not insignificant that, in seventeenth-century Hungary, Gyor was a major
centre for Catholic spirituality, Baroque literature, and the visual arts, nor
that the Jesuit college played an important role in the religious and cultural
life not only of the town but of the whole of north-western Hungary.
As Géza Galavics has pointed out, the theme of the struggle against the
Türks at the beginning of the 1640s first appears in Baroque art in Hungary,
and with unique splendour and emphasis, on the altarpieces in the Györ
Jesuit church, where it is coupled with veneration of the Virgin. At the time
of the Wars of Liberation, the Jesuits were among the most conscious guard-
ians of the artistic tradition of the fight against the Türk and, even after the
expulsion of the Türks, they continued this tradition for some time, using an
iconographie system devised expressly for the purpose.37 The Grand Stair-
case at Györ appeared after the immediate Turkish danger had receded but
in an atmosphere still receptive to the artistic representation of the struggle
against the Türks twinned with the cult of the Virgin. This may help to ex-
plain why the altarpieces in a church built some fifty years earlier emphasize
the fight against the Türk while in our fresco series that theme is subordinate
to praise of the triumphant Virgin. The importance of the Györ cycle is rein-
forced by the observation that frescos are probably the rarest form of art in
Hungary associated with the fight against the Türks.
If we now, finally, turn to the Grand Staircase at Graz, constructed fifteen
years after the Györ series and also with a programme centring on the Virgin
Mary, both the similarities between them and the differences become ob-
vious.38 The placement of the two ensembles within the buildings and the
architectural structure of the stairs are identical. Both ensembles were in-
tended, above all, to be objects of meditation whose main task was the repre-
sentation of abstract ideas through the medium of the visual rhetoric inher-
ent in the emblematic mode of expression itself and the Jesuit tradition of
meditation. The same emblematic language appears in both ensembles: a late
manifestation of the hermeneutics of the Renaissance and of mannerism,
shaped and honed by an idiosyncratic Jesuit variant of this form of expres-
sion. Grete Lesky's observation that, in Graz, the frescos are designed to be
read downwards and that the last emblem then directs the onlooker's gaze
back upwards, chimes in perfectly with what we find in the parallel readings
of the Györ cycle. But, while at Györ the emblematic, symbolic representa-
tions are generally intended to complement the figurative scenes, in Graz the
emblems, composed almost exclusively of object symbols, play the main role.
