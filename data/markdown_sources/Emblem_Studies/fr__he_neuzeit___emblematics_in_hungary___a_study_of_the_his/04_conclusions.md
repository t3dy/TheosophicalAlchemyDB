## Section: Conclusions

Conclusions
It would appear that the general characteristics found in emblematic material
right across Europe also held sway in Hungary. A significant number of the
main types of emblem book and of the genres using emblematic material
were known in Hungary, as was the unique relationship between picture and
text. But the vogue for the emblematic surfaced in Hungary later than it did
elsewhere, was less pervasive, and tended to be dominated more by didactic
religious and moral purpose and by the textual element in the composition.
What is conspicuous by its absence is the printed emblem book based on
amorous and erotic themes and also the emblems stressing the visual side
of sophisticated court and city culture. There is also little or no indigenous
Hungarian engagement with alchemy, music, or emblem theory. In so far as
one is concerned with the history of a genre or with formal and structural
qualities, there are European models and parallels for everything we find in
Hungary. It is clear that most of the influences came from the German-speak-
ing lands of Europe. The delay in the arrival of emblematic material in Hun-
gary as well as its comparatively small impact can largely be explained in
terms of the local historical and social situation, different literary traditions,
and other circumstances. By comparison with the rest of Europe, the Hungar-
ian material has a higher proportion of what we might call >occasional< publi-
cations; emblem books proper also appeared later and in less profusion. The
proportion of emblematic works with pictorial illustrations is also much
lower than in most Western and Southern European countries.
If we turn our attention to the relationship between picture and text, we
can distinguish three main categories. The first consists of regular series of
emblems with frequent links made between picture and text. Works of this
kind - if we count second and later editions of the same title - make up
almost half the entire corpus. The number of emblems in any given work will
vary enormously - between three and a hundred and thirty - but most have
between ten and fifty of them. On occasion, a picture will be re-used in
another work. The second of our three types consists of prints containing
just a textual description of the emblems. There are almost as many works

mental title-page, only appearing less frequently on the first page of the text
as a header, or after the title-page. The relationship of the picture to the
work varies: normally it has some connection with the themes of the work
as a whole, but sometimes it simply refers to just a section of it.
An examination of the circumstances surrounding the creation of em-
blematic material shows that about 80 per cent of the authors involved were
Hungarian, with the other 20 per cent coming from abroad. The list of for-
eign authors involved provides incontrovertible evidence of the vogue for
European emblematics in Hungary. But, although one of Drexel's works may
have been published in Hungary only five years after the death of its author,
there are clear indications of Hungarian dilatoriness in becoming aware of
what was going on beyond the national frontiers: Sucquet's work was only
published in Hungary fifty-one years after it first appeared, and several oth-
ers had to wait more than a hundred years before they, too, were published
there. This delay is significant; in other Eastern European countries the situa-
tion was different: works by Hugo and Gerhard were published in Poland
very much more quickly.
The majority of Hungarian authors were Catholics and most belonged to
a religious order; a smaller number were secular priests. The Jesuits predomi-
nate: about one-fifth of the entire corpus consists of Jesuit emblem books, a
figure comparable to the international average. After the Jesuits come -
some way behind - the Piarists. Apart from these, we have the odd author
or two who was a Benedictine, an Augustinian, a Pauline, or a Franciscan.
Jesuit and Piarist publications were often not the work of a single person but
rather of an entire religious community. The Hungarian situation is unusual.
Protestant authors involved were, without exception, Lutheran preachers,
and among the secular compilers we find doctors, pharmacists, Palatines,
mayors, city judges, secondary-school teachers, university professors, histori-
ans, and principals of secondary schools for girls. This breadth of authorship
shows that in Hungary emblematics was cultivated primarily by members of
the upper aristocracy in direct touch with court culture, by nobles, and by

emblematic forms of expression only occasionally but who played a role in
shaping literary emblematics between the sixteenth century and the eigh-

moralizing emblematic poetry.103 The poem, consisting of ninety-four stro-

its meditative tone and its listing and allegorical interpretation of Stoic views

origin, one can also place alongside the Amicitia János Petki's Fortuna, a

If we now turn to the places where the engravers (or designers) of the
pictures we find in printed works operated, Nagyszombat (Trnava) looms
large; beside it, the role of the Pozsony (Bratislava) and Eperjes (Presov)
engravers would seem also to be significant. Buda, Kormocbánya (Krern-
nica), Kolozsvár (Cluj-Napoca), and Pest are known to have been the opera-
tional base of engravers of just one or two works. The most significant en-
graving centre outside the country was Vienna; other cities, such as
Augsburg, Graz, Nuremberg, and Linz also figure. The majority of engravers
are known to us from a whole variety of graphic work; the emblematic aspect
of their works associated with Hungary has, however, so far escaped the
attention of art historians. Amateur engravers also figure alongside profes-
sionals.
The picture becomes more complex when we examine the places where
our books were actually published. The most important printing centre in
Hungary was the Jesuit university printing shop at Nagyszombat (Trnava),
where roughly a quarter of the prints examined were published. Pozsony
(Bratislava) comes second in order of importance; after these two, come
Eger, Kassa (Kosice), Lóese (Levoca), Kolozsvár (Cluj-Napoca), and Szeben
(Sibiu), other cities only accounting for one or two publications. Of foreign
cities, Vienna once again occupies first place, followed by Antwerp and Graz.
Other cities only occasionally produced publications of emblematic prints
associated with the Hungarian market. Altdorf is one of these: a further
indication of connections with a significant centre of European emblematics.
Tbrning to the dates of publication, the late appearance of emblematics
in Hungary becomes even clearer. If we leave to one side the Zsámboky
editions, which date from the sixteenth century, printed emblematics do not
appear in Hungary until the 1610s with the activity of Kristóf Lackner.
Following this, there was an upsurge from the 1660s onwards, lasting - at
least in respect of first editions - until approximately 1720. From the 1710s,
new editions and publications containing only text (and these were normally
texts available elsewhere in the seventeenth century) come to dominate the
scene. This second flowering of interest lasts until around 1770. Overall, some
two-thirds of the entire corpus was published in the eighteenth century: clear
evidence of the length of time this kind of publication was flourished on
Hungarian soil.

V The English reception of a late humanist emblem
book by a Hungarian author: Zsámboky (Sambucus)
and Whitney
After 1550, late humanist Latin poetry in Western Europe continued to
flourish only in the Low Countries and Germany. In France, Latin and ver-
nacular literature coexisted during the second half of the sixteenth century.
In England, many authors such as William Gager, Walter Haddon, and Chris-
topher Jonson achieved renown as neo-Latin poets and they wrote either
wholly, or mainly, in the learned language. However, a look at the three most
eminent neo-Latin poets of the period reveals that the works of the Scot,
George Buchanan, were published mainly in France and in Portugal, while
the poems of the Welshman, John Owen (a member of Philip Sidney's circle)
were published and presumably appreciated mainly in Germany and the Low
Countries. Thomas Campion wrote in English as well as in Latin. He is val-
ued today for his English poetry, but in his own day he was also a Latin poet.
In the Low Countries, scholarly Latin poetry flourished more than anywhere
else in the second half of the sixteenth century, and thus the few English
poets who continued in the Latin humanist tradition had particularly close
ties with the Netherlands. If we look at the scene some decades later, we
find George Herbert and Andrew Marvell; John Milton is the greatest Eng-
lish poet to write Latin poetry and his Latin works formed an important part
of his literary output.1
The situation was different in central Europe, where neo-Latin poetry
continued to be widely written, particularly by Czechs, Poles, and Hungar-
ians, but also in the Scandinavian and Baltic countries. Both earlier local
traditions and the influence of the German universities were mainly responsi-

The Emblemata
Zsámboky's collection of emblems deserves a prominent place among the
neo-Latin emblem books of the second half of the 16th century. It has been
regarded as the first important example of the moralizing and philosophizing
branch of mannerist emblematic poetry based on the imitatio of classical
models. The book's key characteristics are a pronounced stoicism, a Chris-
tian-humanist orientation, and strong didacticism. It clearly embodies the
author's concept of the emblem, as well as his personal views and attitudes.6

Zsámboky's concept of the emblem is part of his wider poetic theory which
focuses on allegorical imitation. Zsámboky's foreword, with its emphasis on
how the emblem conveys hidden meanings, reveals a profound knowledge
of the ideas of Alciati, Bocchi, Giovio, and Ramus: the esoteric nature of the
emblem is meant to challenge the reader into reflecting about its meaning
and, as a result of the understanding gained, induce him to strive for im-
provements in his behaviour and his life.7
The emblem pictures were commissioned partly by Zsámboky himself
and partly by his publisher, Plantin, after the epigrams had been written. As
a result, the images have a largely supporting or illustrative role to the writ-
ten word. Some of the emblem picturae present a moment in the fable narra-
tive. Pictures and texts stand in a figurative and analogous relationship to
each other. Zsámboky's epigrams are written in numerous individual varia-
tions of different ancient metrical forms, revealing his wide knowledge of
classical authors. Most of his subjects have classical literary origins too. They
are taken from the human world, usually from everyday life, and from nature
and mythology. Zsámboky aimed to include as many aspects of life and as
many lifestyles as possible. Thus, there are emblems on the private life of the
individual as well as on the relationship of the individual and the wider pub-
lic, on practical problems as well as on transcendental values. Fortune, the
Virtues, and the Vices all also figure in many of the emblems.8
Structurally, the epigrams fall into three broad categories. In the first, the
epigram moves from the particular to the general: it begins with the naming
of the topic, with a postulate, with a description, or with an account of a
particular situation. An interpretation follows, and at the end there is a gen-
eralizing conclusion or moral. Epigrams of the second type proceed from the
general to the particular: they begin with a general statement which indicates
the thrust of the interpretation, and this is then followed by an exegesis in
which the thought is applied to a concrete topic or analogy. In epigrams

Whitney's Choice is a loosely structured string of emblems, the result of
a complex process of adaptation and compilation in the spirit of the neo-
Latin poetic lusus. For the most part, the poems correspond closely to exist-
ing texts and images.36 The arrangement of the emblems was influenced to
some extent by the stock at Plantin's printing works. While 207 of the 247
emblem-pictures were re-printed from existing blocks that had been cut for
earlier books, twenty-five pictures had to be copied from elsewhere, and fif-
teen were completely new designs. Printing began with Whitney still compos-
ing, devising, and commissioning; because of this, virtually all the emblems
that were newly prepared for the book, as well as all those that depended
on sources other than Plantin, appear in Part II. There is no overall structural
concept, and the arrangement of the emblems follows no apparent thematic
order. Short sequences of emblems, multiplied and expanded, are character-
istic of the work.
