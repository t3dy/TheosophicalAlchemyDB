## Section: Conclusions

Conclusions
This survey shows how a study of emblematic material in sermon literature
can reveal new information about the inventiveness (or otherwise) of preach-
ers. The different ways it is used indicate a peculiarly limited form of original-
ity. The emblematic character of the material derives, in the first place, not
from the mainly traditional content of the various emblematic elements but
from the way in which emblematic habits of thought and expression were
applied, from the diverse techniques used to incorporate them into the ser-
mon, and from the novel interpretations given to them. Preachers only rarely
created something completely original. Where they did, however, we find
evidence not only of confidence in their ability to shape the material but also
wit and a recognizably individual style.
Starting out with a working definition of emblematics as an identifiable
set of forms of literary expression built around a picture, we quickly recog-
nized that the expression >picture< {imago) was, in both poetics and rhetoric,
a technical term for a particular kind of simile, and one that served, together
with others such as speculum, figura, typus, similitude>, symbolum, pictura,
hieroglyphicum, and emblema, to designate allegorical references of the kind
we find in classical, medieval, and early modern theories of allegory. As the
research progressed, both the theory and the various concepts associated
with literary pictoriality changed; we have now suggested working distinc-
tions between various forms that have hitherto been unrecognized or unre-
garded, and we have a clearer idea of just how a writer might set about
constructing a sermon around a visual concept. As a consequence, it is now
possible to define the process by which the most important rhetorical forma-
tions based on pictures not only came to be closely associated with one other

IX Religious prose: Emblematic biographies
of Jesuit saints
In the past two decades international research has achieved spectacular re-
sults in the area of Jesuit emblematics.1 One clearly defined field of Jesuit
emblematics consists of the series of emblems connected with the lives, mira-
cles, and virtues of Jesuit saints or of Jesuits considered to have lived a saintly
life. Mario Praz noted this special genre of writing on religious subjects and
warned against the arbitrary classification as emblem books of works con-
taining illustrated scenes from the lives of saints with a few lines of text by
way of explanation.2 Since then it has become clear that, as well as the tradi-
tional genre of illustrated biographies of saints, series of emblems about Jes-
uit saints or Jesuit candidates for canonization had been printed from as
early as the first two decades of the seventeenth century in numbers which
grew to keep pace with Jesuit propaganda on behalf of their canonization
candidates. Such pieces constitute a considerable proportion of the heroic-
laudatory emblematic imprints, and an even higher percentage of such mater-
ial produced in connection with outstanding individuals. In these emblematic
biographies of saints, typically structured along the lines of a traditional ha-
giographie narrative, the biographical account becomes fragmented and its
place taken by an episodic relation of key episodes, rather as happens with
the stations of the Cross. By this means the exemplary quality of the life
related is reinforced both verbally and visually until it becomes a clarion call
to those who would imitate it.
These emblematic biographies of Jesuit saints offer us a chance to trace
the emergence, spread, and eventual disappearance of a theme and genre in
the history of meditative, religious prose. At the same time, they supply us
with further information for an understanding of the specialization process
undergone by many of the subjects and occasions for emblematics and cast
new light on the question, hitherto unaddressed, of the extent and degree of
Jesuit assimilation of humanist emblematics.3 These emblematic biographies

The theoretical background and the rise of a genre
The force and impact of the humanist emblem depended fundamentally not
just on the talent and learning of the writer, but also on the very intellectual
and imaginative qualities it demanded. It was these which enabled it to cap-
ture the attention of the reader and to be an effective pedagogical tool. The
main purpose of Ignatius Loyola's Exercitia spiritualia was to recreate his
own spiritual experience in others, reinforcing their belief, deepening their
faith, and reshaping them in a fresh mould. However, Ignatius declined in
his Exercitia to seek a balance between intellectual knowledge and sensory
experience:4 his overriding concern was with the communication and acquisi-
tion of lived religious experience, and so he spurned the abundantia scientiae,
giving preference instead to the conscious use of the senses.
The Jesuits discovered early on the relationship between the humanist
emblem and the programme set out in the Exercitia. The meditations of Je-
rome Nadal and Jacobus Pontanus not only followed Ignatius's prescriptions
but also showed a significant affinity with emblematics.5 Their activity was
soon followed by illustrated editions of the Exercitia,6 among them a seven-
teenth-century edition illustrated with material from a Jesuit emblem book.7
At the same time as they were issuing editions like this, the Jesuits started
to transform the world of emblematic forms and techniques to make them
conform to the Society's own intellectual world view. This was a lengthy
process. In the course of it, they perfected their own theories and discovered
the seeds of them in certain trends that are discernible in sixteenth-century
humanist emblematics, above all in the Italian symbol (or impresa) theory.

The commitment of the Jesuits to the symbol appears first, in implicit
form, in Roberto Bellarmino's imago theory.9 For him, the picture not only
signified an object which can be experienced by actual physical sight but also
had a real similarity to the things of the world which, as in Ignatius's Exer-
cises, can be approached through the senses.10 Bellarmino introduced the
concept of >utilitas imaginis< according to which the imago: (a) is an instru-
ment of education and erudition; (b) serves to foster and increase the venera-
tion of God and the saints; (c) inspires the reader/spectator to emulate the
example; (d) helps maintain in a person's mind the memory of Christ and
the saints; and (e) is an instrument for confession one's faith.11 This theory
allowed the Jesuits to include, on the basis of the utilitas principle, the pic-
ture-like emblem among the literary forms suitable for expressing religious
messages (a list that contains himnus, cantata, apophthegmata, litania, exem-
plum, carmen, elogium heroicum, and so on.).
At approximately the same time the need arose for a new way that might
prove more effective than the old, familiar cult-propaganda of the Middle
Ages, at making the veneration of those Jesuits who had lived a saintly life
attractive. In the campaigns to secure the canonization of fellow Jesuits that
emerged at the turn of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, pictorial rep-
resentation - that is, the evolution of the >vera imago< of future saints, came
to play an important role in what remained a largely medieval >vita et mira-
cula< propaganda effort.12 Virtue anthologies served to bolster the cult of
the candidate, operating autonomously alongside the old >vita et miracula<
approach and with a fresh dynamism injected by what came to be recognized

As we indicated earlier, the first Jesuit emblematist, Jacobus Pontanus
(Spanmüller), extracted the concepts of similitudo and delectatio from hu-
manist emblematics and harnessed them to the idea of utilitas.13 Antonio
Possevino (1593) recommended the symbol as especially appropriate for the
articulation of individual qualities, among them pietas, virtutes, honestas, and
anima. According to him, these are qualities central to sainthood, useful in
heightening awareness of new saints, and a spur to imitado.14 Horatio Mon-
taldo advocated the separation and divorce of the symbol from those forms
of expression accounted suitable for hidden mediation (such as the nota,
aenigma, emblema, parabola, apologus, and hieroglyphum);15 a theoretical
explanation of how this might be achieved was elaborated by Nicolas Caus-
sin.16 What Caussin did was to home in on the symbol and turn it into a
collective category, as the form best suited to Jesuit emblem practice and to
the representation of saints, and then identify suitable similarity (similitudo
idonea) - already deemed by Montaldo a vital feature of the symbol - as
the key concept in describing just how an emblem functions. From this point
onward, similitudo idonea became an essential requirement in the represen-
tation of saints and emblem and symbol became synonymous for the Jesuits,
each constantly usurping the function of the other.
Alessandro Donati's approach to emblematics was predominantly func-
tional.17 The chief claim of the symbols he defined as >figurata epigrammata<
is that they >monent et docente As an example, Donati took the Ignatius
Loyola symbols of Fammiano Strada: in addition to the functions mentioned,
he says, they also satisfy the requirement of interpretability. Silvestro Pietra-
santa considered the sobriety, or dignity (dignitas), of the symbols chosen
especially important;18 dignity could be achieved by a laudatory and respect-
ful pictorial and literary style of representation.

Jesuit emblem centres and authors at the beginning
of the seventeenth century
The earliest emblematic biographies of Jesuit saints were composed in the
major Jesuit colleges of Europe at the end of the sixteenth century and the
beginning of the seventeenth, initially as anonymous, collective enterprises.28
The first centres of such production came into being in Italy.29 Although the
histories of the Society do not go into detail about festivities at individual
colleges, it is fair to assume that the pattern established in Rome was then
imitated by other Italian colleges wishing to stage ceremonies. This was the
period when Fammiano Strada was working in Rome, and he was acknowl-
edged by several Jesuit emblematists to be their master. A number of Stra-
da's symbols were exhibited to the public at gatherings of Marian sodalities
in that city.30
At the beginning of the seventeenth century, Mantua, Palermo, Naples,
and Milan were still significant Italian centres of emblematic production. In
Mantua, for example, Ignatius's virtues were displayed in the form of >epi-

Structure and function
We define a series as a composition associated with the life of a saint, orga-
nized according to chronological or other principles, and consisting of more
than two autonomous emblematic constructions. On occasion we have also
included in our investigation individual emblems, not arranged into a series,
but which represent a single scene, virtue, or miracle from the life of a saint
or the cult associated with him.
There are two basic structures for such series and they are easily distin-
guishable from each other. In the first, the material is ordered in terms of
the life of the saint; in the second, in terms of his virtues. These two structures
were common from the first half of the seventeenth century onwards.
From the beginning of the seventeenth century, the standard structure for
the emblems consisted of a concise text describing an episode in the saint's

[Page 209]
201
life, a motto (lemma), a picture or picture description, and an explanation
either in verse or in prose. The emblems of saints we find in emblematic
compendia and textbooks are rather different: there, because of the purpose
of such works, we get a didactic prose explicatio and, in addition to the pic-
ture (or picture description) and motto, a further explanation of the origin
and meaning of both pictorial expression and lemma, and an account of why
the particular picture is appropriate for presenting this saint (similitudo,
transfictio). Carlo Bovio opted to adapt the above scheme for teaching
purposes and he developed the lemma and the picture, by dint of the inclu-
sion of multiple explicationes, into three separate literary forms: prose, epi-
gram, and elogium.54 Others, such as Gábor Hevenesi and Kazimierz Wierus-
zewski, followed Bovio's example and included multiple explicationes,
Hevenesi composing explicationes in both prose and verse, while Wieruszew-
ski favoured two poems (epigramma and elogium).55 For his series presenting
Jean-François Régis, Le Jay employed the so-called emblemata duplicata
form, linking, by mean of a common inscription, two independent emblems,
each consisting of picture, motto, and epigram.56 The use of anagrams on the
saint's name was relatively rare.57
Of these two main types of series, the first - that based on the saint's
life - was the preferred option. There are three variants on it:
(a) the series follows a chronological sequence, displaying the life of the
saint from birth to death (series like this were composed for beatifica-
tions and canonizations);58
(b) (a more popular option) the emblems are arranged in a vita-et-miracula
structure,59 with an emblematic representation of the saint's death con-
cluding the section on his earthly life and connecting it with the miracles
ascribed to him after his death (there are several variants on this basic
pattern);60 and
(c) an expanded vita-et-miracula structure, now including the events sur-
rounding the saint's canonization. A good example would be Antonius
Maurisperg's series of sixty flower symbols composed in 1726 on the
figure of Stanislaus Kostka.61 The first three of these symbols repre-
sented the three qualities most characteristic of the saint: veneration of

Hie relationship between picture and text: Sources
Emblems of the lives of Jesuit saints were composed in a particular way. The
central feature of the method involved was that authors used and reinter-
preted humanist emblematic material from the sixteenth century while
adapting, transforming, integrating, and combining the emblems and their
motifs in accordance with Jesuit canonization strategies and the larger goals
of the Society. One further feature is that they successfully linked such em-
blems to the goals of the Society by using and consciously adapting classical
works edited by humanists. The classical literary concerns prescribed by the
Ratio studiorum70 are on show in equal measure in the pictures of the em-
blems, the mottoes, and the explicationes, a ploy which also ensured that
interpreting and understanding the emblematic material employed would not
be problematic.
Thus, we can see a double process of mediation at work. On the one
hand, Jesuit hagiographical emblematics christianized classical secular motifs
that had been popular with humanist emblematists while, on the other, it
transmitted the ideology of the Society to a broader public. We have selected
a few examples to demonstrate the regularity with which, in emblems of
Jesuit saints, the explicatio was a piece of original work while the motto and
the imago were borrowed.

emblematist) for the canonization of Stanislaus Kostka (1726) with the list
of mottoes in the Henkel/Schöne compendium allows us to classify these
emblems into four groups on the basis of the relationships between motto,

(vi) Transferability of motifs to other Jesuit saints. A motif used for one saint
could also be employed for another Jesuit candidate for canonization, who
was either endowed with similar qualities or to be canonized along with him.

ple, appears in Pietrasanta with the motto >Alterutra clarescere flamma< and
there represents the fame of the sanctity of Aloysius Gonzaga. Pietrasanta

(vii) Transfer of a motif from a non-Jesuit saint to a Jesuit one. The impresas
composed by Girolamo Ruscelli for famous people include one for Charles
Borromeo. In it, we see the widely popular motif of the stag festooned with
snakes and running towards a spring, together with the motto >una salus<. In
the explicatio, Ruscelli develops the biblical metaphor of stag - first the
person of Christ, then a verse from Psalms (Ps. 42(41): 2) - to explain the
image of the spring of clear water: Borromeo longed to hear the Word of
God (the clear water), just as the stag wishes to free itself from its sins (the
snakes) in water from the spring.
Typotius's uses this same image for Borromeo in his impresa of cardinals.
His commentary, here more extensive than Ruscelli's, whom it does not men-
tion, refers to one of the classical sources of the motif, the natural histories
of Oppian and Pliny, and adds that the representation is fitting for the
cardinal because of his virtues, his clear thinking, and his strength. Pietra-
santa, too, uses the same image for Charles Borromeo, who was canonized
in 1610 (see Fig. 70). In his brief explicatio, unlike Ruscelli and Typotius who
treated the stag and the spring together as one single motif, he focuses on
the spring and interprets it as water drawn from the source of salvation
(Isa. 12:3).94
