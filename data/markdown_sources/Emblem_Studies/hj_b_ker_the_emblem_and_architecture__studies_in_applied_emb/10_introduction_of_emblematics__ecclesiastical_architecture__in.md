The Emblematics of J.B. Santini's Church Our Lady of the
Visitation in Obyctov
LUBOMIR KONECNY
Charles University, Prague, and Prague College of Studies
in Art and Architecture
Jan Blazej Santini-Aichl (1677-1723), an architect of Italian origin active in
the era of the Bohemian Baroque, undoubtedly ranks among the most
original artists of the time. 1 The artist designed a number of sacred and
secular buildings that are often distinguished both by a unique ground plan
and by unusual architectural elements. Two good examples are Santini's
Church of the Ascension of the Virgin in Kladruby, built 1712-26, and the
Church of Saint John of Nepomuk in Zelenâ hora u Zdâru, built 1719-22
(Figs. 1 & 2). 2 The latter was built on the land of the nearby Cistercian
cloister in Zdâr, whose learned abbot Vâclav Vejmluva commissioned a
number of buildings from Santini. One of them was the monastery guest
house in Ostrov nad Oslavou, which is laid out in the form of the letter
"W," alluding to the abbot's name.3 Similarly, the windows and lantern of
the Church of St. John of Nepomuk in Bestvina have the form of a six
pointed star, one of the attributes of that saint. 4 A further example is a
charming little church in Chrasice, completed in 1721, which is stylized in
the shape of an episcopal mitre-probably because its construction was
ordered by Bishop Vratislav of Mitrovice.5 From these and other buildings
designed by Santini it is evident that not only their interior furnishings or
small symbolic details are the bearers of the buildings' significance, but also
their ground plan and the spatial conception of the architectural whole.
The case in point here will be the parish Church of Our Lady of the
Visitation in Obyctov, a small village in southern Moravia, which was built
posthumously according to Santini's design (Fig. 3 & 4).6 Although in the
case of Obyctov the details of the commission are not known, "tradition has
it that the ground plan of the church in the form of a tortoise was proposed
by the abbot [Vejmluva] himself" (Figs. A & B).7 1 intend to argue that the
zoomorphic reading of the Obyctov church is not a manifestation of our
wishful thinking or uncontrolled imagination, but a legitimate reconstruction
of Santini' s original intention. This seems to be reinforced by the fact that
the church was comissioned by the Ab bot of Zdâr and designed in 1722. W e
must also take into consideration that the "tortoise aspect" of the Obyctov

church was more obvious before 1798 than it is today. In that year the
sacristy was replaced by a new four-sided tower, which significantly altered
the appearance of the church.
As early as 1911 and with no further discussion, the tortoise shape of
the ground plan of the Obyctov church was called "a symbol of permanence
in faith [Symbol der Glaubensbestandigkeit]. "8 Recently a longer, more
detailed interpretation has appeared. "The Visitation church in Obyctov,"
Jaroslav Petru argues,
was to have been a place for pilgrims where the statuette of the
Madonna was kept, their shield, i.e., Latin: testudo. And
because testudo originally meant 'tortoise,' the architect decided
to lay out the church like the body of a large tortoise ... And
because testudo also means 'vaulting,' the oval nave is decora
ted with fiat vaulting throughout the entire space between the
supports.9
This interpretation at least has the advantage that it seems to be in line with
well-known architectural texts. As Vitruvius writes about "foreign tribes"
in De Architectura Libri Decem (II, i, 4) composed ca. 40 B.C., in his
account of "The Origins of Building":
. . . fronde ex luto tegentes efficiunt barbarico more testudinata
turrium tecta.
[They cover it with leaves and mud, and thus construct the roofs of
their towers in a rude form of the "tortoise" .]1°
Apparently, Isidor of Seville was expanding on this passage when he wrote
in his seventh-century encyclopedia (XV, viii, 8):
Testudo est camera templi obliqua. Nam in modum testudinis veteres
templorum tecta faciebant; quae ideo sic fiebant ut coeli imaginem
redderet,quod constat esse convexum.
[Tortoise refers to the oblique vaulting of a temple. It is so called
because the ancients used to make roofs in the manner of the
tortoise's shell. They did so in order to provide an image of heaven,
of which it is said that it is convex in form.]11

However, 1 find this explanation too general. It is well known that sacred
statues and pictures of the Virgin were kept in a large number of Baroque
sanctuaries, including several designed by Santini, but only the church in
Obyctov is in the shape of a tortoise. Given what we know about the
character of the symbols in Santini's late buildings, this explanation of the
form of the Obyctov church does not seem specific enough; most of the later
buildings allude precisely through their ground plans or individual archi
tectural elements quite specifically to the person of the patron (his or her
name, rank, or title) or, in the case of sacred buildings, to the saint or event
to which they were consecrated.12 So the key question remains: why a
tortoise?
If we wish to discover what testudo or tartaruga meant in the
symbolic language of the period, there is no better first source than the most
complete compilation of Baroque symbols-Filipo Picinelli's Mundus
Symbolicus. On five folio-size pages we find thirty-five possible applications
of the tortoise as a symbol. 13 According to the first and most important
entry, the tortoise is a symbol of feminine chastity (mulieris castitas).
In ancient art beginning in the sixth century B. C., the tortoise was one
of the attributes of Venus in her manifestation as Aphrodite U rania or Venus
Coelesta, i.e., the goddess of moral, matrimonial love.14 Pausanias in his
Guide to Greece (VI, 25) described perhaps the most famous example of this
iconographie type-the statue of gold and ivory created by Phidias for the
sanctuary of Venus in Elis. It is precisely this famous statue that Plutarch
refers to in his "Advice to the Bride and Groom" (32):
Phidias made the Aphrodite for the Eleans with one foot on
a tortoise to typify womankind keeping at home and keeping
silence.15
Natural historians of antiquity, namely Pliny (Nat. Hist., XI, lxviii, 180)
provided the basis for this moralistic interpretation through their description
of the tortoise as an animal without a tongue that never leaves its shell.
Plutarch's explanation of the tortoise as a symbol of the behaviour
expected from a good wife was a characteristic interpretation of the motif
during the Renaissance as well. One of the emblems in the famous collection
of the Italian lawyer Andrea Alciato Emblematum liber, first published in
Augsburg in 1531, depicts Venus with her left foot resting on a tortoise
(Fig. 5).16 The motta taken from Plutarch, "Mulieris famam, non formam,

vulgatam esse oportere" [A woman's reputation, not her form, should be in
the public eye], is supplemented by the following epigram:
ALMA Venus, quaenam haec facies? quid denotat illa
Testudo, molli quam pede Diua premis?
Me sic effinxit Phidias, sexumque referri
Foemineum nostra iussit ab effigies:
Quodque manere domi, & tacitas esse puellas,
Supposuit pedibus talia signa meis.
[Bounteous Venus, wherefore this appearance? What is the
meaning of that tortoise which you, goddess, press down with
your soft foot? This is the way in which Phidias has represented me,
and he enjoined that the female sex should fashion itself after my
image: and so that it would appear seemly to maidens to stay at
home and keep silent, he placed such signs under my foot.]
Alciato' s collection of emblems was published in the sixteenth and early
seventeenth centuries in about one hundred editions and translations; and a
large number of emblems was inspired by Alciato's bestseller. As an
example I present emblem no.50 from a book by Hadrianus Junius published
in Antwerp for the first time in 1565 (Fig. 6).17 This emblem's motto reads
"Uxoriae virtutes" and its epigram tells us that the depicted figure-standing
on a tortoise with keys in her right hand and left hand in front of her
mouth-has the following significance: A woman should not stray too far
from home; she should take care of her husband's property and should not
talk too much. We find the identical emblem in George Whitney (1586), and
similar ones in La Perriére (1536), Guillaume Gueroult (1550), Giulio
Cesare Capaccio (1592), Hieronymus Reusner (1587), and by many others.18
This brief summary shows that the tortoise was widely viewed as a
fitting symbol of the good wife in the period, used not only in humanistic
literature written mostly for scholars, but also in vernacular didactic writings
such as La Pelfecta Casada by Luis de Le6n and Glazemaker's Deugdelyke
Vrou. 19 However, the popularity of the motif was not enhanced only through
literature. It was probably supported also by the fact that at least one ancient
statue of Venus kneeling on a tortoise was well known since the beginning
of the sixteenth century. 20
It is well known that iconographers of the Catholic reform tried to use
as many motifs as possible from the old symbolic tradition. Hence we find

a female figure with a tortoise on her head on the left wall of the Mark
grafen Chapel in the Roman Church of Santa Maria dell'Anima, which was
decorated with frescoes by Francesco Salviati in 1549-50.21 On the basis of
the tradition represented by the names Pausanias, Plutarch, and Alciato, we
could easily consider the tortoise a symbol of the virtues of a good wife.
Moreover, the sacred context indicates that we are witnessing the beginning
of the remarkable process appropriately called by William S. Heckscher "the
transformation of nude Venus into veiled nun. "22 The unique visual docu
ment of the culmination of this metamorphosis is the personified pudicitia
[m orality, chastity, purity, innocence], as described by Cesare Ripa in his
Iconologia (Fig. 7):
Vna giouanetta vestita di bianco, in testa habbia vn vela
dell'istesso colore, che le cuopre la faccia sino alla cinta, con
la destra mana tenghi vn giglio parimente bianco, ... Sotta al
destro tiene la testuggine, per dimostrare, che le donne pudiche
deuono stare assidue nelle case loro corne fà la tartaruca nella
sua casa datale dalla natura, ...
[A young maiden all dressed in white who should have on her
head a veil of the same col or, covering her face and reaching
down to her waist. In her right hand she is holding a lily ...
Under her right foot she has a tortoise in order to indicate that
modest ladies should stay assiduously in their house, just as the
tortoise stays in the house which nature has given it.]23
Plutarch is once again cited as the main authority; others cited include
Christian authors and the Bible, especially in connection with the lily.
However, this "christianizing" of the tortoise began in medieval literature,
where the animal is often used as a "paradigm of the behaviour of a modest
Christian wife. "24 Hence, in a broad historical perspective, we see that the
common denominator of all the aforementioned interpretations, antique
humanist, and Christian, was the idea of staying at home, manere domi.
This idea magnetically attracted diverse passages from the Bible, Christian,
and pagan classic works. One of these is Luke 1 :56, the passage which
concludes the Visitation, the very event to which Santini's church was
consecrated: "And Mary abode with Elizabeth about three months, and she
returned to her own house." And 1 would like to mention in this connection
that the important place the Visitation occupies within the narrative cycle of

the Immaculate Conception and miraculous birth of Christ seems to
correspond with the following information about tortoises provided by Pliny
(Nat. Hist., IX, xii, 37): "Sorne people think that they cherish their eggs by
gazing at them with their eyes. "25
The answer to the question, "Why a tortoise?" can perhaps be drawn
from the preceding explanation. The intention to consecrate the Obyctov
church to the holiday of the Visitation of the Virgin evoked a chain of
associations connected with the concept of home and staying home in the
mind of the educated abbot Vejmluva. Those crystallized in the symbol of
the tortoise through the tradition described above. The zoomorphic ground
plan of the church is consequently connected with its consecration. As noted
above, the Obyctov church has an explicitly iconic character. We can even
call it one of the most remarkable manifestations of Baroque concettism in
architecture. Like Ripa's figure, Santini's church literally stands on a
tortoise, it is adorned in the white apparel of chastity and covered by the
veil of the roof.
At the same time, it is, however, necessary to point out that although
Santini's church in Obyctov is unusual in its conception, it is certainly not
unique. It can be grouped with those buildings whose ground plans and
spatial lay-outs reflect their function or consecration. 1 am not thinking of
such trivial examples as the use of a triangular ground plan for a church
consecrated to the Holy Trinity, but rather cases similar to the "emblematic"
design of the Obyctov church, the decoding of which assumes a knowledge
of specific literary and iconographie traditions. A history of this type of
architectural design and symbolism, however, still remains to be written. 26
