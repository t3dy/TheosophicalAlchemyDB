## Section: Introduction

Introduction
The seventeenth-century restoration of the Temple de Lyon offers us a clear
example of how the employment of the emblematic mode in the construction
and the decoration alike of a major civic scheme endowed it with an
extraordinary wealth and complexity of meaning, and demonstrates some of
the ways in which architecture could creatively enter into this mode of
conception. Here we have something far removed from the mere exploitation
of buildings as neutral supports for symbolic decorations, screens onto
which emblems could be "applied." Instead they perform leading roles in a
multimedia and multivalent rhetoric, by acting emblematically.
The Autel de Lyon
In 1645 the Lyonese Consulate, under pressure from the city's
Governor, Nicolas de Villeroy, and its Lieutenant, Camille de Neufville, 1
laid the foundations for a new Hôtel de Ville, larger and more fashionable
than its Parisian counterpart. Following the completion of the main structure
in 1655, the frontispieces of official civic publications exploited realistic
images of it within allegorical contexts, to symbolise civic power. In Lyon
dans son lustre (1656)2 it is collaged together with a lion, whose skin serves
as map of the city, focusing on the new building, and bearing the four
Consuls' arms on its paws and the Provost's on its forehead; the three fleurs
de lys in the sky above enable this lion also to be read as the city's arms.
In Les Forces de Lyon (1658)3 a lion strides across the square in front of
the town hall, whilst Renowns carrying the Governor's and Lieutenant's
arms fly overhead.

effectively provided some of the mottoes required to make these paintings
serve as emblematic figurae, along with unambiguous explanations of the
allegorical programme. Subsequent published descriptions of the building,
written again by these Jesuits, would progressively reveal further levels of
meaning. Allegorical rhetoric in many forms-including emblems-is thus
shared here amongst constructed spaces, painted walls, danced ballets and
published texts.

Louis failed to fulfil the town's expectations of extended privileges,
let al one of acquiring capital status. Moreover, soon after his visit, he
abolished the post of Prime Minister, which Nicolas de Villeroy had
assumed would be his. Henceforth the city government turned its back on
Paris, and official accounts of the decorative scheme reinterpreted it
accordingly. The zodiac paintings no longer referred to Louis but instead to
Lyons, symbolising its major trades and providing lessons for its magist
rates. For instance, the centaur in the Sagittarius picture is to remind us of
Lyons' horse tracte. The moral les son declared to the magistrates through the
same painting is drawn from a tiny detail in it, the crown at Chiron's feet.
This is to admonish magistrates to defend the rights of the Crown, and to
consecrate themselves utterly to the service of their prince.18 The program
me thereby fitted the room's new function, no longer a royal reception
chamber but a council chamber, to be occupied by local merchants who had
been elected Consuls or Councillors. Separating motta from figura had
evidently prolonged the life of these emblematic paintings, or rather had
given them the chance of a second life.
Likewise the central panel depicting "Le Soleil au Signe du Lion"
quietly dropped its royal allusion, in order to eulogise the city authorities
instead, now being interpreted as "Authority supported by Force. "19 It is
more than coincidental that two Consular commissions from the early 1660s
also chose this topos. The frontispiece to the Hagiologium Lugdunense,
written, of course, by a Jesuit from the town college,20 includes a celestial
sphere turned to the House of Leo, immediately above the Consuls' arms;
the Consular token (jeton) issued in 1663 shows a Golden Age landscape,
its sky filled by a section of zodiac strip with Leo at its centre, representing
the Consuls' care for their city.21

The Temple de la Sagesse
The restored temple, however, had now spread beyond the town hall's
walls. The official account of the Hôtel de Ville published in 1661 describes
it as a "twin building," its other half being the Jesuit-run Collège de la
Trinité nearby. 36 This theme was reiterated bath in Trinity Sunday sermons
delivered to the Consulate37 and in a J esuit theological treatise. 38 In 1662-
3 the Consulate paid for the decoration of the college's Grande Cour as "Le
Temple de la Sagesse" [Temple of Wisdom] 39 , thus alluding, albeit in
Christianised manner, to the second dedicatee of Lyons' Roman temple,
Minerva.40 This was evidently a suitable allusion for an educational
institution; furthermore it also enabled an allusion to the alternative
secondary dedicatee, Rome, which was equally appropriate for the Jesuit
community housed there.
The double theme of the glorious Roman past and of subsequent
Christianisation was invoked in the entrance gallery, through its paintings
of three temples of wisdom: the pagan Temple of Minerva, Solomon's
temple at Jerusalem, and Justinian's Haghia Sophia at Constantinople41 in
;
the last case construction was attributed to a master-mason called Ignatius,
thus implying the role of the Jesuits, as St. Ignatius Loyola's "workmen,"
in the restoration of the Lyonese temple.42
The nature of the type of wisdom invoked here was explicated through
the five registers of painted emblems and other symbolic images decorating
the four courtyard walls: allegorical figures of the subjects taught in the
college together with medallions of eminent exponents (not to mention
related hieroglyphics, bas reliefs and the like) suggested wisdom could be
attained through studying the arts and sciences in this college; emblematic
sundials, with symbolic gnomons, implied the Jesuit fathers' prowess in
advanced mathematics, thus indicating their superiority as purveyors of such
knowledge. Yet the central panel, depicting Louis XIV under the guise of
Salomon, receiving wisdom from on high, 43 emphasised its unique source
as the divine one; moreover, a contemporary sermon elaborating on a festal
hanging, designed to complement this painting, indicated that this divine
Wisdom was being conveyed to the monarch by none other than the
Pope.44

The Restoration of the Temple de Lyon
The overall scheme now concerned the temple's restoration, rather
than the temple itself. lndeed, the Grand Escalier by which one acceded to
the Temple d'Auguste (within the town hall's Grande Salle) was now
decorated as "The Pire of Lyons "46 to underline this point. The painted
walls and ceiling illustrate Seneca's account of the fire which destroyed the
city-including its temple-in Roman antiquity; they also showed its Good
and Evil Destinies,47 the former needing to triumph over the latter in order
that the city could arise phoenix-like from its ashes, and even "greater than
before," as the Stoic had prophesied.
The first painting, of the Pire itself (and indeed of its blazing temple)
was the only major one in the entire scheme to bear a motto, "Una Nox
interfuit inter urbem magnam et nullam" [A single night intervened between
this town being the greatest and nothing]. This quotation from Seneca was
far from neutral, however, since recent local historians had progressively
added allegorical interpretations to the original narrative of this incident
from Roman antiquity; in the 1660s a parallel was drawn quite explicitly
between the city's physical destruction overnight by fire in Roman times
with its spiritual destruction due to its overnight capture by Huguenots a
century before, which also destroyed many Catholic churches, or
"temples. "48 The motto applies at once to the painting immediately above it,
to all the paintings decorating the staircase, and indeed to the entire (now
tripartite) decorative scheme. As with Raphael's well known painting of the
"Incendio del Borgo" in the Vatican, it invokes a historical incident, one of
physical destruction, as a metaphor for spiritual destruction, in such a way
as to imply the Church's sole agency in effecting the subsequent restoration.
Significantly, the new Hôtel de Ville stood on the very site of the Huguenot
Temple de Lyon49 , thus materially demonstrating how the destruction of
this "pagan" temple was prerequisite to the current restoration, which would
in turn assure Lyons' attainment of even greater glory.

The stated theme of fire, the first of the three spaces encountered by
the visitor, is the Pire, and the covering of ail its wall and ceiling surfaces
with imagery pertaining to this endows the space itself with the quality of
fire. The visitor is thus unable to accede to the sacred space of the new
temple without first being purified by passing through fire; in fact the
entrance to the Temple d' Auguste physically abuts the painting of the
blazing temple, in such a way that one effectively passes through that old
temple in order to enter the reconstruction of it in the Grande Salle.
The ballet performed to commemorate the completion of the nearly
quarter century long project of construction and decoration was entitled
"Lyon rébâti, ou les destins forcés" [Lyons rebuilt, or the Destinies
constrainedJ53 (emphasis added). Occurring in 1668, it also celebrated the
centenary of the final eradication of Huguenot powers in town through the
legal Catholicisation of the Consulate. It opened to reveal a backdrop
representing "Le Soleil au signe du Lion" [The Sun at the Sign of the Lion].
Then it recounted, by means of allegory, the tale of Lyons' foundation,
destruction and, under the government of a Consulate led by the Neufville
Villeroy family, its restoration by Religion. Its restored, Christian temple
surpassed its pagan precedent of antiquity, in terms of international renown
as well as religions purity.
The medal distributed at the conclusion of the ballet bore an emblema
tic reverse, showing a figure of Eternity bearing a phoenix: the phoenix's
resurrection from its own ashes ushers in a new Golden Age.54 As the
ballet had indicated in some detail, Nicolas de Villeroy and Camille de
Neufville had forced the Destinies to restore their town, indeed to make it
greater than before, thereby assuring it of eternal fame.
