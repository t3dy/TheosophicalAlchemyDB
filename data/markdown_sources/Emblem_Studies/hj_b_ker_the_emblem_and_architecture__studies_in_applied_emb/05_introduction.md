to consider the specific, even idiosyncratic function of bath bimedial genres,

Ecclesiastical buildings are the subject of two essays. Annemarie
Sawkins considers royal and imperial emblematics in the architecture of
François I, with special reference to the late Gothie architecture of Notre
Dame at Senlis. While the pre-emblem tradition of royal initials and de vices
in secular architecture is both political and more commonplace, these
features are also found in ecclesiastic architecture thus implying that the
study of "applied emblematics" needs to be extended to all building typolo
gies.
Lubomir Konecny investigates the emblematic conception of the parish
church of Our Lady of the Visitation at Obyctov, built posthumously accor
ding to the design of Jan Blazej Santini-Aichel. The ground plan reveals the
contours of a tortoise, which it is shown was traditionally understood a
symbol of feminine virtue, and thus provided an emblematic concetto for a
building dedicated to a sacred event in the life of the Virgin Mary.
The last two essays turn the question around in order to consider the
role of architecture in the printed medium of the book. On the basis of a
rich selection of examples Dietmar Peil shows that architectural motifs play
a significant role in both emblems and frontispieces. They can fulfil a
decorative function, but they also frequently embody the main significance
of a central emblematic cluster, contextualize the main motif or symbolize
important secondary meanings.
In his essay "Respiciendo et Prospiciendo" Hans J. Bôker investigates
the significance of the allegories of Architecture and Sculpture on the title
pages of Leoni's editions of Palladio's and Alberti's treatises and their
implication for contemporary political understanding of Classical vocabulary.

The Investigation of Emblem Programmes in Buildings:
Assumptions and Tasks
WOLFGANG HARMS
Universitat München
From the sixteenth to the eighteenth centuries, emblematics and architecture
entered into many associations. 1 I shall not consider emblematics on the
exteriors of buildings, even though the function of buildings could be
explained emblematically to a wider public through the connection with sun
dials or portais or the whole side of a bouse, and even though emblems
could, in a different manner, continually address the passers-by. In the
interiors of buildings attention should be paid not only to permanent
emblems on walls, ceilings and windows; the emblematics of such furnish
ings as pulpits, chairs, tiled stoves and wall hangings also had comparable
functions.
The concept of "programme"2 which lies at the heart of my topic
should not be understood too narrowly. In a wider sense, every emblematic
decoration of space is programmatic, if it is not solely decorative, in that it
addresses the beholder through its content, seeks to teach and thus bas an
appellative function. In a special sense 1 understand by "programme" a
structured collection of emblems, which have been arranged together with
a functional intention; the structural forms can be strict or open.
Unlike "programme," the concept "emblem" creates fewer problems.3
That the third, interpretative and textual part of the emblem is frequently
missing in architecture is easily explained. This is a calculatedly fragmentary
kind of emblem, which is to be understood as an open form, i.e. the
beholder is challenged to provide the conclusion of the emblem by actively
continuing the line of thought to produce a possible interpretation, thus
completing the emblem. Underlying this is an aesthetic that depends to a
high degree on the recipient and functionally relates to the viewer.4
Research on emblem programmes in architecture still relies heavily on
individual case studies, since, but for a few encouraging exceptions,5 we
lack systematic, broadly-based inventories. Insofar as sources can be identi
fied such investigations should consider the once existent but now lost body
of emblems. Thus, it bas now been possible to reconstruct on the basis of
handwritten sources an emblem programme for the Sophienhof, the seat,
long since destroyed, of a Silesian duchess. lt was an emblem programme

commissioned by the Duchess Anna Sophia of Liegnitz and which came into
being within the circle of influence of the poet Friedrich von Logau between
1655 and 1658 in Silesia.6 We are dependent on such old descriptions or
first drafts, where under the sign of the Enlightenment, especially in Austria
under Emperor Joseph II, architectural emblem programmes were destroyed,
whether by a simple painting over or through structural changes, as the new
age turned away from the Baroque.
In several case studies of emblematics in architecture, an old failing
emerged which emblem studies can no longer afford: a writer proved that
an object found in an emblem of a building also appeared in a specific
emblem book, and then believed, in some cases quite rightly, that the speci
fic source had been found, unfortunately neglecting to ask further about the
different function in the new context. An eagle flying toward the sun or a
sunflower turning toward the sun occur frequently in emblem books; such
architectural motifs can have their source-and that means alluding to their
meaning-in an emblem book. But they could just as easily have a non
emblematic source. We know that emblematics is only one specific form of
the many older modes of interpreting the world which we can call, for
simplicity's sake, an allegorical reading of the Book of Nature or an
understanding of the world as "mundus symbolicus. "7 But when we can
really prove that a certain emblem book is the only basis for an emblem
programme in architecture, what do we actually know? In the first instance,
only that the book was known, not the purpose that dictated its transforma
tion into the new medium, into the painted picture in the building.8 And
that, above all, is what research into emblematics in architecture should
investigate. Here it is not sufficient to compare the emblem in the book with
the emblem in the building. It would take historical and socio-historical
studies of the patron, his situation in life und the purposes of the archi
tectural space, which would provide the definite assumptions for determining
the aims or functions of the emblems. Only after the historical and spatial
situation has been clarified, can the intended or actual effect of the emblems
be established, hypothetically or with certainty. Let me give a concrete
example. The emblems in Castle Eggenberg in Graz9 all have as their
source Saavedra's Mirror of the Prince, but that does not imply that their
actual function is derived from Saavedra's interpretation. The meaning of
the sun in the emblem of the eagle's flight, for example, can represent any
"summum bonum," but in this case, instead of a spiritual reading pointing
to God, the context would suggest a political interpretation with respect to
the emperor, given the fact that the whole programme is intended to make

The method for determining function differs in the case of multiple
sources. In order to raise further questions, I will turn to a multi-facetted
example, the manor house Ludwigsburg in northern Schleswig-Holstein.10
The exact dating of the 175 emblems in the so-called "Bunte Kammer" in
Ludwigsburg (Fig. 1) was made on the evidence of the earliest of the eigh
teen emblem books that were used there as models. In the year 1673,
everything that the young nobleman Friedrich Christian von Kielmannseck
did could be related to his intended career at the near-by Gottorf Court of
the Duke of Schleswig. He therefore made it a principle in his architectural
emblems to quote one part of his emblematic source-in this case following
Saavedra, recognition of the power and duty of the ruler [Regit et corrigit]
in this bridle-emblem-and, as a new second part, to incorporate an archi
tectural reference to the place of his political objectives. In another emblem
(Fig. 2) there is a combination of the pictura of wreaths and a motto from
Camerarius with the tilt-yard and stables in the foreground as an architec
tural quotation of the Amalienburg, a part of Castle Gottorf, named after the
wife of the then sovereign, Duke Christian Albrecht in whose services the
lord of the Ludwigsburg manor ho use aspired to make a career. In a similar
manner he added his own castle to the partial adoption of one of van Veen's
love emblems. Kielmannseck's splendid education-the eighteen sources for
the emblems in seven languages belonged to his library-was demonstrated
in his choice of emblems and at the same time rendered functional: a
member of the minor nobility offers himself for higher services by formulat
ing the ethical principles of the European nobility in an emblematic manner.
A doser analysis of the "Bunte Kammer" would show, for instance, that this
sequence of emblems confirms this nobility's acceptance of that social
discipline to which a number of the central demands of Neostoicism corres
pond, as expressed above all by Justus Lipsius: Self discipline, military
perseverance, action and constancy in public misfortune, unwavering organi
zing activity instead of resignation to fate, und beyond the confessional bias
loyalty to the political institution dominates. The number of religious
emblems is small. An exception is the bird of paradise whose supposed
characteristic is to be without feet, which leads to the interpretation that it
avoids all contact with earthly things. Kielmannseck was not lacking in
worldly ethical rigor: he commits himself to "ut mors aut vita decora"
[either death or a worthy life] quoting his source, the Nuremberg
Harsdôrffer.

or decode the pictorial language of the emblems step by step. Here emblems
in architecture are thus themselves emblematized.

In order to appreciate the exceptional quality of great architectural
accomplishments one needs descriptions of average or normal cases of the
reception of emblems in interiors. But precisely these normal cases seem to
be recorded infrequently, probably because they were located in less stately
buildings, which were more subject to changes in fashion. Books purveying
emblems often give the impression of wide distribution (deriving from the
statements of title pages, and the simplification of texts and pictures); they
are found in all the important languages of Europe, often multi-lingual, and
are associated with such publishers and printers as Daniel De la Feuille,
Henri Offelen and the Augsburg family Weigel. Thus the extensive two-part

The study of emblematics in architecture can be fruitfully developed
from the question whether it is possible to determine the history of the
influence of individual emblem books. From case to case this can result in
a broad spectrum of possibilities. The extensive programme on the ceiling
of the knights' hall of Castle Dillingen21 derives solely from Joachim
Camerarius's Symbola et emblemata. One could ask whether the aristocratie
patron intended to convey the scientific substance and Camerarius's inter
pretation of the world22 in the room. The question presents itself in a
different way, when a small selection of Camerarius's emblems are taken,
as in the church of the castle in Silesian Oels23 or in a bourgeois house in
Lübeck24 or in the so-called Lady Drury's Oratory (originally in Hawstead
Hall)25 which was directed to "contemplatio," or again in the collection of
Ludwigsburg emblems. Especially in Hawstead Hall and Ludwigsburg,
where emblems from other sources appear, the question of emphasis should
be addressed which could derive from the career or the world-view of the
patron or in special pedagogical aims.
Yet another aspect cornes to the fore when one asks, which emblem
books dominate as sources in the different regions. For the Catholic regions
in Bavaria and Swabia the question arises, what it means that books by
Anton Ginther and the Symbolographia by Jacobus Boschius are transmitted
or cited most frequently.
The fact that 1 have chosen my examples from the German area in no
way detracts from the fact that emblematics is a European phenomenon. One
should consider regional differences and that always means political and
confessional differences among the assumptions underlying the function of
emblems in buildings. The perspective of the viewer is determined different
ly in the Catholic area than in protestant-pietistic Stetten, for example,
where the individual is expected to discover by himself even the message of
salvation in the emblems. The viewer in the Benedictine Abbey of St.
Lambrecht26 in Styria is guided in a different way when he enters the
library and wants to discover the meaning of the emblems. He is hardly
through the door when he sees this entrance as the location of an emblem:
he is shown that foolish virgins are denied entrance. He must prove himself
an adept of sapientia. There is no doubt that the emblems on the sides of the
supporting columns literally display the supporting function of the library.
In one of them the burning of books of non-catholic theologians is justified,
in another one the reading of pages on the tree of learning is elucidated as

On the whole we should regard emblematics in book form, in archi
tecture and on movable objects as one coherent realm for the functioning of
emblematics. Shared and yet heterogenous traditions are used as a basis and
varied: spiritual and worldly allegory, imprese, heraldics, hieroglyphics,
proverbs and others. When determining the function of emblematics in
architecture, it is certainly worthwhile differentiating between secular and
sacred purposes of the buildings, but the emblems themselves can easily
cross the boundaries between the sacred and the secular. Instead of grouping
narrowly by location, one should examine the object in the manner by which
many other phenomena of the Baroque epoch have been elucidated. One
should ask about the history of piety with the expectation of a socio
historical classification in order to obtain the testimony of a social group.
Rather more difficult procedurally but very tempting is the question about
the history of mentalities. Especially suitable are the terminology and the
research perspectives of intertextuality. One Ludwigsburg emblem, for

Architecture means something. lt has always meant or symbolized some
thing. One's parental home may symbolize the innocence of childhood-but
it might also stand for the opposite, depending on the occupant' s point of
view, on his or her experiences. Y et for the neighbor next door the same
house might signify something totally different.1 As one's life changes and
one re-evaluates past events, one's view of the house in which one spent
childhood will change. What once, to the child, appeared large will resize
itself and appear small. There exists no single view-a situation that com
plicates any attempt at an interpretation. One is confronted with a "shifting
sea" of recollections and interpretations.
What has been said about one's home, i.e., a private building, may
also be said of public buildings like schools, colleges, churches or govern
ment buildings. In all these instances, the individual observer will have his
or her own point of view that determines what a given building means to
that persan. Yet as one shifts from private to public, the complexity of the
meaning increases. Those designing the school, the college, the university,
the State House or Capital have created their own sets of meanings that are
official rather than private. In order to be understood, the official meaning
needs to be communicated. lt is through the art of communication, through
texts and images, that a building can be made into a symbol. Yet even its
meaning, or meanings, are by no means fixed-they may be fixed within the
confines of a society, a state, or a country, but may assume the opposite
meaning once tho se confines have been left. 2
At a time of national crisis, public buildings are often made to assume
a patriotic role, they are made rallying points for a society that seems adrift.
In the 1930s, dictators in Europe used architecture to give their people a
sense of pride and accomplishment. In the United States, the New Deal
Administration transformed downtown Washington creating the so-called
Federal Triangle, government buildings enclosed by Constitution and Penn
sylvania Avenues and 151 h Street. These projects have in common the convi-

In eighteenth-century parlance, the word frequently found to denote
the significance of something is "emblematic of." Soit seems meaningful to
approach the issue of meaning in architecture through emblems.
Like the page of a book, the traditional carrier of a painted or printed
emblem, architecture can also be such a carrier. The addition of a symbolic
image is an ornament to a building that adds meaning, identifies the struc
ture as carrying a unique meaning and/or belonging to a specific persan or
party, be it religious or political. In these instances, the building can be
thought of as wearing a device-on the outside or the inside-comparable to
Renaissance portraits in which the sitter is represented with a device
attached to his or her hat or costume. This symbolic image may be a device
or an emblem (with or without accompanying text). lt must be assumed that
in the case of a public building any symbolic image must be bath recogni
zable and understandable, while in the case of private buildings more
enigmatic images may be appropriate.6

In addition to persona! symbolic images that were more profuse during
the Renaissance and the Baroque period, one must consider national
symbolic images. These national symbols or party ensigns are worn as part
of military uniforms or on the lapel of a civilian clothing. While those
symbols are (mostly) displayed voluntarily, enforced display of symbols
(like the Star of David) belongs to the same category. Attached to a building
the se symbols serve as signs of national sovereignty. One of the most
frequently employed images was the eagle. On the same level as national
symbolic images one must place corporate logos or icons, because both are
linked to an institution rather than to individuals who often play only a
passing role.
If one thinks of a symbolic image as architectural ornament, then its
removal nullifies the meaning previously attached to this building, turns the
building into something el se without, however, necessarily changing its basic
architectural function. It still can be used for a similar or new and totally
different purpose. The Venetians, for example, attached monumental reliefs
showing the lion of San Marco to the city gates and major buildings of those
towns that had fallen under their jurisdiction. As soon as the Venetians had
lost that jurisdiction, the lions were removed but usually in such fashion that
the shape of the lion was still clearly recognizable. The purpose was to
destroy an ensign, but leave enough of it to allow any viewer recognize
whose ensign had been destroyed. Clemens Klott's Ordensburg Vogelsang

no matter who owns the building.

translated into stone, namely the religious nature of the architecture through
the addition of God's band and his, not man's, role in the erection of the
columns and temple. The temple of the Union is God's will and work. What
better image could be employed to visualize the pervasive belief, pervasive

In 1851, a celebration was held on the occasion of the laying of the
cornerstone of the new Senate and House wings of the United States Capitol.
The oration filled with symbolic references was delivered by Secretary of
State Daniel Webster of Massachusetts. The new and larger building was to
symbolize how far the Union had corne in less than a century and to express
that the Union would last forever. What had begun as an experiment turned
out to be a reality to be reckoned with on the political and economic lev el.
The center section of the Capital was not changed. It continued to be
crowned by a dame patterned after that of the Pantheon in Rome, that was
considered the most perfect building and was believed to have been erected
at the time of the Roman Republic.37
Four years after the enlargement of the building was commenced,
Congress decided to replace the old dame by a new one, patterned after the
world's two most impressive dames, St. Peter's in Rome and St. Paul's in
London, and designed by T.U. Walter. Work on the dame was halted, how-

The meaning of the building created in the 1790s and reinforced in the
1860s had not been forgotten at the beginning of the twentieth century. No
building is a more fitting document of the continued existence of an
American architecture than the shrine of the president whose birthday
Americans celebrate today: Abraham Lincoln. The Lincoln Memorial was
built between 1914 and 1922 according to a design by Henry Bacon that is
truly superior to a number of proposals ranging from a Colonnade to
pyramids (that also functioned as smokestacks of an eternal flame), from
Beaux-Arts designs to imitations of classical architecture.39 Bacon was able
to transform the idea of a Greek temple and its image of a god or goddess
into an accessible space, open to all. The over-lifesize image of the
President is noble without the divine features of a god that are so evident in
Horatio Greenough's statue of George Washington, patterned after recon
structions of the image of Zeus in Olympia. What makes the building truly
American is the symbolic use of its members. The 36 columns surrounding
the cella stand for the number of states in the Union at the time of Lincoln's
death; their names are carved in the metopes. The exterior attic walls carry
the names of the 48 states of the Union at the time the memorial was com
pleted.
It all seems so obvious, so clear, and yet many visitors to the site are
oblivious to the meaning of the architecture, as only a few take the time to
read the texts engraved on the walls: the Gettysburg Address and the Second
Inaugural Address. Unless we want to forget the meaning associated with
this site, we have, again and again, to point out that a building, especially
a public one, and aboveall one that honors one of the most important presi
dents of this country, is a les son in stone that deserved to be learned and
obeyed: what counts in life, the personal as well as that of a nation, is what
joins people, not what sets them apart.
