# Roger Bacon and the Incorruptible Human, 1220–1292

## Section: Introduction

Introduction
In a small fragment of a work now called the Liber sex scientiarum, the
English Franciscan friar Roger Bacon (ca. 1220–1292) explained that the
human body had the potential to be immortal. If Adam had remained in
the Garden of Eden, he would have been able to live forever, sustained by
the blessed fruit produced by the Tree of Life. This was God’s original
plan for mankind. But even though mankind had sinned, and thus fallen
from Grace, Bacon understood the Bible to teach that all humans would
eventually reach the ideal state of immortality, albeit only after the resur-
rection. At the moment of the resurrection, each person, both saved and
condemned, would receive an incorruptible, and therefore immortal body,
which would be subject to neither disease, decay, nor aging. Those who
had achieved salvation would live in the New Jerusalem: a physical king-
dom, which they would inhabit with physical bodies. Those who were
condemned, however, would spend eternity suffering the torments of hell,
feeling pain yet unable to die. Despite mankind’s fall, Bacon said, wise
men could learn much about their future bodies during this life. Not only
that, but man could also learn how to make approximations of these bod-
ies, so that their own lives would be greatly lengthened. Though no one
could live forever before God’s judgment and resurrection, wise men
might be able to extend their lives by decades, even centuries, through the
prolongatio vitae—the extreme prolongation of life.

This book examines Bacon’s alchemical and medical theories, and
explains how he believed that the key to extending life lay not in the cur-
ricula as taught in the medical faculties of the universities, but in the study
of alchemy. But what exactly was it about alchemy that made it so vital to
medical practice? Though twelfth- and thirteenth-century alchemy was
generally concerned with the transmutation of metals, Bacon’s alchemy
was a much larger area of study, and encompassed the generation and cor-
ruption of all material things in the sublunary world. It was this aspect of
alchemy, which Bacon referred to as speculative alchemy, that explained
how the four elements of fire, air, water, and earth interacted with each
other to make the basis of reality as man could know it. Thus, the study of
alchemy in conjunction with humoral medicine could explain not only
how the human body worked, but how it interacted with the materials
around it, illuminating the method of prolonging life to extreme lengths.
Indeed, is it even possible to draw a distinction between alchemy and
medicine in the works of Roger Bacon? Though Bacon does differentiate
between the two terms, it is not a relationship of two separate but related
fields, but rather that of primary and secondary—alchemy provided the
ground on which medicine was built, so that medicine without alchemy
was unstable.
Equally important as the study of Bacon’s ideas is understanding the
milieu in which he was creating them. During the increased professional-
ization of the medical field during the thirteenth century, physicians were
becoming more interested in theoretical questions regarding the nature of
disease and aging. Bacon was certainly not writing in a vacuum, and these
discussions occasionally appear in his works. They do not, however, appear
frequently enough to suggest that Bacon received any formal medical
training, or was indeed even actively engaging with physicians or new
Latin translations of Greek medical works. Bacon tended to focus only on
those areas of medical practice that could be resolved using alchemical
means: either through speculative alchemy, which taught about the ele-
ments and the workings of the human body; or through practical alchemy,
which provided the tools to make the medicines that speculative alchemy
envisioned.
The early thirteenth century was also a time of reform for the church,
with the creation of two new religious orders: the Franciscans and the
Dominicans. Created by St. Francis of Assisi (d. 1226) and St. Dominic of
Calaruega (d. 1221), respectively, these two orders changed the public
face of the Catholic Church. Rather than being sequestered in cloisters,

Bishops were, the two mendicant orders traveled and preached, often
using natural philosophy to explain the nature of God. Bacon joined the
Franciscan order in Oxford around 1257, and his often-complicated rela-
tionship with their teachings and his superiors appears throughout his
works. How can the relationship between Franciscan natural philosophy,
especially the study of light as the paradigmatic example of Divine action,
and Bacon’s understanding of incorruptibility best be characterized?
Bacon was highly influenced by Christian scripture, which he incorporated
seamlessly into his program of the prolongatio vitae.
Bacon has been credited as the author of over 100 works, although
most of these are likely spurious, attributed to him in the centuries after
his death as his popularity grew among English alchemy enthusiasts.
Despite the numerous misattributions, Bacon’s authentic oeuvre was
impressive, and the amount of time he dedicated to the problem of pro-
longing life over the course of 30 or so years between 1266 and 1292
shows just how committed he was to the concept. This book relies on the
following works concerning alchemy and medicine, all of which have
ample support for their authenticity. Perhaps the most famous of Bacon’s
works were the Opera, composed of the Opus maius, Opus minus, and
Opus tertium. Completed between 1266 and 1267, the Opus maius was
written at the request of Pope Clement IV (d. 1268), and contains Bacon’s
plans for educational and ecclesiastical reform through the study of arts
and sciences, which Bacon saw as having been neglected in the curricula of
the major European universities.1 In writing the Opus maius to Clement
IV, Bacon wanted to demonstrate that not only were the sciences a solid

The related texts of the Opus minus and Opus tertium were shorter
clarifications and emendations to the Opus maius, and were completed
between 1266 and 1268. Worried about the dangers of travel, Bacon sent
the Opus minus and Opus tertium, should the Opus maius not reach its
final destination.3 These were not merely abridged versions of the Opus
maius: both the Opus minus and Opus tertium contain much longer sec-
tions on alchemy than the original. It is possible that this is an example of
Bacon’s changing attitudes toward the importance of alchemy in his
understanding of nature, but it is also very probable that this was a case of
dispersa intentio. Dispersa intentio was a technique frequently used in
medieval and early modern alchemical manuscripts to prevent the knowl-
edge contained within from falling into what the authors saw as the
“wrong hands”: either people who would use it inappropriately, or were
not worthy enough to understand it fully.4
Sometime after sending the Opera to Clement IV, Bacon wrote three
shorter works specifically on medicine. The first, the Antidotarius, is
largely a description of simple and compound medicines that are available
for purchase. Its most notable feature is that while it contains no explicit
references to the extreme prolongation of life or alchemy, it stresses the
importance of fermentation, and the popular idea that a compound medi-
cine was somehow “greater than the sum of its parts.” That is, a com-
pound medicine’s strength was not equal to the sum of the strengths of its
constituents, but was greater than all of them combined, because during
the process of fermentation, the matter of the compound would receive a
new form, greater than all of the individual forms. Here, Bacon was

Bacon’s next medical work was De erroribus medicorum, in which
Bacon proposed to outline what he saw as the 36 errors of the doctors, by
which they were ignorant of their practice and failed their patients. Like
the Antidotarius, De erroribus medicorum relied heavily on Galenic medi-
cal theory, but greatly expanded the role of alchemy in understanding the
operations of the human body. It is here that Bacon made the claim that
medicine without alchemy was useless, for alchemy was the only subject
that taught about the generation and corruption of all things on earth.
These themes were reworked and elaborated on with great enthusiasm in
the Liber sex scientiarum, mentioned at the beginning of this chapter.
Possibly a fragment of Bacon’s unfinished Scriptum principale or the final
section of the Compendium studii philosophiae, it was likely written later in
Bacon’s life, and describes how three special sciences, alchemy, astronomy,
and perspectiva, can prolong human life to great lengths.5
The final work that deserves attention here is not entirely by Bacon, but
rather consists of Bacon’s introduction and glosses to a supposedly much
older work, the Secretum secretorum. Supposedly a letter from Aristotle to
Alexander the Great, the Secretum secretorum was incredibly popular in
the Middle Ages, and was translated from Arabic several times. It provided
all types of information that an effective ruler would need, as well as medi-
cal, astrological, and talismanic advice. As with most of Bacon’s medical
and alchemical works, the exact date of composition is unknown, although
internal evidence places it after 1264, and likely between 1275 and 1280.6
These seven works, written over a period of almost three decades, dem-
onstrate Bacon’s commitment to the idea that it was possible to extend
human life using a combination of alchemy and medicine. He had good

souls of men, the latter of which were immortal and desired the felicity

had an innate goodness of nature, which was incompatible with death and
misery, and desired participation in God. Once the immortal rational soul
had been united with an immortal body, man could participate fully in
God, which was the highest felicity. For Bacon, any study that attempted
to heal the body and restore even a part of its former glory could not be
shunned lightly.
In the remainder of this book, I will examine the arguments in each of
these seven works, along with Bacon’s mathematical writings, to explore
how he believed that alchemy provided the key to extending life. The sec-
ond chapter focuses on Bacon’s understanding of how human life has
shortened drastically. Bacon, like many other thirteenth-century scholas-
tics, believed that human life had been significantly shortened since the
beginning of the world. The biblical patriarchs lived to be almost
1000 years old, but today men only live to 70 or 80. What has caused this
change, and is there anything that can be done to remedy this? I demon-
strate that Bacon believed that there was no fundamental difference
between the aging of the patriarchs and the aging of modern man, only a
difference in speed. The fact that this shortening of life was not inherently
different, nor mandated by God or nature, meant that it was remediable
using human techniques. I also demonstrate exactly how Bacon believed
the aging process occurred, and that his theories were different than many
of the most prominent ideas at the time.
