The prolongatio vitae was an example of the first and second preroga-
tives of experimental science, which taught how to prolong life in several
ways. The first way was by following the examples of the brute animals,
and then following that with self-experimentation. Bacon described this
process in Liber sex scientiarum:
And they found through experience which brutes use precious stones and
wonderful herbs, and how they use them, and [they found] also the secret
spices and special food and drink that are placed within the stellar rays of
wonderful power, whose proper times may be observed using astronomical
investigation, and can be gathered with perspective instruments, with which
the rays are collected by reflections and fractions, and positioned in whatever
place we may desire, whether in tables or elsewhere [. Thus] the powers of
the stars may be harnessed, and [we can produce] stellified foods, drinks,
spices, medicines, stones, and herbs, suitable things which wise men may
make use of to prolong life and continue health, in order that the failure of
the regimen of health, which we enter in infancy, may suddenly be renewed,
and the passions of old age delayed for a long time.132
These animals had, by their own nature, learned how to prolong their
lives by using stones, herbs, and other natural materials. This was one way
in which experience could be incorporated into the prolongatio vitae: wise
men must follow the examples of the animals. But this passage also contains
another example of how experimental science can be used in medicine: it

Though the Opus maius made experimental science the most important
area of study for learning how to prolong life, it was given equal footing
with two other sciences in the Liber sex scientiarum: alchemy and astrono-
my.134 At the beginning of the Liber sex scientiarum, Bacon said: “In the
proper regimen of the body and of the prolongation of life to the ultimate
natural limit, which God and nature set up, the shining power of astron-
omy, alchemy and perspective, and experimental science will be admired.”135
Bacon tied his interest in transmutational alchemy, with its talk on medi-
cines for stones, to the program of prolongatio vitae, to create a medicine
for people that acted on the same principles.136 In fact, not only was he the
first to connect alchemy to the prolongatio vitae, but he was also the first
to explicitly extend life to a state of “quasi-immortality.”137
In the Opus maius, Bacon again made it clear that he appreciated experi-
ence and practice, especially in understanding pulse and urine, two pieces
of evidence often considered essential for diagnosis in the Middle Ages.
Bacon lauded certain physicians for their ability to make correct diagnoses

the father of medicine, not because he was the first to practice it or teach it
(that would be his father, Apollo),139 but because he was the first to rely on
“the truth of experience.”140 For Bacon, experience was a necessary crite-
rion for effectively practicing medicine, though he considered experience
and experiment to be closely related, and the boundaries between the two
were often blurred.
Bacon was not alone in demanding a certain level of experience from the
medical profession. In fact, testing and experience, especially in pharmacol-
ogy, went back to antiquity. The academic physician would often partner
textual learning with testing to determine the efficacy of pharmaceuticals,
developing rules for such tests that others were expected to follow. Galen
himself had conducted tests on medicines in an attempt to determine their
complexions.141 But physicians did not necessarily have to conduct their
own experiments: they could refer to those done by previous physicians.142
Despite the continual expansion of experimental protocol in pharmaceuti-
cal testing, it was practiced only sporadically—there was no systematic test-
ing of drugs.143 What constituted evidence in medicine was an important
question in the late Middle Ages, but for Bacon, like many medieval physi-
cians, “experience” did not necessarily have to be one’s own experience,
rather that of a trusted authority.
Bacon had a very good reason for including the experience of others
under the heading of experiment. In De erroribus medicorum, Bacon
explained that physicians should be excused for not always having the truth,
because they were unable to experiment in the same way that others were.
Medicine was faced with a problem found in no other discipline—its sub-
ject was the human body, and one could not undertake experimentation on
the human body lightly. As Bacon explained in that text:

Creative and experimental sciences whose experiments are made on inani-
mate objects can be retested many times until all faults and errors are cast
out. But doctors are not able to do this because of the superiority of the
material on which they are experimenting. One cannot arrive at truth except
by means of experimentation, therefore the doctors are excused when they
make mistakes, more than other experimental scientists.144
Here there seems to be an inconsistency between theory and practice in
Bacon’s “experimental method”—the inability of alchemical medicine to
follow the rules for experiment set out in the Opus maius. However, this
was neither a contradiction nor an inconsistency in Bacon’s understanding
of the role of experiment in the sciences—rather, the definition of experi-
ment in the context of Bacon’s writings has to be expanded. Again, “exper-
iment” for Bacon included the experiments and experience not only of
oneself, but of trusted authorities as well. Proof can be found in the
Secretum secretorum: Bacon’s glosses demonstrated that he had seen mirac-
ulous medicines, namely the gloria inestimabilis, at work, although it was
not he who administered them. Bacon wrote: “Indeed I saw a skilled doc-
tor who, through this medicine, cured the greatest man in France after the
king.”145 Though Bacon had not taken the gloria inestimabilis himself, or
even made it himself, he had, in a way, “experienced” its power, and is thus
certain of its effects.
It was thus within Bacon’s “experimental method” to consider the sto-
ries he heard, especially those that come from seemingly reliable sources,
as evidence for the existence of alchemical remedies. The key here was that
the sources are reliable—they were not just assumed to be correct because
they came from an authority, but because they had proof. His work is full
of references to the experiments of other authors (and not just those in the
medical field), including Robert Grosseteste (d. 1253), Avicenna, Petrus
Peregrinus de Maricourt (fl. 1270), and others. A more fruitful approach
is to see Bacon as engaging in the typical experiment of his time—not an
individual, systematic set of experiments done by one person, but rather
the incorporation of experiment and results by others.
The reports of experiences by a trustworthy authority were themselves a
type of “experiment,” especially if they were things that the experimentator
could not have personal knowledge. It is even possible that Bacon

Ultimately, if truth could only come from an individual’s direct experience
with things, then our understanding of the world would be incredibly

Despite allowing for the experience of others to count as experiment,
Bacon did not reject self-experiment entirely. There were ways in which the
doctor could and should experiment, even if they generally did not. The
first method was, of course, using the methods demonstrated by the brute
animals in the Liber sex scientiarum. These animals, such as eagles, snakes,
deer, and even the phoenix, were able to prolong their lives by ingesting
herbs and stones. Somehow, these animals had the knowledge that these
items were good for them, despite not having a rational soul or any capacity
for reasoning. Wise men had been able to prolong their lives by following
in the footsteps of these animals, a perfectly acceptable means of experi-
ment in Bacon’s eyes, and one which ordinary physicians should try
as well.146
The second way for doctors to experiment would be to use alchemy to
prepare a body of equal complexion, which provided the means for pro-
longing human life by restoring elemental balance.147 Wise men and doc-
tors should strive to experiment with alchemy to create this body of equal
complexion. Bacon did not prohibit experiment per se in medicine, only
dangerous experiments that had no backing in alchemical theory.
Experiment was needed to remove the errors that had been copied in
books and would help physicians deal with factors outside of their control,
such as changes in the heavens, innate heat, or even bad translations.148
Twentieth-century scholars tended to criticize Bacon for his lack of
experiment, especially in light of his proposed experimental science. Even
the idea of “experience” from a trusted authority apparently went against
the statement in the Opus maius that authority was not a good way to
acquire knowledge, because only experience could remove all doubt. How

does his approach to experiment in medicine hold up to these claims? If we
look at Bacon from a modern perspective, then certainly, his experimental
practice was lacking. In De erroribus medicorum, when discussing all the
different things that can effectively purge humors and prolong life, Bacon
said that the only one that he had tried was rhubarb, but he had been very
pleased by the results. Specifically, he said, “And I myself use it [rhubarb]
against phlegm according to the words of Aristotle, but I do not use the
gross substance since I know that it is better to extract its virtue.”149
However, this discussion of rhubarb did not appear in the context of per-
forming experiment, but in a debate about misinformation regarding sim-
ple medicines! Most Latin doctors agreed that rhubarb purged choler,
while the Greeks (namely Aristotle) claimed that it worked against phlegm.
Bacon was stating that he successfully used rhubarb against phlegm not to
provide an example of experimentation, but to support Aristotle’s claim
against the Latin doctors. His reasoning that rhubarb worked against
phlegm was thus a mix of self-experimentation and Aristotelian authority.
In fact, very little can be said about Bacon’s personal experimental prac-
tice, at least in terms of the prolongatio vitae. While his mathematical
works show that he was testing ideas, such as burning mirrors, and parts
of the Opera contain sections on alchemy that have enough practical infor-
mation to suggest that he was at least dabbling in practice, there are no
comparable passages in his discussions of the prolongatio vitae.150
Bacon never explained why he engaged in so few experiments concern-
ing health and drugs. However, the lack of experimentation may not have
been omitted because Bacon did not think it was worthy, but because he
did not have the funds to cover such expensive trials. Many of the ingredi-
ents Bacon advised were expensive, and he did not have much money.
Despite coming from a wealthy family, Bacon complained in his introduc-
tory letter to the Pope sent with the Opus maius that he no longer had
money. Bacon admitted that he spent close to £2000 on scientific instru-
ments and books before joining the Franciscan order. Whether it was
because his family lost their wealth in the English war between the Crown
and the nobles, or because the Franciscans frowned upon such spending,
the result was that Bacon did not have the money to source rare ingredi-
ents such as pure gold, pearls, or Ethiopian snake flesh.151

But if it was possible to prolong the human life, then why hadn’t more
people done so? The first, and most obvious reason, was that they simply
did not know how. Bacon said that even wise men like Aristotle and
Solomon, who knew so many things, were ignorant about the prolongatio
vitae.152 Aristotle, for instance, did not know the quadrature of the circle,
and if he did not know something so simple, then how would he be able to
comprehend how to extend life?153 But lack of knowledge about prolong-
ing life may not just have been the result of an individual’s inability to
comprehend difficult concepts. The section of the Opus minus found in
Vatican MS. Reg. Lat. 1317 suggested that this knowledge was deliberately
held from most men by God. The knowledge of the prolongatio vitae was
given by God through divine revelation to special individuals: “But never-
theless neither Aristotle nor Solomon arrived at the same [knowledge].
This great secret is not found except through divine illumination or from
the great wisdom from the Lord God, which I demonstrate in the second
part of the work.”154 With this passage, Bacon could avoid claims that his
medicines were impossible because they had not been used by the great
sages of the past.
Bacon was also concerned about how long one could expect to live
after using an alchemical medicine or fixing the regimen of health. Most
importantly, no one could possibly live forever before the resurrection, so
while his medicines could prolong life, they would not provide immortal-
ity. To do so would be heterodox to the extreme.155 The Bible teaches as

this, although one can only assume that it was because Artephius’ medi-

6 conclusions
Given the nobility of the human body above all other forms of creation,
Bacon taught that alchemy’s greatest use was in prolonging human life and
curing all diseases.161 Alchemy also taught man about the future, post-
resurrection body, which will also be made of a corpus equale.162 Bacon’s
approach to medical alchemy was unique, both in relation to the Arabic
alchemists, who were mainly interested in transmutational alchemy, and to
the medical doctors, who occasionally saw a potential place for alchemy in
medicine, but were, on the whole, unwilling to integrate it into medi-
cal theory.
There are four concluding points which will summarize the material
from this chapter. The first is that Bacon was writing at a time when the
possibility of the prolongatio vitae was not only widespread, but was a
socially and scientifically acceptable pursuit. Many individuals, including
those trained at some of the best medical faculties in the European universi-
ties, believed that humans could and should try to regain some of the time
lost by previous generations. The idea that life had significantly shortened
since the age of the patriarchs was nearly universally accepted, and it made
sense that something should be done to address this. Bacon may have been
unique in his approach to the prolongatio vitae, using speculative and prac-
tical alchemy to confer incorruptibility, and equating this incorruptibility to
the resurrection body, but he certainly was not alone in believing it possible.
The second point which I wish to argue is that Bacon, and the others
who wrote serious treatises on the prolongation of life, did so without pre-
tense. Bacon and his contemporaries fully believed that not only was it
theoretically possible to prolong life, it was doable as well. Both authority
and experience showed them this. The accounts of men who had pro-
longed their lives, which Bacon so frequently cited, were for them

tradition of alchemy, especially that regarding elixirs of health. This sub-

Looking at three major Franciscans who wrote on alchemy (Roger
Bacon, John of Rupescissa (d. 1363), and Vitalis of Furno (d. 1327),
Matus connected the “subjunctivity” of the Franciscan ritual practices to
that of alchemy. In theory, the prolongatio vitae could be achieved, though
in reality, it was not. But this does not accurately represent their endeavors:
Bacon and his contemporaries did not view either their alchemy or their
religious practices as “subjunctive”—both were attainable and had real-
world consequences.
Matus’ characterization of a Franciscan approach to both alchemy and
religious practice as ‘subjunctive’ does a disservice to the thirteenth- and
fourteenth-century intellectual and medical culture in which these men
were living and writing. In saying that such ideas were subjunctive, it no
longer becomes necessary to understand how these men were interacting
with theories of matter, or how they were creating their own. It also makes
it easy to ignore the actual experimental practices that many of these men
incorporated. Bacon’s alchemical theories represented the actual manipula-
bility of the natural world. That is, the descriptions of the creation of the
medicines and their effects on the human body are real processes that can
and do happen, not ideals that can only be achieved in a perfect world.
The third point deals with the different approaches in Bacon’s work on
the prolongatio vitae. He wrote two “Galenic” medical works, De erroribus
medicorum and Antidotarius, which dealt almost exclusively with com-
pounds and simples that treated humoral imbalance. There was little dis-
cussion of a corpus equale, and alchemical practices and theories were only
used to aid the physician in the production of pharmaceuticals. On the
other end of the spectrum, however, are Bacon’s Opus minus, Opus ter-
tium, and the Liber sex scientiarum. These were heavily theoretical works,
which relied on a body of equal complexion to pass its equality on to a
recipient. They involved complicated recipes and incorporated techniques
from other fields, such as optics and astronomy, concentrating stellar rays
into medicines. In the middle of the spectrum are the Opus maius and
Bacon’s glosses on the Secretum secretorum: both works contained a mix

How is it possible to reconcile the theories in these works with each
other? It does not work to say that each group was written at a different
period in his life, as the Opus maius, minus, and tertium were all produced
within 18–24 months of each other, and both the Liber sex scientiarum and
De erroribus medicorum seem to date from late in his life. One explanation
may be found by taking into account the different audiences for which
Bacon was writing. De erroribus medicorum reads as if it were written for
the public, whereas the Opus minus and Opus tertium are more along the
lines of private correspondences. There is ample evidence for the circula-
tion of Bacon’s mathematical works during his lifetime: it is entirely possi-
ble he meant for certain medical works to be circulated as well.164 We do
not know for whom De erroribus medicorum was written, if Bacon even had
an intended person or persons in mind. However, the contents—warnings
about apothecaries, practical advice on preparing drugs, lists of simples,
and the dangers that result from incorrect treatment—and plain, non-
coded language suggest that the reader could be any literate individual,
either those in the medical profession who wish to improve themselves, or
those wealthy patients who wish to protect themselves from medical
“malpractice.”
Such an occurrence would not be unheard of—the main text of the
Secretum secretorum explained how to avoid seeing a doctor unless abso-
lutely necessary.165 Perhaps De erroribus medicorum was written for both
groups: physicians were meant to learn about the dangers of practicing
medicine without a grounding in speculative alchemy, and how their pro-
fession is being degraded by constant in-fighting and ignorance; and the
literate non-practitioner was meant to become aware of the potential risks
of relying too much on medical advice. Indeed, it could even have been
written for other natural philosophers who were unsure of the abilities of
alchemy: either in terms of its feasibility or its applications to anything prac-
tical. In any case, it was not meant to be a practical manual for either alche-
mists or apothecaries—Bacon did not explain how to prepare drugs using
the techniques supplied by alchemy—he merely wished to arouse curiosity.

And if, as Steven Williams has argued, the Secretum secretorum and its
glosses were meant for a court, something akin to Aristotle’s own letter to
Alexander the Great, with Bacon as philosopher and Henry III or Edward
I of England as the great ruler, it would understandably fall somewhere
between the Opus minus and tertium and De erroribus medicorum.166 The
vast range of audiences targeted may explain why there were so many dif-
ferences. A version of the Secretum secretorum dedicated to an English
king would be written so that he could direct his doctors on how to treat
him using a corpus equale—a king would not be making his own gloria
inestimabilis. Likewise, De erroribus medicorum, meant not to instruct but
to arouse interest, would not have the same level of detail as found in the
Liber sex scientiarum, which was meant to instruct.
The final point leads into the next chapter, where I show exactly how
Bacon thought his medicines worked, by incorporating the idea of the
multiplication of species. The multiplication of species not only provided
the explanatory mechanism by which a body of equal complexion was able
to pass its incorruptibility on to the recipient (a corruptible human body),
but also how the full equality, which would provide total incorruptibility
and immortality, could not be passed on. This meant that Bacon con-
ceived of medicines as being able to impart some of their incorruptibility,
and thus aiding the body, but unable to fully change the body, which
allowed him to argue, following orthodox Christian doctrine, that no one
could live forever before the resurrection.

Individual Versus Universal Equality
One question that arises from this examination of Bacon’s understanding
of the corpus equale is this: is there one kind of equality, or is equality itself
a movable goal, adjusted to each individual?22 Gold has a corpus equale:
will all bodies in the resurrection be the same as gold? Bacon agreed that
both Aristotle and Avicenna taught that gold was the only item that had a
body of equal complexion and was thus incorruptible. If gold did not have
equality in its active potential of elements, then it would be corruptible.23
However, Bacon also said that gold could also be made more perfect: in
the Opus maius, Bacon claimed that gold could be made in 30 or 40
“grades.”24 How Bacon reconciled the idea of different types of perfection
will be covered later, but it is important to understand that 24, 30, and 40
grade gold were all incorruptible, so Bacon appeared to believe in a system
of degrees of perfection.
In terms of human health, this was important, as each person had their
own baseline of health. Traditional medical theory taught that some peo-
ple were naturally phlegmatic, some were choleric, etc. depending on the
dominant humor in their body. Did a body of equal complexion bring a
person to some ideal state of equality, in which each individual had the
same equality (and thus no one retained their natural dispositions)? Or
was “equality” subjective to each individual, and the corpus equale only
brought a person closer to their own ideal “equality”? Bacon did not
directly address this question, and thus I cannot point to a specific passage
in his works on the prolongatio vitae where this dilemma is answered.
However, his works on natural philosophy, namely the Communia natu-
ralium and the Opus maius, describe the complexions of the heavens and
their equalities. Bacon’s approach to the equalities of the heavens shows
that a similar approach can be taken to the equalities of human bodies:
that is, there was no one universal equality that fits all humans.
In Aristotelian cosmology, the heavens were considered to be eternal
and unchanging. This was generally accepted in the Middle Ages, with the
caveat that the heavens themselves had been created by God at a point in
time, thus making them both generable and incorruptible. Incorruptibility
and generability were often held to be incompatible: that which was

24 grades, could also remove the corruptions of the human body, thus
prolonging life for many years.81 God provided knowledge of these medi-
cines of equal proportion, such as the gloria inestimabilis, to Adam and his
sons. As time passed, the knowledge of these medicines faded, but there
were some, who either by luck or by knowledge, had been able to prolong
their lives. Artephius created a medicine that allowed him to live for
1025 years, a man during the reign of William of Sicily drank a potable
gold that prolonged his life for 300 years, and Papal letters attested to a
500-year-old German captive who took a medicine meant for a king.82
The result of these medicines was that men had a body similar to that
given to Adam in Paradise and provided to all men after the resurrection,
though not with a full equality of the elements.83 This incorruptibility was
provided via the multiplication of the species of the corpus equale.
Most of Bacon’s humoral medicines would act through the operations
of contrary qualities. Without using a corpus equale, doctors needed to
know the qualities of the medicines being prescribed.84 Medicines had to
be prescribed in opposition to the quality of the disease: if a disease was
cold in the second degree, then the medicine applied should be hot in the
second degree to counteract the disease without damaging the rest of the
body. If the medicine was a compound, the compounder needed to be
wary of how the ingredients would affect the total qualities of the mix-
ture.85 Complicating the process of compounding was that the authorities
themselves were not able to agree on how the degrees worked. In De
gradibus, al-Kindi explained that degrees proceed geometrically in a ratio
of 2 (1, 2, 4, 8, 16), but Averroes’ Colliget held that the progression was
arithmetic (1, 2, 3, 4).86 A corpus equale, be it the gloria inestimabilis or
any other alchemically prepared medicine that had been reduced to its

Previous scholars have noted the universal nature of Bacon’s remedies.88
It is important to highlight the universal nature of these medicines: not

these ideas. The corpus equale was never mentioned in connection with
fears regarding diagnosis, nor how it could help different types of people.

It was alchemy that provided the corpus equale, both practical and spec-
ulative. Speculative alchemy, through its study of the interactions between
the four elements, taught how incorruptibility could be achieved by bal-

separate, purify, and recombine the elements into the proper proportion
demonstrated by speculative alchemy. If completed, the corpus equale

of equality, not all medicines made in an attempt to reach a corpus equale

Bacon considered accidental decay to be fundamentally the same as the
natural decay that allowed the body to die at the time appointed by God,
and not earlier. However, Bacon believed that because it was accidental,
not natural, this premature decay could be stopped using the methods
taught in alchemy and experimental science. And while Bacon thought
that medicine was lacking in many aspects, he considered its fundamen-
tals—that is, the composition of the human body from the four humors—
to be sound, and thus certain aspects of medicine needed to be incorporated
into any theories of prolonging life.
This chapter explores the various remedies that Bacon thought could
be used to address the accidental decay that had become pervasive in the
thirteenth century, beginning with the non-alchemical remedies that
Bacon supported, such as the regimen of health and those medicines made
by doctors that are efficacious. I will then move to the alchemical remedies
about which Bacon wrote. These can divided into three categories, which
I have termed the following: the “standard remedies,” which Bacon
derived from contemporary medical literature and worked by balancing
humors; the “prepared alchemical” remedies, which were natural materials
that could be manipulated using alchemical techniques to achieve a corpus
equale; and finally, the “true alchemical” remedies, which had a corpus
equale innate to themselves. While most of these medicines were taken

2 orThodox MediciNeS
Bacon’s medicines can be broadly broken into three categories: standard
medicines, such as those commonly prescribed by physicians and made by
the apothecaries; alchemically prepared medicines, which were those stan-
dard medicines whose activities had been altered or improved by prepar-
ing them with alchemical means; and finally, the true alchemical remedies,
which had an innate corpus equale. Though Bacon did not provide any sort
of nomenclature for differentiating between his remedies, I have devised
the terms “standard,” “prepared alchemical,” and “true alchemical” to
distinguish between these three categories and aid comparison. I will pro-
vide a brief overview of each type of medicine, explaining what purposes
Bacon thought each served, and how they helped to restore health.
The first type of medicines to address were the standard medicines.
These were medicines that were known to all physicians, and could—in
theory—be prepared by any apothecary. Recipes for these were found in
medical books, and usually incorporated easily sourced materials, although
wealthy patients often opted for the addition of finer ingredients, such as
ground pearls or gold. Standard medicines can further be broken down
into three categories: simples, compounds, and foods.
The importance of diet in medieval therapeutics can hardly be over-
stated. The link went back to at least Hippocrates’ De regimine acutorum,
and in thirteenth-century London, apothecaries were considered part of
the company of grocers.20 Despite its importance for his contemporaries,
Bacon did not devote much time to discussing food in the context of
medicine. In the Liber sex scientiarum, Bacon stated that food could be
made to have a body of equal complexion, which of course made it better,
but that was largely the extent of his discussion.21 Whether it was because
food was already included in the six non-naturals, or because he placed
more emphasis on alchemically prepared compound medicines, Bacon
added very little to the discussions of diet in thirteenth-century
therapeutics.

Compound Medicines
Unlike simple medicines, which only used one ingredient, compound
medicines combined two or more ingredients. But if simple medicines
existed (and were effective), then why did doctors prescribe compound
medicines? There were many reasons for doctors to not only prescribe
compounds, but to favor them over simples.29 Bacon provided three: com-
pound medicines were good because two roots could have two operations
in each species of disease30; one root could not necessarily provide all that
was needed, so it must be supplemented31; and compound medicines
could cure different parts of a disease, or even multiple diseases.32 The
twelfth-century pharmacist Matthaeus Platearius (d. 1161) offered similar
reasons: some illnesses themselves were compounded, and thus could not
be treated by one medicine, as one medicine could not have different
effects on different imbalances. Additionally, he said, compound medi-
cines could be used against multiple diseases, and they could incorporate
ingredients that preserved the medicine, made it more palatable, and
repressed any harmful properties that an ingredient may have.33
This idea was not unique or original to Platearius: many Latin and
Arabic medieval books of medicines contain the same ideas, all of which
went back to Galenic pharmacology.34 Galen himself had divided com-
pounds into three types: application-based, which were classified by the
part of the body they targeted (eyewashes, toothpastes, enemas, etc.);
function-based, which were classified by their general effects on the body
as a whole (purgatives, emetics, laxatives); and method-based, which were
classified by the ways in which they were made. Common examples
included confections (medicines mixed with sugar to create a palatable
“candy” that would be eaten), decoctions (medicines cooked in liquid and
then imbibed), ointments (medicines mixed with oil or wax, which would
then be applied externally as a paste), and pills (medicines mixed with resin
and molded into a small tablet that could be swallowed whole). Paula De

Man-Made Gold
The next prepared alchemical remedy Bacon advocated was gold. Gold, of
course, existed in nature, but came in different grades of equality. Pure
gold of 24 “grades” was incorruptible, while gold of lesser grades could be
corrupted, but also perfected. Both artificial and natural gold that had
been prepared using the alchemical techniques of calcination and dissolu-
tion could be added to food and drink and then converted into human
nature, just like other food and drink.77 But if an individual did not have
natural gold, alchemy and experimental science taught how to make it.
One of the longest descriptions of this process is in the Opus minus. Bacon
summarized the steps in the following order: pulverization with congela-
tion, then resolution with ascension and condensation, followed by
inceration,78 and finally the mixing of the parts.
After this, the material was ground and sublimed, then the body sepa-
rated from the spirit with a great heat. If done correctly, the alchemist
would achieve the perfect medicine, which the philosophers called the
elixir. Combined with the “hidden spirit” in the animal, and arsenic, it
would penetrate all things.79 Bacon believed that this elixir could not only
perfect imperfect metals when projected upon them,80 but could also
restore humans to health and prolong life: “And this is because it will
restore weak bodies to health, and will conserve them against all occa-
sions, and life, if God will wish it, will prolong beyond hundreds of
years.”81
Bacon occasionally referred to this elixir as the “medicine” or the “laxa-
tive medicine,” mimicking the medical use of these terms, which purged
excess humors.82 This is unsurprising, given that Bacon thought of the
elixir as a corpus equale that could heal both metals and humans. Such
language can also be found pseudo-Avicenna’s De anima in arte alchemiae.

The Ethiopian Dragon
The final prepared alchemical remedy was the flesh of an animal which
Bacon referred to as the Ethiopian Dragon. As the discussion of theriac
has shown, snakes had long been used as ingredients in medicine, and
Bacon was most likely drawing on this in his discussion of the dragon.
When Bacon wrote about dragons, he was not referring to dragons in the
modern sense of the word, but rather to a large, winged snake. The Latin
draco could be used to mean either dragon or snake, and as many of
Bacon’s translators have chosen to refer to this particular species of snake
as a “dragon,” so I will continue to use this nomenclature, both to main-
tain continuity with earlier scholarship and to distinguish between this and
other types of snakes that Bacon wrote about. In fact, there are three types
of snakes that Bacon recommended using in medicine in De erroribus med-
icorum: the Ethiopian dragon (draco), the snake (coluber), and the viper
(tyrus). All three were good against the diseases of old age, but the viper

There was one medicine which was conspicuously absent from all of
Bacon’s works on the prolongatio vitae: alcohol. This is made more signifi-

Starting in the high Middle Ages, authors began to write about a sub-
stance called aqua ardens. Aqua ardens (literally “burning water,” some-
times called aqua vitae), was not, as its name suggested, a water, but
actually a distilled and highly flammable liquor. The origins of distillation
are murky. Some scholars have argued that distillation first appeared in
Southern Italy, possibly at the school of Salerno, in the twelfth century.
Treatises from Salerno, dating to 1100–1150 CE discussed how to make
so-called beneficial waters using distillation, but it is not until the writings
of Magister Salernus (d. 1167) that such waters were associated with alco-
hol. While beer and wine had been in production for millennia, these
scholars argued that distilled alcohol appeared much later because of the

principles of a corpus equale if it were considered to be a replacement for
the radical humidity. In this instance, it would presumably act the way that
the radical humidity allotted to each man does: that is, it would be con-
tinually consumed by the innate heat. Each time it was consumed, it would
have to be replenished. It would not be a true incorruptibility, but merely
a delaying of the corruption process. Bacon was not interested in “replen-
ishment” in any sense: the path toward incorruptibility is a path
toward stasis.
But if we widen the scope of this question to include medieval physi-
cians as well, an interesting trend emerges. I have not been able to find
evidence that any medieval author thought of the aqua vitae as a substi-
tute for the radical humidity, even those who recommended using it as a
medicine. Even when working against “cold afflictions,” as Alderotti
describes above, it appears that it works more by the power of opposites (it
being hot in nature), rather than any inherent likeness it has to the fuel of
the internal lamp. This does not take away from the argument just made
about Bacon: Bacon would not have been interested in aqua vitae because
he does not consider it a corpus equale. But it is interesting to note that
there does appear to be some agreement between Bacon and the physi-
cians on the use of aqua vitae in the prolongatio vitae, even if they disagree
as to the reasons.

Bacon was a great synthesizer of different sources, often from very differ-
ent genres. He built on the ideas of medicines found in contemporary and
ancient works, and combined them with the corpus equale, alchemy, and
experimental science to explain how they could use their equality of the
elements to prolong life. Like most of his medicines, Bacon did not
describe his personal use with the prepared and true alchemical remedies.
However, they remained central to his idea of the prolongatio vitae. Why
did he believe that these items existed, and that they would even work, if
he never tried them himself? There are two reasons why he was justified in
believing in prepared alchemical remedies. The first is that he had it on
authority that they existed. Bacon had heard stories of men who pro-
longed their lives using Ethiopian dragons, and Aristotle himself, one of
the greatest authorities, describes how to prepare the gloria inestimabi-
lis.110 Bacon also thought that he has seen it for himself. Recall that experi-
ment in Bacon’s case should include not just individual experience, but
others’ experiences as well. It is thus within Bacon’s “experimental
method” to consider the stories he heard, especially those that come from
reliable authorities, as evidence for the existence of prepared alchemical
remedies. The fact that he thought he saw the gloria inestimabilis cure a
man only solidified his belief in their abilities.
The second, and perhaps more important reason why Bacon believed in
prepared alchemical remedies—and also the true alchemical remedies—
was that they were acting within the laws of nature. Most authorities,
including Aristotle and Averroes, taught that an equality of elements
excluded all actions, passions, and corruptions. This was a law of nature
that concerned the fundamental building blocks of the universe, with no
room for variation. Elements (and their qualities) in perfect proportion
were incapable of acting on each other, and must experience a state of
stasis. Even something as powerful as the rational soul could not change
this—as in the case of the resurrection body. This meant that so long as
Bacon believed that these medicines had a body of equal complexion, or
at least something approaching equality, they could only act in this manner.

Was Bacon alone in these beliefs? There were certainly practicing physi-

was perhaps the physician Bernard of Gordon, who wrote about a miracu-
lous elixir in his De marasmode. This treatise, which dealt with marasmus,
included a recipe for an elixir that Bernard claims to have invented. It used
dew, distilled human blood, rosewater, and gold to prevent the body from
desiccating, thus prolonging life. It would only work “as far as naturally
possible,” however, and had to be accompanied by the proper regimen of
health.111 This is not mere coincidence: Bernard explicitly connected the
alchemical process to creating the elixir. In his study on Bernard of
Gordon, Demaitre wrote: “Bernard’s own application of alchemy to med-
icine is evident in his citation of “alkemiste” as the source of his informa-

to Bacon, but without a version of these manuscripts, it is impossible to
tell. The point remains, however, that alchemy provided tools that could
be useful to medicine, but the practitioner had to remain aware of this.113
Is this proof that Bacon had a supporter in the established medical
field? All of the main ingredients certainly appear, at one point or another,
in Bacon’s writings on the prolongatio vitae. Bernard provided this recipe
for the wonderful elixir because although theriac worked in antiquity, he
believed it was no longer effective in the thirteenth century because of its
fast action. Theriac also had the ability to poison the patient if they ingested
too much, while Bernard’s elixir was not dangerous at all.114 Thus, Bacon’s
concerns over both the ineffectiveness of medicines on the modern body
and the potential for harm are echoed in the works of trained physicians.

CHAPTER 6
Reshaping Medical Knowledge in Bacon’s
Milieu
This chapter explicitly connects Bacon’s beliefs on the prolongatio vitae to
the larger field of scholastic medicine. To what extent did medical practice
and theory help shape Bacon’s views on the possibility of prolonging life
and recovering health? And is there any evidence that Bacon’s concerns
were taken seriously by contemporary physicians? While certain innova-
tions in thirteenth-century medicine, such as the incorporation of Arabic
pharmaceuticals, influenced his work, Bacon was not always on the “cut-
ting edge” of medical learning. His use of sources shows that he was not
interested in the “New Galen” translations, and much of the material that
he cited had been available for decades (with the exception of Averroes’
Colliget). Nor is the medical literature his source for the division of all sci-
ences into practical and speculative.
Additionally, I demonstrate that while there was some interest in using
alchemy to prolong life in academic medical circles, Bacon himself was not
particularly influential. This lack of influence comes not from any antago-
nism toward Bacon himself, but rather from his status as a Franciscan and
natural philosopher.

1 Defining Doctors: Medicus or Physicus?
Scholars have argued that the medicine of the early Middle Ages was
largely engaged with practice, rather than theory. However, as new trans-
lations became available in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, medical
teaching and theory became prioritized. The doctor should have a solid
theoretical training, as well as be knowledgeable about subjects related to,
but not directly under the umbrella of medicine, leading to the popular-
ization of works on natural philosophy, such as those by Aristotle.1
This stress on academic medicine, especially during its culmination in
the thirteenth century, is considered a turning point in the history of
medieval medicine. Though medicine had previously been associated with
schools, such as that of Salerno, it was in the thirteenth century that it was
fully integrated into the university system. The sheer number of new trans-
lations meant that medicine had to receive some level of autonomy—it
could not just be subservient to a course in natural philosophy, for exam-
ple. And as professionalization of physicians increased, so too did the idea
of a “medical profession” among the laypeople, and with it, an increased
confidence in the abilities of academic medicine.2
One of the most concrete examples of the influence that natural phi-
losophy had over the medical practice was in the names that the doctors
used to refer to themselves: the physicus vs. the medicus. As early as the
ninth century, the word physica began to displace the earlier term medic-
ina to denote medical learning. However, physica also retained its connec-
tion to natural philosophy, and could be used in either sense. By the
twelfth century, physica could refer not only to natural philosophy or med-
icine, but to the entire body of rational medicine itself, including its learn-
ing and its practice. It was also in this century that physicus largely replaced
medicus as the term to denote a medical expert.3 The adoption of the term
physicus, rather than medicus, was emblematic of the rise in position medi-
cine was attaining: physicus held a level of prestige that medicus did
not have.

2 MeDicine: scientia or ars?
With the incorporation of medicine into physica came new questions con-
cerning the fundamental nature of medicine: namely, was it a scientia or an
ars? Medicus tended to be associated with ars, while physicus was associated
with scientia. The physicians themselves often could not agree on an
answer: Montpellier physicians Bernard de Gordon and Arnald of Villanova
both thought that medicine’s objective of restoring health made it an ars,
not a scientia, while the schools in Italy saw both theory and practice as
scientia.13 The Boethian (and Aristotelian) division of philosophy into
practical and theoretical classified scientia as theory, rather than practice:
scientia learned through doctrina, while practica learned via experience.
Practica itself was different than ars/operatio, for ars did not require the
experience that practica did.

3 speculative versus practical sciences
The final question concerning the nature of medicine was to what extent
it should rely on practice versus theory. The increasing influence of scho-
lasticism on the medical field meant that theory was incorporated into
practice, and many medical texts of the thirteenth century were also
divided into practical and speculative sections. Johannitius’ Isagoge, for
instance, began with the claim that medicine had two parts: theory and
practice.26 Theory was further subdivided into three additional sections:
the study of the naturals, non-naturals, and contra-naturals. All were nec-
essary to understand sickness and health.27 Likewise, the Methodus medendi
explained Galen’s division between theory and practice, and included
examples of each.28 Galen, borrowing from Aristotle, saw sickness as
belonging to a specific entity, and should be understood as such. Theory
was universal, practice was individual. For Galen, the universal was far
more important: he cared more about the disease than the individual
patient’s experience.29
In terms of diagnostics, this meant that when doubt arose, the physi-
cian should use reason, not individual evidence. If the diagnosis came
down to either what was visible to the senses or what was clear to reason,
then reason should prevail.30 Thus for Galen, theoretical medicine, and its
allowance for the “clinical schematism” popular in the late Hellenistic
period, was superior to practica. He was also afraid that if he placed too
much weight on individual patients when designing his nosographic
scheme, medicine would be reduced to a mere accumulation of cases, and
the doctor would be stripped of pathology or therapy not tied to the indi-
vidual, something dangerously akin to the empiricist school of medicine.31

Easy to divide on paper, the differences between theory and practica were
harder to determine in real life. By the late Middle Ages, a new genre of
literature had appeared: the clinical case study. Although it had been in
existence since antiquity—Galen recalled some of his cases in De praecog-
nitione and Rhazes’ Pantegni, Book 1, Chapter 24 contained many stories
of individual patients42—it wasn’t until the first half of the fifteenth cen-
tury that a trend of describing individual case histories and pathologies
became popular. Medieval authors had done this, but not to the same
extent. Bacon, for example, used very few case histories in his medical
writings. There are only two instances when he referred directly to an
individual case that he had seen treated: one is in the De erroribus medico-
rum, and is self-referential: he described how he had used purified rhubarb
on himself and was impressed by its marvelous effects.

Secretum secretorum, and involved the use of the gloria inestimabilis in
treating the unnamed French royal, possibly Alphonse of Poitiers.43 Matus

he knew that his remedies were fantastical and that there was no way they
could accomplish what they claimed. He takes this as evidence for a lack of
medical practice.44 But this is neither true nor fair to Bacon. This is merely
one of the ways in which Bacon was typical of his contemporaries, who
would not usually include case histories in medical writings.

5 authority in the Marketplace
Despite the foundations of Oxford and Cambridge universities in the elev-
enth and thirteenth centuries, respectively, London was the place to which
most medical practitioners flocked. Anyone in search of treatment or
advice went to London, though the practitioners there tended to special-
ize not in giving advice on a healthy regimen or lifestyle, but in providing
quick fixes to whatever was ailing the patient. Perhaps unsurprisingly,
then, medical “learning” in London was not unique to the academically
trained. From at least the twelfth century, people who were not academi-
cally trained physicians were giving both medical judgments and legal
judgments regarding medical matters. At the same time, however, medical
experts were employed by civil authorities, and there was an attempt to
regulate the retail of drugs—a practitioner could be asked to examine
drugs that had been made available for sale to the public. Overall, though,
there was no consensus about medical and pharmaceutical standards, and
no successful control over who could or could not practice medicine.102
That laypeople were increasingly interested in medicine is demonstrated
by the Gilbertus translation. This was a translation of Gilbertus Anglicus’
(d. 1250) Compendium medicinae from its original Latin into Middle
English, thus making it available to anyone who could only read the ver-
nacular. The translation provided a list of treatments, broken down into
diet, regimen, surgery, and pharmacy, for easily identifiable conditions.
The information contained would have been accessible to anyone: foods
were classified on their appearance, feel, and taste. Medicines were classi-
fied as general or specific, and each medicine listed included a description
of the humors that it would purge. Occasionally, a dosage or method was
provided, but not always.103 The Compendium medicinae shared some
traits in common with the De erroribus medicorum, such as a reliance on
humoral theory and a description of the humors targeted, as well as a gen-
eral system of classifying medicines.
But this does not necessarily mean that Bacon was influenced by the
Compendium medicinae as a medical text. Nothing about the Compendium
medicinae itself is necessarily groundbreaking: its theories and content
would have been standard in any medical work in England or Western
Europe in the first half of the thirteenth century. It is the Compendium’s

Bacon on Diagnosis and Prognosis
Despite their new status and attempts at gatekeeping their profession
through university regulations, the physicians’ position in society was still
precarious. One of the best ways for physicians to secure patient confi-
dence, and thus maintain their social hierarchy, was through accurate diag-
nosis and prognosis of disease—two of the three critical factors that Galen
defined when separating the role of the doctor from that of the
philosopher.
We should expect Bacon to say something about diagnosis or prognosis
in any of his works on the prolongatio vitae, especially in either the
Antidotarium or De erroribus medicorum, but these works are disappoint-
ing in that regard. Only one section of De erroribus medicorum discussed
diagnosis, and only to the extent that Bacon complained that Latin doc-
tors were bad at it. Bacon believed the doctors lacked diagnostic skill
because they did not have enough diagnoses for all of the diseases. The
Latin doctors borrowed from the writings of Avicenna and Rhazes, and
Avicenna was a philosopher, not a physician. In fact, the translator of
Avicenna’s Canon despised it, and it was rarely used by Arabic
physicians.115

Previous chapters have demonstrated how Bacon had serious concerns
about the practice of medicine. It is important to stress again here that
these problems did not arise from any fundamental issues regarding
humoral theory, the basis of thirteenth-century medicine, but largely deal
with how he felt medicine was being taught. Incorrect translations, the
practice of endless disputations, lack of experiment, and above all, a com-
plete ignorance of the basics of natural philosophy, as taught by sciences
such as alchemy and astronomy, were his chief complaints. If one is
tempted to say that Bacon did have a problem with certain medical theo-
ries—namely the radical humidity—I must point out that nowhere in his
corpus did Bacon actually say that these ideas were incorrect. He merely
chose not to discuss them, perhaps because they could not be as easily
explained by speculative alchemy as humoral theory. By this, I mean that
there is a clear progression from the qualities to the elements to the
humors, and then finally to the compound humors. Given that there were
different ideas about what exactly innate heat and radical humidity were,
it was not as easy to describe how they could be made from the elements.
But absence of discussion alone does not prove that Bacon disagreed—
merely that he did not see radical humidity as important as humoral theory.
These fears were what prompted Bacon to write De erroribus medico-
rum, Antidotarius, and potentially the seventh chapter of the Epistola
(and to an extent, the glosses on the Secretum secretorum). They also
appeared in various forms in the Opera and Liber sex scientiarum, but were
not the primary reasons why he chose to write them. These works chose
to emphasize the importance of alchemy in medicine, rather than high-
lighting the ways in which contemporary medicine failed. These works
demonstrated the numerous ways in which physicians failed their patients,
often by providing specific examples of common types of ignorance among
Latin doctors. Bacon’s goal, at least in De erroribus medicorum, was not to
instruct but to arouse curiosity, but he did provide those interested with
the tools to further explore his ideas, revealing where more knowledge can
be found. They also served as a warning to those who would potentially
be misled—both the new doctors themselves, who did not know what
they were ignorant of, and the patients whose lives were in potential danger.
Despite the obvious interest displayed by some late thirteenth-century
physicians, Bacon seemed convinced that the medical circles were not
heeding his advice, especially in regard to incorporating other sciences

Is there any truth to Bacon’s complaints? Authority was one reason why
physicians might not be inclined to accept Bacon’s claims about prolong-
ing life through alchemy. Because Galen emphasized wasting as the path
to death, many strict adherents to his teachings were skeptical that life
could be prolonged artificially.124 Simon of Faversham (d. 1306), for
instance, argued that the wick could not be restored by any type of extrin-
sic nourishment.125 Some Arabic writers also provided authority to claims
that alchemy could not prolong life, but the main ones seemed to be more
against the ability of any treatment to restore the wick or the radical
humor. There were also members of the apologist school, who believed
that prolonging life was “neither possible nor desirable,” as it was not
found in the New Testament (and would presumably not be necessary,
given the resurrection).126

and Avicenna, for instance, both rejected the possibility of chrysopoeia, or
transmutational alchemy. Avicenna thought that humans were incapable
of truly changing species, for human art was weaker than nature. Because
man only knew the superficial differences in things, rather than their true
differences (found in their essences), he could not change the true differ-
ences. But just because someone did not believe in alchemy did not mean

Bacon was not the only philosopher to be ignored: most of the contem-
porary philosophers, including Grosseteste and Thomas Aquinas (d.
1274), were left out of medical texts. Even the naturalist Albertus Magnus
was only mentioned a few times. Nor was this a case of physicians refusing
to cite their sources: the number of sources explicitly named in medical
works greatly increased after 1300.129 Thus, two conclusions appear from
this study. The first is that Bacon was not influential on medical thought,
not because of any “outlandish” positions that he held—many of his posi-
tions were fairly standard—but because scholastic philosophy as a whole
had a difficult time integrating into the medical field. His professional
status, rather than ideas, was likely what prevented him from being seen as
an authority. It is also evidence that those in the century following his
death did not see him as a practicing physician, despite claims some
historians have made to this effect.

The final chapter explores how Bacon’s understanding of the Bible and his
involvement with the Franciscan order shaped his understanding of the
resurrection body, as well as natural philosophy more generally. Bacon
joined the Franciscan Order around 1257, after he had spent years teach-

reasons for joining the Order are not certain, it is possible that he was
interested in their approach to God. Franciscans desired deification, or the
ecstatic union with God, which could be achieved through a ladder of
contemplation, beginning with the natural world—God’s creation.1
Whether Bacon held similar ideas before he joined the Order, or if he was
influenced by their teachings after joining, the fact remains: Bacon’s works
show an interest in corporeal deification and unity with God. This unity
would continue into the age after the resurrection, and would influence
how Bacon conceived of mankind’s eternal body.

This chapter will demonstrate that many of Bacon’s ideas regarding the
possibility, as well as the physicality of this post-resurrection body came
from two sources: the Bible and the Franciscan pseudo-Dionysian tradi-
tion, which above all emphasized the study of light. I will not argue that
there was any overarching “Franciscan approach” to alchemy, only that
individuals each had their own way of incorporating their understanding
of Franciscan ideals into alchemical practice. The continued interest in
alchemy, especially alchemical medicine, among thirteenth- and fourteenth-
century Franciscans is well documented, but an explicit connection
between the Order and alchemy is impossible to draw without a rigorous
study of many Franciscans. It was Bacon’s conception of the resurrection
body that was shaped by Franciscan teaching, not his approach to
alchemy per se.
It is from the Bible that Bacon took two of his main ideas regarding the
potentiality of an elixir to prolong life: that man in this life originally had
the potentiality of living forever, as Adam would if he had not eaten from
the fruit of the Tree of Knowledge; and that despite the proliferation of sin
and the resulting shortening of life, our bodies will eventually be made
incorruptible by the power of God.2 By extension, the human body could
be improved, because it had the aptitude to become immortal.
While Bacon repeatedly stated that a human body approaching equal
complexion through the corpus equale will be similar, but not identical to,
the resurrection body, there has been little consistent study of how the
idea of human incorruptibility after the resurrection gave Bacon ideas
about the potential for reduced corruptibility in this life.3 The short answer
is that while Bacon’s improved body would be less corruptible and will
have a better balance of the elements, it would not be perfectly balanced,
and would eventually decay and die. The truly incorruptible, and by exten-
sion, immortal body could only be found after the resurrection.4 Mankind
would experience death, but at each individual’s appointed time, and not
earlier from accidents, for it was the purview of God alone to create a truly
incorruptible body. And while this short answer is acceptable, it raises fur-
ther questions that must be addressed in order to understand exactly how
Bacon envisioned the healed and the resurrected bodies.

Knowledge and certainty of the resurrection, as well as who is privy to
such information, was a theme that frequently occupied Bacon. In the
seventh and final section of the Opus maius, Bacon argued that in-depth
knowledge of the resurrection in this life was hindered by four things: sin,
the care of the body, the trammels of the sensible world, and a lack of
revelation. By addressing these issues—that is, purifying the soul from sin,
creating separation between soul and body, thinking on higher things, and
accepting revelation—it was possible to learn a little more about man-
kind’s future happiness.5 Knowledge about the resurrection, much like
knowledge about alchemy and the regimen sanitatis, had been obscured
partially through the fault of man.
Indeed, the statement “we can know a little bit about our future felic-
ity” shows that while some knowledge of resurrection would always be
hidden from mankind in this lifetime, the fact that it could be remedied
made it akin to an accident, rather than the direct will of God.6 A parallel
can be drawn between this knowledge and Bacon’s understanding of the
deterioration of the human body—some deterioration would always hap-
pen, as was God’s will (as evidenced by the fact that no man may be
immortal in this life), but much of it was due to man’s own failings. Almost
all men died by potentially remediable accidents before the time appointed
to them by God.7 Though Bacon believed this knowledge to be universal,
Christians had the gift of faith, and thus were privy to more knowledge
than others.8 Of course, that the resurrection would even happen was a
given, and would not have been controversial to Bacon’s audience at all.
To take the resurrection for granted is the only way to understand Bacon’s
continued insistence of the correlation between the resurrection body and
the body in this life helped by his alchemical medicines.

Spiritual and Physical: Soul and Body
It is evident that Bacon attributed immortality to an equality of the ele-
ments, but what about the type of matter that would compose the body?
Bacon argued that there were three types of matter: spiritual, sensible, and
mediate. Spiritual matter was that which formed the angels and the ratio-
nal souls of men. It was not subject to quantity, movement, or contrariety.
Sensible matter was that which was found in the sublunary world, and was
subject to quantity, movement, and contrariety. The third type of matter,
mediate matter, was that which composed the heavenly bodies. This mat-
ter was subject to movement and quantity, but not to contrariety.15
Bacon’s understanding of the different types of matter had strong par-
allels to the description of the resurrection in 1 Corinthians. In his letter
to the Corinthian church, Paul emphasized the different nature of the
resurrected man “The first man was from the earth, a man of dust; the
second man is from heaven. As was the man of dust, so are those who are
of dust; and as is the man of heaven, so are those who are of heaven.”16
Humans would not have earthly bodies made of dust because they would
not be on this earth, but a new heavenly one made of heavenly material.

soul was required to maintain identity; and (2) the body received in the
next life would be the same as the body had in this life, for the essence of

3 lighT and Vision
The idea of sight and light as parallel to the resurrection process appeared
throughout Bacon’s oeuvre. In his Perspectiva, Bacon likened the ability
to be perfect to a type of threefold sight. Salvation required spiritual vision,
which in turn required that man combine his own power of sight (an alle-
gory for free will) with an external gift of grace. This perfect vision could
only happen when the soul and body were reunited, because the soul and
body had a natural appetite for each other that must be satisfied, and noth-
ing perfect could happen until that appetite was filled.40
In Part 4 of the Opus maius, he compared the reception of God’s grace
after the resurrection to the multiplication of rays of light from different
angles. In this lifetime, good people refracted grace (light), because while
they tried to receive the grace, their imperfectness prevented them from
doing so. Bad people, on the other hand, reflected grace (light), because
they actively rejected it. After the resurrection, those who have been saved
will receive grace directly and perpendicularly, with no deviation in its
path, which was the strongest way a species could propagate through a
medium.41 The fifth part of the Opus maius added an optical discussion to
the comparison between light and grace. Here, Bacon wrote:
Man has a threefold vision: one perfect, which will come after the resurrec-
tion; the second in the soul separated from the body in heaven until the
resurrection, which is weaker; and the third in this life, and is the weakest
and happens by reflection. The soul will not possess perfect vision and other
blessings in full measure before the resurrection.42
The reason that the body and soul could not have perfect vision before
the resurrection was because “there is a certain natural desire in the soul
for its own body, which cannot be realized except for the resurrection.”43
Once again, Bacon stressed that the unity of soul and body, specifically, the
body that the soul had in the present life, was necessary to receive any
benefits from the resurrection. Additionally, knowledge itself came literally

Just as important was the Franciscan tradition surrounding the study of
light. Franciscan natural philosophy was largely influenced by pseudo-
Dionysius (ca. sixth century CE). Dionysius, and by extension, the
Franciscans, believed that light, its rays, illuminations, and emanations
were not merely evidence of God, but were actually God in action. Light
was equivalent to God and The Good, for light is what emanated from
God, and was the cause of all things. It was no less than the form of cor-
poreity, which first gave form to formless matter. When St. Francis received
the stigmata in 1224, it was the light from the seraph that imprinted the
wounds onto his body. St. Francis achieved the pinnacle of ecstatic union
with God—deification—through the action of light. For this reason, light
was held sacred to the Franciscans, who were above all interested in physi-
cal oneness with the Divine.46
Thus, not only should Bacon not be viewed as unique in his use of light
metaphors in describing the soul’s reception of grace, but it is apparent
that the Franciscan practice of natural philosophy was incredibly influen-
tial on Bacon’s understanding of the resurrection. Indeed, even Bacon’s
Opus maius can be described as being Franciscan in character. Franciscans
created texts that would allow the contemplator to understand the sym-
bolism of things in the world, which would then explain their allegorical
meaning in the Bible. The familiarity with natural things gleaned from
such texts could help the contemplator ascend the ladder to deification.47
According to Augustine, if Adam and Eve had not sinned, and thus
remained in Eden, then they would have eventually achieved immortality
by eating from the Tree of Life, and thus man has the possibility of not
dying. Unlike Augustine, Bacon did not believe that eating from the Tree
of Life would eventually give Adam and Eve total incorruptibility and
immortality. Rather, it would continuously sustain them forever, but the
innate nature of incorruptibility would never be fully passed on to them.
But in Bacon’s scheme, there would be no need for such a transference.
Without sin, there would be no reason for man to leave Eden, and thus
the Tree of Life, and so whether man was innately immortal or was sus-
tained in immortality through his consumption of the fruit did not par-
ticularly matter.
