This appendix addresses the authenticity of a work commonly attributed
to Bacon, the Epistola de secretis operibus artis et naturae et de nullitate
magiae (henceforth Epistola). using a three-tiered approach of place-
ment, content, and themes, I argue that the Epistola is possibly a genuine
work by Roger Bacon. Without a reference to the Epistola in Bacon’s
known works in which he claims authorship, or an extant copy in Bacon’s
hand, it is impossible to claim with absolute certainty that it was written
by Bacon. However, the evidence weighs more heavily in favor of it being
a genuine work, or at the very least, written by someone soon after his
death who was very familiar with large sections of his oeuvre.1
The Epistola, occasionally called De mirabili potestate artis et naturae,
is a text consisting of 11 chapters, each of which deals with the marvelous
things that man can create using art to surpass nature. Brewer lists chap-
ters and contents as follows2:

1 iMportAnce of deterMining Authenticity
Given the unusual nature of the last three chapters, scholars have debated
the authenticity of the Epistola for the past century. In 1861, Emile charles
argued that the attribution of this work was suspect (especially the last
three chapters), attached to Bacon’s name after his death, while John
Bridges’ 1914 work, “The Life and Work of Roger Bacon,” claimed that
all 11 chapters of the Epistola were genuinely Baconian.3 Today, there are
three broad schools of thought: (1) the entirety of the Epistola is genuine;
(2) the entire work is spurious, but was written by someone very familiar
with Bacon’s genuine works and philosophies4; and (3) chapters 1 through
8 are genuine, but chapters 9 through 11 were a latter addition, written by
someone else who was familiar with Bacon’s work, but the chapters are
not representative of his own thinking. This has been the view adopted by
many scholars of medieval science and alchemy, most notably dorothea
Waley Singer and Lynn Thorndike.5 In recent years, other scholars have
reevaluated this argument: these chapters, especially chapter 9, contain
references to works that Bacon is known to have written, such as the gloss
on the Secretum secretorum and the part of the Opus Minus that deals with
alchemy.6
It is of particular importance that the authenticity of the Epistola be
established. Sébastien Moureau has suggested that there is a link between
this philosophers’ egg—which appears in both Bacon’s edition of the
Secretum secretorum and the Epistola—and the gloria inestimabilis, the
miraculous medicine that has the ability to prolong human life.7 This is
especially important, given the debates about what the philosophers’ egg
actually is. In the Secretum secretorum, pseudo-Aristotle provides a recipe

2 deterMining the Epistola’s Authenticity
As none of the extant copies of the Epistola are in Bacon’s hand, an analy-
sis of even the earliest copy’s handwriting will not be useful in establishing
the authenticity of the text. Instead, authenticity is based on an examina-
tion of three aspects of the manuscripts: common themes, content, and
placement. common themes concern those ideas that are found in both
the Epistola manuscripts and Bacon’s other alchemical and medical writ-
ings, such as his De erroribus medicorum and the gloss on pseudo-
Aristotle’s Secretum Secretorum. Such themes include the ability of certain
materials to prolong life and how life has come to be shortened. The
examination of content consists of a line-by-line close reading of each of
the extant Epistola manuscripts for additions, deletions, and variations
among texts. finally, the study of placement involves an inspection of the
larger codex in which the manuscripts are contained.
for many years, scholars assumed that three works were written by
Bacon solely on their placement in manuscripts. These three texts, De
balneis senum et seniorum, De universali regimine senum et seniorum, and
De compositione quarundam medicinarum, were all found in manuscripts
after a supposed Baconian work, De retardatione accidentium senectutis,
and thus were thought to be appendices of it. once Agostino Paravicini
Bagliani suggested that De retardatione was not genuinely Baconian, the
authorship of the other three works immediately came into question.16
Thus there is good precedent for examining the placement and location of
texts within a manuscript to determine authenticity.
