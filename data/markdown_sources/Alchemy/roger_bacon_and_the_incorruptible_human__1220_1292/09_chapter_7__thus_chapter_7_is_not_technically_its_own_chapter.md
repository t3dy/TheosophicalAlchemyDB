Two points appear from this examination of chapter divisions. The first
is that chapters 1 through 3 are often viewed as a single chapter, and the
second is that chapter 7 is occasionally incorporated into the second half
of chapter 6. of the two points, the second is far more significant. It could
be important that chapters 1 through 3 are often combined, but as the
earliest versions do not have any chapter divisions and only include 1
through 6, there is no argument to be made for or against authenticity on
the basis of this alone. The argument that just chapters 1 through 6 are
genuine is highly unlikely, given the similarities between chapters 6, 7, and
8 and the genuine oeuvre of Bacon.
Regarding the second point, if we return to the beginning of this chap-
ter, we see that chapter 6 discusses marvelous experiments (perpetual
lamps, weapons of war, astronomical instruments) and occult natural phe-
nomena. At first, this does not seem to be related to the prolongatio vitae,
the subject of chapter 7. But there are two places in the genuine Bacon’s
works where marvelous experiments appear alongside the prolongatio
vitae: the section of the Opus maius that deals with experimental science,
and the Liber sex scientiarum. The Liber sex scientiarum discusses how
experimental science, optics, perspecitva, and alchemy can make instru-
ments that can be used to prolong human life with the aid of celestial rays.
The comparison between the Opus maius and the Epistola is more con-
crete. The chapter on the second prerogative of experimental science in
the Opus maius includes three examples: (1) Mathematical Science, (2)

The Prolongation of Life, and (3) Alchemy. The contents of these exam-

Epistola. Even much of the wording is the same. The main difference is
that the contents of Epistola chapter 6 correspond to Opus maius examples
1 and 3 (mathematics and alchemy), while chapter 7 is the same as exam-
ple 2, meaning the sections are slightly out of order in the Epistola.
However, much of the text is verbatim, which is not unusual for Bacon—
as many of his works were re-written at various points throughout his life,
it is not unusual to find sections copied word for word from one text into
another.17
However, there are some differences between the text of the Epistola
and example 3. Most notably, the Epistola does not explicitly state that
there is a connection between the ability to heal metals and the human
body. However, this may not be evidence that the Epistola is not a genuine
work by Bacon. Bacon’s ideas surrounding the relative importance of
alchemy in the prolongatio vitae are not always consistent. In the Opus
maius, Bacon does not consider alchemy to be an especially important sci-
ence for the prolongatio vitae. In the Liber sex scientiarum, however,
alchemy is much more important. overall, the comparison of exempla 1
and 3 and the Epistola provides evidence in support of the theory that this
is a genuine work of Roger Bacon. other parts of the Epistola also corre-
spond with Bacon’s genuine works: these will be discussed below.

As I demonstrate below, the last three chapters of the Epistola contain
many Decknamen, or code names. Two of these are particularly striking:

numerous references to Adam in the genuine works of Bacon, he never refers
to his bones, perhaps suggesting that the last chapter is not genuine.

criticism which states that in the case of variations between manuscripts,
the more unusual reading is likely the original.22 According to this princi-
ple, when faced with unusual or unfamiliar words and phrases, scribes
would replace them with more common alternatives. Thus “ossa adae”
could be the correct interpretation, and was only replaced with “omnibus
et” at a later date. determining which alternative—if either—is the correct
one will require more work.
There are many other variations between most of the manuscripts and
the printed versions, although they are generally less significant in terms of
identifying a potential author for the Epistola than the previous two. for
instance, almost all manuscripts that contain chapter 7 state that peasants
who live without the aid of doctors regularly live to be 160, while half of
printed versions change that to 100 years.23 In his other works on the
prolongatio vitae, Bacon does not specify an age to which the peasants
tend to live, only that they live long lives, so this provides no additional
information as to whether the Epistola is genuine. Also in chapter 7, most
manuscripts read “ut in potentatibus vivant homines vxxx annis” (while as
a rule men live 80 years), while most print versions read “ut in pluribus
vivant homines vxxx annis” (while many men live 80 years). Both versions
are in accord with Bacon’s thought, but the first is a bit more fitting with
his idea that life has progressively shortened over time.
There are also variations among the manuscripts themselves. Aside
from certain manuscripts ending at the beginning of chapter 6, the varia-
tions between the extant copies are largely minor. Most are some form of
word swapping—most manuscripts will have one word, such as “elemen-
torum,” but one or two manuscripts will have a different word, like
“oleorum.”24 Another example is “hominum” instead of “ovum.”25
A second common type of variation is the addition or deletion of a
phrase. Such phrases, usually not the main clause of the sentence, are
found in some manuscript versions but not in others. Two of the most
common are found in chapter 7: only two manuscripts add a description

whole suggests that Thorndike and Singer’s argument—that chapters 1
through 8 are genuine, while 9 through 11 are spurious—may not be cor-

argument. While it is true that the earliest two manuscripts do not include
the last three chapters, they also do not include chapters 7 and 8, which
Singer and Thorndike considered genuine. Thus, there is no direct evi-
dence to support the idea that chapters 1 through 8 are genuine, and that
9 through 11 are spurious. of course, this is dependent entirely upon the
extant manuscripts: it must be restated that the original manuscript appears
to no longer be extant, and thus all of the surviving copies are just
that—copies.

Regarding the information provided by an examination of content, the
manuscript variations, especially those between the manuscript tradition as
a whole and the printed versions, demonstrates that there is no good rea-
son to assume that the Epistola is not genuine. Additionally, based on the
variations within the different manuscripts, it is possible to create a tenta-
tive stemma. The original manuscript, α, is no longer extant. It likely con-
tained all 11 chapters, and included the stories about the man who
extended his life during the time of octavius Augustus and the additional
information on the preparation of mulsum. The remaining descendants of
α form three families: β, which contains manuscripts om and R, and their
descendants, fm and ot and P and L, respectively; γ with manuscripts Lh
and c; and finally, Rc and its descendants, Ls and od. Each family has one
piece that is not found in the other two: family β contains the information
on petroleo albo; family Rc has the story of octavius Augustus, and family
γ has the information on the preparation of mulsum.
The manuscripts were placed into the three families based on which of
the three stories they contained. They were then further organized based
on other internal variations (fig. c.1).30
The most reliable of the manuscripts are thus om, Lh, c, and Rc, and
any critical edition should rely heavily on these manuscripts. R and its
descendants are unreliable, for they contain many inaccuracies and large
sections of text are out of order.
Themes

the Epistola are very similar to a number of Bacon’s genuine works, espe-
cially the Opus minus.31 In fact, Newman has argued that the main differ-
ence between the Epistola and the relevant sections of the Opus minus is
not theoretical, but practical: the Epistola gives more practical techniques
