# Roger Bacon and the Incorruptible Human, 1220–1292

## Section: Conclusion

Conclusion
Very little is known about the end of Bacon’s life. There is some evidence
that he was back in Oxford around 1270, and may have spent the rest of
his life there. There is also no official record of his death, although tradi-
tion holds that he died in June 1292, and was buried with the Franciscans
in Oxford.1 In 1919, the Oxford Franciscans (Greyfriars Church and
Friary) moved from their original location to a new site in Oxford, and
Bacon’s final resting place has been lost.2 Still, memorials to Bacon can be
found throughout Oxford: the Oxford University Museum of Natural
History has a statue of Bacon in the court, where he takes his place among
other famous scientists throughout the ages, including Aristotle, William
Harvey, Francis Bacon, and Charles Darwin. There is also Roger Bacon
Lane in the center of the city, near Pembroke College, which has a plaque
commemorating Bacon’s dedication to science.
As with the professional physicians, there is still debate as to what extent
Bacon was considered an authority, or was at least influential on his fellow
Franciscans. Elizabeth Brown has argued that the fourteenth-century

Likewise, Bacon is often credited as being a part of a larger practice of

between the theories of all the thirteenth- and fourteenth-century
Franciscans who studied alchemy. And while it is true that there were

“Franciscan alchemist,” the fantasticus John of Rupescissa (ca.

only curing disease, but also prolonging life and stabilizing the church in
times of hardship.
However, by examining their alchemical philosophies in light of their
goals, it is evident that Bacon was an unlikely source of inspiration for
Rupescissa. The relationship between Franciscans and alchemy is more
complicated than merely providing a means to prolong life. This compari-
son of two of the more famous Franciscan medical alchemists of the thir-
teenth and fourteenth centuries suggests that in the decades immediately
following his death, Bacon was not as much of an authority on the subject
as he would have wished, even among his fellow Franciscans. More impor-
tantly, it suggests that there was no uniquely “Franciscan” approach to
alchemy in the late Middle Ages.

According to Rupescissa, only something that was itself incorruptible
would be able to prolong life. This incorruptible substance would be able
to restore equality to an imbalanced body, much like Bacon’s corpus equale.
In De consideratione, Rupescissa wrote that it would “reduce to equality
all things, and removes all superfluous things of whatever quality; and
restores all lost quality.”11 This substance must be something that has the
same nature of the four qualities that human bodies were composed of,
but it must be like the heavens with respect to the elements—that is,
incorruptible. Such a substance does exist, and is called the quintessence:
Now the philosophers have called the sky, the fifth essence, with regard to
the four elements; for the heaven in itself is incorruptible and unchanging;
not receiving in itself mutations or foreign impressions; if not by the com-
mand of God; likewise also the thing that we seek, with regard to and respect
for the four qualities of our bodies, is the fifth essence, in itself incorruptible,
if it remains eternally; and it is not hot, nor dry like fire, nor wet nor cold
like water; nor hot nor wet like air; nor cold nor dry like the earth; but it is
fifth essence, from contrary things, just like the incorruptible sky; and it
gives what is needed: sometimes wet, sometimes hot, sometimes cold, and
sometimes dry.12

Despite its superlunary origins, the quintessence’s actions on earth
would not be entirely incorruptible. The quintessence was incorruptible in
regard to the four elements, which undergo transmutation and change,
but was not as incorruptible as the heavens, which were truly unchang-
ing.14 This enabled Rupescissa to avoid claims that the quintessence did
not work as promised. If it were truly incorruptible, then it would pre-
serve human bodies forever. Rupescissa wrote: “For if it were totally incor-
ruptible, like the sky, it would perpetuate our bodies. Which our Lord
Jesus Christ, creator of nature forbids.”15 Humans could not completely
capture the incorruptibility of the heavens not only because Christ forbade
it, but because human art was not sophisticated enough to mimic nature.
Bacon, however, believed that scientia experimentalis could perfect nature.
Bacon’s corpus equale could be truly equal, but could not pass on its equal-
ity fully due to the inability of its species to enact a total change in the
recipient matter.
The fact that the quintessence could be made from many different
things, and could be made in different degrees, is reminiscent of Bacon’s
alchemical remedies, which he said came in varying degrees of perfection.
Artephius produced a medicine better than that of the ploughman, and so
he was able to extend his life much longer. However, this is not evidence
that Bacon was a direct source of inspiration for Rupescissa. For one, the
idea that medicines came in different degrees was quite common in
European medical schools by the end of the thirteenth century and begin-
ning of the fourteenth, thanks to translations from Arabic.16 Given
Rupescissa’s obvious reliance on John Mesue, it is not a stretch to say that
he had incorporated such ideas into his own works. Likewise, Halleux has
shown that Arnald of Villanova’s work directly influenced Rupescissa, and

Even with its superlunary nature, the quintessence was meant to be
incorporated into the standard pharmacology, either to strengthen tradi-
tional remedies or as a separate, but still fundamentally Galenic, medicine.
One cannot, therefore, argue that Rupescissa’s quintessence was an
entirely new remedy that had never been seen before—its properties were
unique, as were its connections with aqua vitae, but its basic mode of
action—restoring the humors, was not. The quintessence provided what
the body was lacking and removed what was in excess.18 This was the same
concept that Bacon used in his corpus equale. Rupescissa’s quintessence
did not stray radically from either medieval natural philosophy or standard
Galenic medical practice. It still relied on the four humors within the body,
the processes of digestion that converted food to body, and even the
humidum radicale responsible for aging, something which Bacon had
largely ignored.
Was Roger Bacon a source for John of Rupescissa’s alchemy? Probably
not, or at least not in a significant way, despite their breaks with Jabirian
thought on conferring incorruptibility and their adherence to a Galenic
medical framework. Although Rupescissa did not cite many of his
sources—the differences in their understanding of both the role of apoca-
lypticism in alchemy and their notions of the elixir make Bacon an improb-
able source of inspiration. Additionally, Bacon’s alchemy, especially that
involving metals, was heavily based on the pseudo-Avicennan De anima,
which combines human blood (a substitute for the prima materia) with
mercury and calces of the metals, while Rupescissa did not incorporate the
principles from this text.
Even without the previous discussion, there should be little reason to
assume that Rupescissa would have been significantly influenced by Bacon,
just because they were both Franciscans who wrote on alchemy and the
prolongatio vitae. There was serious interest in prolonging life in the thir-
teenth and fourteenth centuries, not to mention the growing body of
alchemical literature, both practical and theoretical. Rupescissa had every-
thing he would need to develop his own theory of medical alchemy with-
out turning to Bacon. The intellectual connection between the two, based

If Bacon was not the authority he had hoped to be in the decades
immediately following his death, his fortunes were to change. By the
beginning of the early modern period, Bacon had become an exemplar of
English alchemical thought.19 The French physician and Paracelsian devo-
tee Joseph du Chesne (d. 1609) considered Bacon to be one of the men
who had worked on discovering the “universal medicine.” Du Chesne’s
list of doctors and philosophers who worked on this panacea included
many names familiar from the history of alchemy: aside from Bacon, he
lists Ramon Llull (d. 1316), John of Rupescissa, George Ripley (d. 1490),
and Christopher of Paris (fifteenth c.).20 Work on the prolongatio vitae had
continued after Bacon’s death: letters from the reign of Henry VI of
England (d. 1471) show that his own doctors were interested in the pos-
sibility of alchemy to enhance medicine, and he granted them a charter to
practice alchemy, despite chrysopoeia being illegal since the reign of his
grandfather, Henry IV.21
Bacon’s place in the history of science continues to be a point of discus-
sion, although much of it is focused on his role in the development of
mathematics and experimental theories in the Middle Ages. This book has
shed light on an understudied area of his work—his continued interest in
alchemy and its role in medicine, and how it connected with the thirteenth-
century milieu in which he was writing. Alchemy, rather than solely being
about transmuting base metals into noble ones, found its greatest use in
the prolongatio vitae. Borrowing the idea of a medicine for metals from
pseudo-Avicenna’s De anima, Bacon was the first to develop a 1:1 corre-
spondence between the medicine of metals and the medicine of people.
He synthesized material from many different sources, including the genu-
ine and spurious Aristotle, Avicenna, and Averroes to develop a theory
uniquely his: that a body of equal complexion, in which the elements were

Rather than needing a specific medicine for each disease (or metal, as De
anima taught), Bacon’s corpus equale for humans was universal,22 and
worked by virtue of its own equality.
This equality was passed on to the “sick” individual through the multi-
plication of species, in which the agent containing a corpus equale enacted
a change in the potentiality of the matter of the recipient body, bringing it
to a state closer to equality. However, species could not enact a complete
change in the recipient matter, and so the corpus equale could not bring
the human body to a state of total equality. Before sinning, Adam and Eve
had bodies that had an almost perfect balance of the elements, and would
have been able to live forever by eating the fruit from the Tree of Life,
which was itself a corpus equale. However, as sins were compounded over
the years and man lost access to the blessed fruit, the elemental balance of
the human body had deteriorated and man had experienced a shortening
of life to its absolute minimum of 80 years. But Bacon believed this decay
to be accidental, not natural or ordained by God, and so was remediable
using the powers of art and nature.
Additionally, humans retained the possibility of not dying, for God had
decreed that the body man would receive after the resurrection will be
immortal and incorruptible, and thus have this perfect equality of ele-
ments. Many of Bacon’s ideas about the body improved by his medicines
of a corpus equale came from his understanding of the resurrection body
and his familiarity with the Bible. The Bible taught that man had the abil-
ity to live forever, and that this ability will be realized in the next life. Thus,
his knowledge of biblical exegesis, as well as discussions in the greater
Franciscan community about the nature of the human body and its poten-
tial for immortality, played a significant role in shaping his ideas about the
abilities—and limits—of medicine.
Bacon, despite having many original ideas regarding the role of alchemy
and a corpus equale in the prolongatio vitae, was far from alone in believing
that such an extension of life was possible. Almost everyone in the thir-
teenth century—and even throughout the Middle Ages—recognized that

Even many university-trained physicians of the late thirteenth and early

As Bacon has tended to be studied in a vacuum, especially in regard to
his medical theories, one of the questions asked at the beginning of the
book was: how involved was Bacon in the medical community? Bacon
often castigated the doctors for not knowing the basics of their practice,
namely speculative alchemy, which taught of the generation of things from
the elements. Was this true, or was it a rhetorical device Bacon used to
emphasize his point? It was certainly not a mere exaggeration on Bacon’s
part: alchemy was not incorporated into medical learning at the university
in a meaningful way, at least not in the decades after his death, and no
medical writers explicitly named him as an important source of information.
However, this does not mean that Bacon was entirely ignored. Many
physicians in the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries were interested
in the possibility of alchemy to create medicines, but there was no system-
atic incorporation of it into university curricula. Additionally, physicians’
disinterest in Bacon’s works likely stemmed not from any ideas that Bacon
held, but from his position as an unpatronized Franciscan. It was not com-
mon practice for physicians to cite those outside of their field, even if they
were eminent natural philosophers or encyclopedists.
And despite his remonstrations against the state of medical learning,
Bacon was largely content with its theories. He agreed with Galenic teach-
ing that there were four humors that comprised the body, and that they
should be in the correct proportion with each other for health. His use of
medical sources was mixed: occasionally he cited works that were decreas-
ing in popularity, but he also relied heavily on newly translated Arabic
works, especially those of Avicenna and Averroes. Perhaps the most sig-
nificant break he had with contemporary medical literature was his disre-
gard for the lamp metaphor, in which the body’s innate heat consumed its
own radical humidity like a flame consumed oil, thus determining an indi-
vidual’s lifespan. This rejection may have led him to ignore the potential
uses of ethanol, or aqua vitae, despite its growing popularity in the thir-
teenth century with the works of men like Peter of Spain and Taddeo
Alderotti. But even this was not a rejection of the medical system at large,
merely one facet of it.

The corpus equale and its universal nature was what made alchemy so
important to Bacon’s scheme of the prolongatio vitae, even when many of
his contemporaries were rejecting its place in medicine. Alchemy provided
a system of natural philosophy in which everything in the sublunary world
was connected, and provided a logical and knowable way to understand
the interactions of all things, be they organic or inorganic. Through spec-
ulative alchemy, one could come to understand the generation and cor-
ruption of all things, and how the elements and their qualities would
interact, while the practical aspects of alchemy taught how to make the
corpus equale. At the beginning of this book, I asked if it was possible to
clearly delineate between “alchemy” and “medicine” in the works of
Roger Bacon. I hope to have shown that alchemy was the foundation
upon which medicine was built. Without the concepts found in medical
alchemy, Bacon considered the physicians to be no better than apothecar-
ies, blindly feeling their way in the dark. Harkening back to Galen’s fears
about the empiricist school of medicine over 1000 years before him,
Bacon was worried that medicine without theory was nothing but trial and
error, which could be fatal for the patient.
The study of the history of medicine in the medieval period has blos-
somed in the past decades. While scholars in the first part of the twentieth
century saw medieval medicine as largely derivative—a type of place-
holder between the beginnings of medicine in Ancient Greece and Rome
and then medicine’s development to its fullest potential in the renaissance
and modern periods—scholars of the past half-century have worked hard
to remedy this incorrect—and Eurocentric—narrative. New work on the
medieval theories and practice of medicine, both in the Latin West, Middle
East, and Arabic-speaking Mediterranean—and their connections with
each other—have greatly enhanced our understanding of this period in the
history of medicine. Scholars have also recently begun to branch out into
other, understudied areas of medicine, such as pharmacology, gynecology
and obstetrics, and the practices of individuals who were not Latin-
speaking men trained at universities.
And while scholarship has begun connecting medicine to other sciences
in the Middle Ages, much remains to be done on the relationship between
alchemy and medicine. Though this topic has become popular in the early
modern period, outside of a small group of French and Italian scholars,
the role of medieval alchemy in medicine has largely been ignored.
Likewise, the study of alchemy in the Middle Ages has tended to focus on
transmutational alchemy, rather than its applications to medicine and

pharmacology. This book unites these two fields of study, to show that the
connections between alchemy, medicine, and pharmacology that fre-
quently appear in the early modern period extend back to the Middle
Ages. As potentially the first person to fully connect the idea of a medicine
for metals to a medicine for people, it is vital that we understand Bacon’s
thought processes, and their evolution, surrounding the role of alchemy in
medicine. Without understanding the complexities of Bacon’s thought,
and how they changed over time as he became more engaged with litera-
ture such as pseudo-Avicenna’s De anima, we cannot hope to understand
the progression of the idea of (al)chemical medicine over the longue durée.
Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholarship viewed Bacon as a
visionary, unique in a world reliant not on experiment, but on authority.
These historians tried to make Bacon into something he was not—a fore-
runner of modern science who anticipated the scientific method and was
always at the forefront of innovation. He was their beacon of light in the
“Dark Ages.” Bacon was unique in many aspects, especially the incorpora-
tion of alchemical practice and theory in medicine, but if we look back at
the history of medicine and are aware of its progression throughout time,
we see that he was not a forerunner in the true sense of the word. That is,
he was not the first to incorporate new translations or engage in the
debates among medical faculty in the universities. He was not writing
books that the next generation of medical men, those active in the early
fourteenth century, were reading and explicitly incorporating into their
practice.
Previous historiography about Bacon’s medical knowledge seems to
indicate he was squarely within his time, and looked back to the past,
rather than being on the forefront of new knowledge. Bacon’s uniqueness
was generally attributed to his ability to synthesize large amounts of mate-
rial from different sources, incompatible with each other, into a largely
cohesive idea. His medicine was no exception: Getz has argued that
Bacon’s innovation (and importance in the history of medicine) came
from the fact that he was able to collate many sources into the idea of the
prolongatio vitae found nowhere else.23 He was influenced almost as much
by scripture as he was by encyclopedias and medical works, and stressed
the moral aspects of the liberal arts.
And yet, Bacon continues to be a prominent figure in the historiogra-
phy of medicine and alchemy. This status is not undeserving—Bacon’s
