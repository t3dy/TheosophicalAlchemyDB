# Roger Bacon and the Incorruptible Human, 1220–1292

## Section: chapter 4 of the Epistola does not appear elsewhere in Bacon’s genuine

chapter 4 of the Epistola does not appear elsewhere in Bacon’s genuine
works. Additionally, Bacon does not mention the corpus equale in chapter
7, which is unusual, given his reliance on it in his other medical works,
even those written early in his career. However, given the number of pas-
sages of the Epistola that correlate to a wide range of Bacon’s known
works, there is much evidence to support the theory that the Epistola is
genuine, or at least a compilation of the ideas found in his genuine works,
made by one of his followers.

As is evident from the list, over 2/3 of the Decknamen do appear else-
where in Bacon’s genuine works, and almost all of them mean the same
thing in the Epistola as they do in the confirmed genuine work. Additionally,
they are found in a selection of his works, not just one or two. This lends
support to the argument that this could be a genuine work of Bacon, as
the author of the Epistola would have to be familiar with a large portion of
Bacon’s work on alchemy. However, it does not discount the theory that
a later follower of Bacon compiled the Epistola from the material found in
Bacon’s genuine works.
The Philosophers’ Egg
Throughout the past century, scholars have argued about the identifica-
tion of the philosophers’ egg. famously, Lieutenant-colonel Hime tried
to argue in 1914 that the last three chapters of the Epistola were a cipher
that described the creation of gunpowder. Hime went so far as to claim
that Bacon was the originator of gunpowder in Europe.42 However,
Thorndike observed that there were serious flaws with this argument,
including the fact that Bacon already speaks of gunpowder by name in the
Opus tertium, where he describes it as being used in toys. If Bacon had
invented gunpowder, Thorndike says, then the Epistola would have to
predate the Opera, although there is no evidence that it was produced that

More recent scholarship has rejected the theory of the philosophers’
egg as a cryptogram for gunpowder. one of the more widely accepted
theories now was proposed by Newman in 1989: the philosophers’ egg is
identical to the philosophers’ stone, which Bacon found in the Secretum
secretorum of Pseudo-Aristotle. According to Bacon, the “stone” is any-
thing upon which alchemy begins, although the best stone is human
blood.45,46 The stone, made into a corpus equale, can cure both people and
metals, which he derives partially from reading Pseudo-Avicenna’s De ani-
ma.47 Bacon identified the corpus equale with the prima materia in the
Opus minus. The prima materia is simple blood, made of the qualities hot
and wet. This humor is found in all things on earth, and all things can be
reduced into it.48
As was shown, Bacon thought that speculative alchemy taught about
the generation of all things from the four elements of fire, air, water, and
earth. These combined to form the simple humors: blood, phlegm, cho-
ler, and melancholy, which then combined to form the four complex
humors. The blood that flows through human veins is complex blood, and
can be separated into the simple humors, which after stratification and
distillation, go by the terms aqua vitae (phlegm), humor aereus (blood),
virtus ignea (choler), and calx (melancholy).49 I see no good reason to

The final comparison involves the abilities of the egg. As is evident from
the previous sections, the egg of the Epistola is meant to be a medicine for
metals. Even the term “laxative medicine” is found in both contexts—that
is, human and metal—throughout Bacon’s known works. This corre-
sponds to the egg of the Opus tertium: the following is a description of
how this elixir can transmute base metals into noble ones. first, Bacon says;
Medicina, vel medicina laxativa, vocatur que, projecta in plumbum liquatum,
convertit illud in aurum; et cuprum convertit in argentum.50
Then later in the paragraph:
Et quod talis medicina sit possibilis, Avicenna et omnes attestantur. Vel
maius opus dicitur quando fit operatio super partes animalis ut queratur
medicina; minus vero, quando super arsenicum, vel sulphur, vel aliud inani-
matum, vel super plura eorum, quia nunquam tam nobilis medicina potest
haberi per hec inanimata, sicut per partes animalium. Lapis vero preparatus
est medicina iam facta, quod est ipsum elixir, potens transmutare metalla
viliora in nobiliora.51
This description sounds like a medicine entirely for metals, not humans.
However, there is a single line at the end of chapter 11 of the Epistola
that changes the abilities of the egg. Here, the author writes:
Et haec estimatur simplicior medicina quae reperiri potest, et purior; et haec
valet contra febres, et passiones animi, et corporis.52
This is not the medicine of a metal, but one intended for a human.
So why does the Epistola attribute (human) healing powers to the phi-
losophers’ egg when the Opus tertium does not? It cannot be that Bacon
has not considered the power of a corpus equale on a human body when
writing the Opus tertium: this theme of equality of elements excluding
action and corruption appears in some form in all three opera. Looking at
the timeline of Bacon’s oeuvre may provide some clarification. Bacon was
heavily influenced by the alchemical practices in pseudo-Avicenna’s De
anima, which helped him formulate the idea of a 1:1 correspondence
between a medicine for metals and a medicine for people. His changing
ideas surrounding the prolongatio vitae between writing the Opus maius

Sources in the Epistola and Genuine Works
Additional support for the argument that the Epistola is a genuine work of
Bacon is provided by the sources it utilizes. The 11 chapters of the Epistola
contain references to 29 works and 16 authors.54 All 16 of these authors
can be found elsewhere in Bacon’s genuine corpus: all 16 can be found in
the Opus Maius, and 14 of the 16 are found in at least two works by
Bacon. Authors who write on broad topics, such as Josephus and Pliny the
Elder, are referenced in no less than six works by Bacon. Regarding the
citations to specific works, 22 of the 29 are referenced elsewhere in Bacon’s
oeuvre. Additionally, the majority of the specific ideas referenced in the
Epistola appear elsewhere in Bacon’s corpus. for example, the Epistola’s
reference to Solinus’ De mirabilibus mundi, in which he says that female
horses can occasionally become impregnated through the scent of a male
alone is also found in the Opus maius. The consistency of these citations
across Bacon’s corpus, especially those that occur in Bacon’s more special-
ized works, such as De erroribus medicorum and De multiplicatione
specierum, suggests that the author of the Epistola, if not Bacon himself,
was very familiar with Bacon’s works. The following Table c.2 contains a

It is possible that the Epistola was a later work, written by someone who
was intimately acquainted with Bacon’s works, especially the three Opera
and the Communia naturalium. However, applying the principle of parsi-
mony, the simplest answer is that the Epistola is a genuine work of Roger
Bacon, and is an example of his reuse of materials (and ideas) in different
ways throughout his career. Reviewing the evidence provided by the three
topics studied, content, placement, and themes, there is nothing in any of
the three of them to automatically exclude the Epistola from Bacon’s gen-
uine corpus. In fact, there is every reason to suggest that it should be
included: the incorporation of material similar to that found elsewhere in
his work, the uniformity of the manuscripts, and the reliance on authori-
ties that Bacon is known to have used.
Even when the evidence does not directly support authenticity, such as
the placement within larger texts, it is merely inconclusive, not evidence
against the argument in question. The strongest evidence against the
authenticity is the dating of the last two chapters to the years 1206 and
1233, respectively. But this does not necessarily mean that they were not
at least copied down by Bacon in an otherwise original work. Bacon was a
skillful synthesizer of different materials and ideas—it would have been
possible that he was merely incorporating someone else’s practical knowl-
edge, much like he had when writing the glosses to the Secretum secreto-
rum, whose main text contains practical instructions for creating the gloria
inestimabilis. In light of this study, the Epistola should retain its position
within the corpus of Bacon’s genuine works.
