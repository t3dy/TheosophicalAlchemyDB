Following mannerist perspective back into deep space, the engraving

inward and yet out into the light, in much the same way as the alche-

All three readings of this image are imaginative responses to the degradation
of the engraving in the 1609 edition (compare the three images in Fig. 2.44),
something which must have intrigued and puzzled contemporary readers,
encouraging them to develop their own interpretations.334

Szulakowska does, however, make the thought-provoking observation
that the presence of motifs drawn from perspectival geometry, architecture,
Pythagorean mathematics, and alchemy in this fourth circular figure all sug-
gest the astrological influence of the planet Saturn, symbol of intellectual and
metaphysical thought as well as the melancholy attached to such endeavour
(not to ignore Saturn’s significance as the alchemical symbol for the metal
lead and thereby one possible candidate for primal matter).339 This interpre-
tation receives support from the above-mentioned words on the tablecloth,
which refer to sacred music’s ability to dispel ‘sadness and evil spirits’. While
it is true that Khunrath deals with the issue of melancholy in his medical trea-

the ‘emblematic tradition of “Melancholia” […] which originated in Albrecht

disregarding as it does the fine balance in Khunrath’s figure between the active
and contemplative life, the terrestrial, celestial and indeed supercelestial
worlds, as well as ignoring the jocular, buoyant and even rhapsodic elements
in the Amphitheatre.340

This consideration of sleep and dreams leads us to another possible inter-
pretative perspective, with the presence of the phrase ‘Dormiens Vigila’ (While
Sleeping, Keep Watch) on the architrave of the portico leading out of the
main hall into the room with bed and drapes on the left. In the Amphitheatre
Khunrath writes that ‘sleeping … you will be Divinely admonished, taught
[and] instructed with Visions’.349 Even in sleep, vigilance is necessary to be
receptive to God’s angels as both bringers of dreams and themselves appari-
tions in visions.350 Purš suggests that the Oratory-Laboratory’s distorted per-
spective evokes ‘the deformation of space occuring in a dream or fantastical
vision’ and that the admonition to vigilance doubtless alludes to the Song of
Solomon 5:2 ‘I sleep, and my heart watcheth’.351 Certainly, many of Khunrath’s
readers would have been well aware of the theme of dream revelation in
alchemical literature, from Zosimos of Panopolis in antiquity to Giovanni
Battista Nazari’s Della tramutatione metallica sogni tre (Three Dreams about
Metallic Transmutation, 1599).352
Thus, it could be argued that these divisions of space in the engraving create
four axial points: Oratorium and Laboratorium, Auditorium and Dormitorium,
each relating in their own way to the overarching notion of divine inspiration.
Khunrath’s foregrounding of sacred music’s ability to dispel evil spirits should
perhaps be seen as an apotropaic device for turning away harmful influences
prior to the reader’s entry into the other spaces.
Heuer’s arguments concerning the Hampton Court painting can be applied
to Khunrath’s engraving: in the ‘dynamic switching of attention prompted by
the disjointed spaces’, in the eye’s movement ‘among the different spaces –
foreground, background, ceiling, floor’,353 there is an inherent dynamic-
tension between the Oratory and the Laboratory.

18 Underlying Geometries and Symmetries in the Four
“Theosophical” Figures354
Returning to De Villiers’ hypothetical link between the clothed second figure of
Adam Androgyne and the naked third figure of the Rebis brings us to the ques-
tion of possible intended connections between the 1595 engravings. The first
three circular figures each display highly-structured geometrical layouts, var-
iously incorporating concentric circles, triangles, and squares, some of which
echo elements in other images: Figures 2.2 and 2.3, for example, both have tri-
angles bearing the words ANIMA (SOUL), SPIRITUS (SPIRIT), and CORPUS
(BODY), to emphasise the trinitarian natures of both microcosms: Man as
Adam Androgyne (Fig. 2.46) and the Philosophers’ Stone as Rebis (Fig. 2.47).
The implicit association Khunrath makes between the cabalistic Androgyne
and the alchemical Hermaphrodite becomes clear in On Primaterial Chaos,
where we find him writing in an alchemical context of
Our ADAM, from which his EVE came forth. The Macrocosmically Micro-
cosmic Universal Hermaphrodite of Nature, the Philosophers’ Philosoph-
ical Man of both sex, that is man and woman at the same time … Form
and Matter … it is called Universal ELECTRUM, and Universal Androgyne
of the Wise.355
The interconnectedness of the three realms (Divine, Human, and Natural) rep-
resented by these images is further reinforced by overlapping themes: Figures 1
and 2 both deal with Cabala, while Figures 2 and 3 both include alchemical
material. Neo-Pythagorean number symbolism is yet another linking factor:
Figure 2 contains a formula resembling one found in the works of the magi-
cal abbot Johannes Trithemius (1462–1516) and the Paracelsian Gérard Dorn
(c.1530–1584): ‘Let the Binary be rejected, and the Ternary be reduced, by
means of the Quaternary, to the simplicity of the Monad’.356 This is echoed in
