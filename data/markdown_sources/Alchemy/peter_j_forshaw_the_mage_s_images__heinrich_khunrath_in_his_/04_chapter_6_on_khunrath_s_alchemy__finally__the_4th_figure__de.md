of the Oratory-Laboratory, is simply said to be teaching Theology, ‘Alloquium
Divinum, conversation with God through prayer and the Holy Spirit.’401
According to Johann Christoph Lenz (1748–1795), editor of an edition of
De Igne reprinted in 1783,402 the anonymous author of the Judicium was none
other than the Lutheran mystic and follower of Melanchthon, the pietist
Johann Arndt (1555–1621), whose religious writings include the famous Vier
Bücher vom Wahren Christentum (Four Books on True Christianity, 1606), who
had praised the Amphitheatre as early as 1596 in his Ikonographia or commen-
tary on the right use and misuse of pictures in the Bible.403 Arndt had also
studied spagyric medicine at the University of Basel with Theodor Zwinger,
though a decade before Khunrath; and his Liber Naturae (Book of Nature),
the fourth book of Wahren Christentum, reveals the influence of Paracelsus’s
philosophy of nature.404 In his Unpartheyischer Kirchen- und Ketzerhistorie
(Impartial History of the Church and Heretics, 1699–1700), the radical pietist
Gottfried Arnold (1666–1714) likewise numbered Arndt amongst those who
had admired Khunrath and his Amphitheatre.405

(active. 1608–1620) also published a reprint of the Geistliche Lieder und

phon, and that even the editor’s preface is dated 1609, it appears that the 1608
Amphitheatre may be a pirated edition. This speculation receives some support
from the fact that Braun was the son-in-law and occasional business partner
and printer of the notoriously piratical Johann Francke, who had published the
1599 edition of Khunrath’s Magnesia.432 As the rest of this work is identical to
the first print run of the more widely available edition of the following year, let
us now move forward in time to 1609.

In a letter to the reader written in Wernigerode, in the German County of
Stolberg, dated the Ides of March, 1609, the Amphitheatre’s editor Erasmus
Wolfart explains that because of the untimely demise of its author, he has had
to see the work into print ‘as much because I was linked to him by intimate
familiarity, as because he had made me participant in his counsels, in this work
to be published, along with others more secret left by him.’446 Little is known
about Wolfart, other than that he was an imperial public notary.447 It is con-
ceivable that he might have been related to the Swiss physician and humanist
scholar Theodor Zwinger (1533–1588), who taught at Basel during Khunrath’s
period of study, and whose step-father was the philosopher, theologian, alche-
mist and Basel professor of grammar and dialectic Conrad Wolffhart, alias

The simplicity of the original two-part 1595 Amphitheatre (text and engrav-
ings), described by Arndt’s Judicium, has now become a more complicated
affair, especially since no two surviving copies seem to follow exactly the
same format, due to the absence of any clear indications of where to place
the engravings. Although the instruction ‘Let the figure of the first [second,
third, etc] Grade, be inserted here’ has been placed at the start of each Grade of
Khunrath’s commentary,452 in the colophon the printer includes an apology,
explaining that the words had been added in error, for none of the individual
grades have their own figures, but the ‘Amphitheatre itself stands with its figures
and has its Introductions’, which seems to imply that the real Amphitheatre is
the engravings and everything else is explanatory supplementary material.453
It is evident from differences in ornamentation and minor typographic
variations that there was more than one print run for the 1609 edition. For
the ornamentation, consider the upper and lower bands on the cover page of
Khunrath’s ‘Interpretations and Annotations’ in the 1609 copies owned by the
Allard Pierson, University of Amsterdam (Fig. 1.51) and the Getty (Fig. 1.52).

in the 1608 Magdeburg edition, which suggests that it is the earlier of the 1609
print runs.
