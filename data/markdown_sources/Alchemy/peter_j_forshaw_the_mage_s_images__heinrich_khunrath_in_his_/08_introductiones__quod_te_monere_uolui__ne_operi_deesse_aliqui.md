While woodcuts could be printed concurrently with movable type in one
process on the same hand press, copperplate engravings had to be printed
separately on a rolling press, which squeezed plate and paper more firmly
together.630 Khunrath’s engravings are not numbered and since early modern
publishers sold text as unbound quires and the engravings would have been
provided as a suite of unbound prints, it is hardly suprising that the individual
book-binder or the person commissioning the binding might be puzzled as to
where to place the engravings. It is unusual, nevertheless, to find such a wide
diversity of sequences.

34 Interconnections and Correspondences
Despite the uncertainty regarding any intended sequence, what is clear is
that many interconnections and correspondences can be found between the
engravings, some of which have already been mentioned in relation to the
original 1595 series of four circular “Theosophical” figures.
Although Khunrath gives no explicit indication of links between the cir-
cular and rectangular engravings, the very fact that the same ‘seven oracu-
lar Laws’ from Reuchlin’s Christian Cabalist De verbo mirifico (1494) appear
in both the Isagoge to Circular Figures 1 and 2 and the rectangular Entrance
engraving implies a conceptual link between them. A relation is implied, too,
between Circular Figure 4 of the Oratory-Laboratory, the School of Nature and
the Alchemical Citadel by the repetition of the kneeling figure of the adept in
cruciform posture, while another appears standing at the far end of the cave
tunnel in the Entrance.633 The two postures respectively represent, perhaps,
following Tertullian, humility and penitence on the one hand and joy and con-
fidence on the other,634 and clearly emphasise the transcendent theosophical
motivation underlying Khunrath’s activities. As has been suggested and will be
discussed in more detail when we address the question of ‘spiritual alchemy’ in
Chapter 6, the image of the Christian-Cabalist androgynous Adam in Figure 2
is clearly intended to mirror the hermaphroditic alchemical Rebis in Figure 3,
relating as they do to the perfection of the microcosm and macrocosm. The
Solar-Lunar nature of the Rebis is undoubtedly echoed in the presence of the
Sun and Moon atop the obelisks in the Citadel.
In a similar way, the pentagram of five large flames with the Hebrew let-
ters הושהי YHSVH of the Christian-Cabalist Name for Christ in Circular Figure 1
(Fig. 2.144) reappears in the Calumniators along with the letter ‘S’ superimposed
on a cross (Fig. 2.145) found in the Entrance engraving, which in its turn con-
tains the Greek word ΜΥΣΤΗΡΙΟΝ [Mystērion – Mystery] (Fig. 2.146) that
appears on the drawbridge into the Alchemical Citadel (Fig. 2.147). This latter
engraving could be argued to be a counterpart to Circular Figure 1 in that the

35 Khunrath’s Dog
The one motif that runs through all the rectangular “Hieroglyphic” Figures,
together with the Portrait, is the image of Khunrath’s dog (Figs. 2.148–2.153),
most often curled up on the ground while his master is engaged in prayer
or conversation, attentively regarding the reader in the Portrait (Fig. 2.148);
bounding enthusiastically towards the entrance of the Citadel (Fig. 2.152).
While this could well be a genuine image of Khunrath’s canine companion,637
it is likely that it also emblematic of fidelity, in particular his Christian faith;
possibly even a symbol of Mercury.638

36 Hermes’s Caltrop in the Cave of Nature
Many of the links between images require a sharp (and possibly obsessive) eye.
A final example is found in the presence of a small pointed object that appears
in the right hand of Hermes-Mercury at the bottom of Khunrath’s Portrait
(Fig. 2.154) and also at the centre of the Calumniators engraving in a small
grotto surrounded by alchemical symbols (Fig. 2.155). Although Szulakowska
describes it as ‘a three-pointed star’, closer inspection reveals it to be not
tri- but quadricuspid, in the form of a tribulus or caltrop, a four-pointed spike
thrown on the ground to incapacitate horses in war.639
There is an allegorical drawing by Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519) of a fig-
ure, sometimes described as hermaphroditic or androgynous, though it seems
to be a hybrid or composite of youth and old age, described as an ‘Allegory
of Pleasure and Pain’ (c.1484–86), in which a bearded male, symbolising Pain,
holds a handful of spiked caltrops, some of which are falling on the ground.

both artist and military engineer, punningly remarks that ‘If you take pleas-

38 Hieroglyphica
Hieroglyphs, as Ashworth remarks, were looked on as a ‘language of symbolic
images – a language in which understanding is conveyed immediately, much as
God understands things, without the mediation of conventional language.’652
In 1419 the Florentine monk Cristoforo Buondelmonti (1386–c.1430), on behalf
of Cosimo de’ Medici (1389–1464), purchased a manuscript on the Greek isle of

44 Part IV: the Roles of the Images
Following this introduction to the Amphitheatre’s images, the question inevi-
tably arises as to what Khunrath saw as their purpose and how they related to
the accompanying text. In The Power of Images, David Freedberg discusses ‘the
need to consider all possible uses of images’, suggesting that we may be ‘calmed
by them, stirred by them’, moved to ‘wonder, desire, fellow-feeling, … to fury,
stirred with disdain … moved to true piety, drawn to devotion and reverence’,
to ‘hatred of sins, disgust of vanity, shrinking from bad companions.’ Images,
moreover, serve as aids to memory and recognition, they inspire awe and rouse
piety.791 In the following chapters, various uses of the Amphitheatre’s images
(devotional, sacramental, meditational, magical) will be considered in relation
to Khunrath’s occult and theosophical practices.792 Here we shall discuss their
more general pedagogical and polemical roles.

47 Retrospective Synopsis in the Rectangular Figures
The alchemical malpractices and mistakes featured in the Citadel relate far
more to Thrasybulus Ricenus’s ‘Treuhertzige Warnungs-Vermahnung’ (True-
Hearted Warning-Admonition) appended to On Primaterial Chaos (1597),
than they do to anything in the Amphitheatre’s text.814 It seems acceptable
to suggest that following their successful reception – attested by the praise
meted out to “Cunradius’s” warnings by Michael Maier in his Examen fucorum
pseudo-chymicorum (Swarm of Pseudo-Chymical Drones, 1617) – Khunrath
decided to increase the appeal of his updated Amphitheatre by including a
visual representation of them in a new engraving.815 The same could be said of
his discussion of the above-mentioned Hebrew word ‘Aben’, first discussed in
Chaos and subsequently appearing above the dragon’s head in the Citadel. The
inclusion of Latin and German versions of the Emerald Tablet in the Pyramid
engraving could be seen as an attempt to appeal to a wider readership, and
more closely resembles the bilingual nature of Khunrath’s other works, which
display a preference for the German vernacular, or at least for Latin with
German paraphrases, in contrast to the predominantly Latin Amphitheatre. Far
from being simple complementary illustrations of the text, these additional
rectangular engravings provide supplementary or alternative rubrics, encour-
aging the generation of further levels of meaning. In this way the Amphitheatre
Prologue and figures act almost like one extended emblematic tableau in that
only by long and repeated reflection on the, at first sight, puzzling relation-
ship between image and text, does the reader eventually arrive at the author’s
intended meanings.816

48 Raising up and Defending Truth
Khunrath does give us some indication of the intended purpose of his figures,
when at the beginning of the Amphitheatre he tells us that the book has been
provided with engravings ‘for the purpose of raising up and defending […]
