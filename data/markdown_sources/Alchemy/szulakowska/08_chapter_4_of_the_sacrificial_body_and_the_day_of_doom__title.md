# Szulakowska

## Section: Chapter 4 of The Sacrificial Body and the Day of Doom, titled Resurrected Flesh:

Chapter 4 of The Sacrificial Body and the Day of Doom, titled Resurrected Flesh: Alchemical
Resurrection and the Philosophers' Stone, explores how the concept of resurrection—central to
Christian eschatology—was reinterpreted by alchemists as part of their own spiritual and
material quest. Urszula Szulakowska focuses on how alchemical imagery and texts from the
Protestant Reformation used the metaphor of resurrection to symbolize the completion of the
alchemical work, particularly the creation of the philosopher's stone, which was seen as the key
to both spiritual and material perfection.
Main Argument of Chapter 4:
Szulakowska argues that the resurrection of the body, a fundamental Christian belief, was
reimagined by alchemists as a metaphor for the final stage of the alchemical process, where
base matter was purified and perfected into gold. For the alchemists, the philosopher's stone
symbolized this perfected state, representing not only material transformation but also the
resurrection of the soul into a state of spiritual purity. The chapter emphasizes that alchemists
saw their work as a form of resurrection, where the death and putrefaction of base matter led to
its eventual rebirth as spiritual gold, reflecting Christ’s own resurrection from the dead.
Key Themes in Chapter 4:
1. Alchemy and Christian Resurrection: Szulakowska begins by outlining the central role
of resurrection in Christian theology, particularly its connection to the body of Christ. She

[Page 60]
then explains how alchemists appropriated this concept to describe their own work,
where the resurrection of matter symbolized its transmutation into a higher, purer form.
In alchemical terms, resurrection occurred after the death and putrefaction of base
matter, which was necessary for its transformation into gold.
She writes: “For alchemists, the resurrection of base matter into gold mirrored the
resurrection of Christ’s body from death. This spiritual and material rebirth was the
culmination of the alchemical process, where the philosopher’s stone represented the
perfected form of matter and soul” (p. 85).
2. The Philosopher’s Stone as a Symbol of Resurrection: The philosopher’s stone,
often described as the quintessence of matter, became the ultimate symbol of
resurrection in alchemical texts. Szulakowska explores how the philosopher’s stone was
portrayed as both the goal of the alchemical work and a metaphor for the resurrection of
the body and soul. Alchemists believed that just as Christ’s body had been resurrected,
so too could matter be reborn into its most perfect form.
She explains: “The philosopher’s stone was not merely a substance that could transform
metals into gold, but also a symbol of spiritual resurrection. Alchemists viewed the
creation of the stone as a type of rebirth, where the death of matter led to its eventual
resurrection as something pure and eternal” (p. 87).
3. Alchemical Imagery of Resurrection: Szulakowska delves into the visual culture of
alchemical resurrection, focusing on emblematic images from texts such as Michael
Maier’s Atalanta Fugiens (1617) and Heinrich Khunrath’s Amphitheatrum Sapientiae
Aeternae (1595). In these works, the process of resurrection is often depicted as a series
of alchemical operations, where matter is broken down, putrefied, and eventually
resurrected in a purified form. These images frequently feature Christ or the
philosopher’s stone as the central figure of the resurrection process.
In Maier’s Atalanta Fugiens, for example, the philosopher’s stone is depicted as a child
being born from the putrefaction of matter, symbolizing the rebirth of both matter and
soul. Szulakowska writes: “In Maier’s emblems, the philosopher’s stone is portrayed as a
resurrected child, emerging from the death and decay of base matter. This imagery
emphasizes the alchemical belief that resurrection was the key to both material and
spiritual transformation” (p. 90).
4. The Role of Putrefaction in Resurrection: A key theme in this chapter is the role of
putrefaction—the breaking down of matter—as a necessary precursor to resurrection.
Szulakowska explains that in alchemical terms, putrefaction was seen as a form of
death, where base matter was dissolved in order to be reborn in a more perfected form.
This process of death and rebirth mirrored the Christian narrative of Christ’s death and
resurrection, where the body was broken down in order to be resurrected into eternal life.
She writes: “For alchemists, putrefaction was an essential part of the process of
resurrection. Just as Christ’s body had to die in order to be resurrected, so too must
base matter undergo putrefaction in order to be transformed into gold. This cycle of
death and rebirth was central to the alchemical process” (p. 93).
5. The Spiritualization of the Body: Szulakowska also explores how the resurrection of
matter in alchemical thought was closely tied to the spiritualization of the body.
Alchemists believed that the philosopher’s stone represented not only the perfection of

[Page 61]
matter but also the perfection of the soul, which had been purified through the
alchemical process. The resurrection of the body thus symbolized the alchemist’s own
spiritual rebirth, where the body and soul were unified in a state of perfection.
She explains: “The resurrection of the body in alchemical terms was not just a physical
transformation, but also a spiritual one. The philosopher’s stone represented the
alchemist’s own spiritual resurrection, where the body and soul were purified and unified
in a state of perfection” (p. 95).
Methodological Approach in Chapter 4:
Szulakowska continues her use of a multidisciplinary approach, combining visual and textual
analysis of alchemical texts and illustrations with a broader historical and theological
perspective. She focuses on emblem books like Maier’s Atalanta Fugiens and Khunrath’s
Amphitheatrum Sapientiae Aeternae, as well as treatises that discuss the metaphor of
resurrection in relation to the philosopher’s stone.
Her analysis places these alchemical works in dialogue with Christian eschatology, particularly
the Protestant belief in the resurrection of the body and the salvation of the soul. Szulakowska
shows how alchemists adapted these religious ideas to their own esoteric practices, using the
language and imagery of resurrection to describe both the material and spiritual transformations
they sought to achieve.
