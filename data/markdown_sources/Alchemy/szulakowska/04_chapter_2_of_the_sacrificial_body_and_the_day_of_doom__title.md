# Szulakowska

## Section: Chapter 2 of The Sacrificial Body and the Day of Doom, titled The Alchemical Euc

Chapter 2 of The Sacrificial Body and the Day of Doom, titled The Alchemical Eucharist,
explores how the Eucharist, a central sacrament in Christian theology, was symbolically
reinterpreted by alchemists during the Protestant Reformation. Szulakowska argues that the
transformation of the Eucharist—specifically, its symbolic reinterpretation as part of the
alchemical process—reflects broader theological debates within Protestantism, particularly
regarding the nature of Christ’s body and the meaning of transubstantiation.
Main Argument of Chapter 2:
Szulakowska’s central argument is that alchemical practices during the Reformation reimagined
the Eucharist as an alchemical transmutation, emphasizing the spiritual rather than the material
presence of Christ in the sacrament. She claims that for alchemists like Heinrich Khunrath and
Robert Fludd, the Eucharist symbolized the philosopher's stone, a substance that could
transform the soul just as Christ’s body was believed to transform bread and wine into spiritual
nourishment. This reimagining of the Eucharist aligns with Protestant critiques of Catholic
sacramental theology, which focused on the symbolic and spiritual nature of Christ’s presence
rather than its literal manifestation.
Key Themes in Chapter 2:
1. Eucharistic Symbolism and Alchemy: Szulakowska explains that alchemists in the
late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries viewed the Eucharist as a powerful
metaphor for their own practices. In traditional Catholic doctrine, the Eucharist involves
the literal transformation of bread and wine into the body and blood of Christ
(transubstantiation). Protestants, however, rejected this literal interpretation, instead
emphasizing the symbolic and spiritual transformation that the Eucharist represented.
Alchemists extended this symbolic reading to include their own work of transforming
base matter into spiritual gold.
Szulakowska writes: “For Khunrath and his contemporaries, the Eucharist was a type of
spiritual alchemy, in which the soul was purified and prepared for resurrection. The
consumption of Christ’s spiritual body was akin to the consumption of the philosopher’s
stone, which purified and perfected the alchemist” (p. 41).
2. The Role of Christ as the Philosopher’s Stone: Alchemists like Khunrath saw Christ
as analogous to the philosopher’s stone, the ultimate substance that could transmute not
only metals but also the human soul. In this sense, the Eucharist became a symbol for
the alchemical work itself, where Christ’s body represented the ultimate substance that
could transform base humanity into something divine.

[Page 55]
Khunrath’s Amphitheatrum Sapientiae Aeternae (1595) includes a detailed depiction of
the relationship between Christ’s body, the Eucharist, and the philosopher’s stone.
According to Szulakowska, Khunrath’s work “portrayed Christ as both the divine
alchemist and the philosopher’s stone, emphasizing that the spiritual transformation
experienced through the Eucharist was mirrored by the alchemical process” (p. 42).
3. Alchemy and the Reformation Debates on the Eucharist: Szulakowska situates the
alchemical reinterpretation of the Eucharist within the larger theological debates of the
Protestant Reformation. Protestants, particularly those influenced by Zwingli and Calvin,
rejected the Catholic doctrine of transubstantiation and instead proposed that the
Eucharist was a purely symbolic act. Alchemists aligned themselves with these
theological positions, arguing that the real transformation of the Eucharist was not in the
bread and wine but in the soul of the believer.
She notes: “The alchemists’ rejection of literal transubstantiation was consistent with the
Protestant view that Christ’s body was present only in spirit, not in substance. In their
view, the real transformation was not in the physical elements of the Eucharist but in the
spiritual and alchemical transformation that it symbolized” (p. 45).
4. The Alchemical Mass: Szulakowska also explores the concept of the “alchemical
Mass,” a ritualized process in which the alchemist’s work mirrors the stages of the
Eucharist. In alchemical texts, the Mass is often depicted as a series of steps that
parallel the alchemical process of purification, putrefaction, and resurrection. The
alchemical Mass thus becomes a metaphor for the alchemical transformation, where the
alchemist, like the priest, performs a sacred ritual of transformation.
She discusses Michael Maier’s Symbola Aureae Mensae Duodecim Nationum (1617),
which describes the alchemical process as a type of Mass: “Maier’s depiction of the
alchemical Mass suggests that the work of the alchemist was not merely a scientific
pursuit but a sacred rite, paralleling the work of Christ in the Eucharist. The alchemist’s
laboratory became a type of altar, where the transmutation of matter was a spiritual as
well as a physical act” (p. 48).
5. The Spiritualization of Matter: Szulakowska emphasizes that for these alchemists,
matter was not merely physical but had a spiritual dimension. The transformation of base
matter into gold was symbolic of the transformation of the soul, and the philosopher’s
stone represented the culmination of this process. This spiritualization of matter was
central to the alchemical reinterpretation of the Eucharist, where the bread and wine
were seen as symbols of the spiritual work taking place within the believer.
She writes: “For the alchemists, the transformation of the Eucharistic elements into
Christ’s body and blood was symbolic of the spiritual work that was happening within the
soul of the believer. The alchemical transformation of lead into gold was thus analogous
to the spiritual transformation of the believer, with the philosopher’s stone serving as a
symbol of this process” (p. 49).
Methodological Approach in Chapter 2:
Szulakowska combines visual analysis with theological and historical scholarship to explore the
intersection of alchemical symbolism and Eucharistic theology. She examines key alchemical

[Page 56]
texts and illustrations, such as Khunrath’s Amphitheatrum and Maier’s Symbola Aureae
Mensae, to show how alchemists employed Eucharistic imagery in their work. Her analysis is
grounded in the broader religious and theological debates of the Protestant Reformation,
particularly those concerning the nature of the Eucharist and the role of Christ’s body in
salvation.
Szulakowska also draws on secondary literature concerning Reformation theology, particularly
the writings of Martin Luther, Zwingli, and Calvin, to situate alchemical thought within the larger
context of Protestant critiques of Catholic sacramentalism. She traces how these theological
debates influenced alchemical practices and how alchemists reimagined the Eucharist as a
metaphor for their own spiritual and scientific work.
