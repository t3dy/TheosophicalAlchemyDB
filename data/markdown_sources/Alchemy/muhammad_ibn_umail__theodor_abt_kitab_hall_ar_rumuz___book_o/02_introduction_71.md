Fig. 23: The method of amplification of a picture or a dream according to C. G. Jung.
A cluster of amplifications (©) and personal associations (0) clarifies the different
symbols (1-4) of a dream or a picture, and makes its central meaning (5) visible.123
Looking at Ibn Umairs writings, we can see that he applies a similar
procedure. As he points out in our text later, the sages «did not coin examples
or descriptions except in order to point to their hidden stone. They did not
coin them just for fun or for amusement».123 124 And, as he explains in his
al-Mä; al-waraqT, «They point (by all their analogies and names) to their
stone, and in so doing indicate its operation, tastes, colours and such other
things which appear from it in the course of its changes.»125 Ibn Umail also
quotes such well-known authors as Hermes, Zosimos, Ostanes and Maria
the Hebrew to explain his experiences with the stone, and to indicate the
agreement of former masters with his own statements. In so doing, he
follows the tradition of the great masters of the art, namely to reconnect his
personal experience with the similar experience of earlier alchemists.126

72 Commentary on the Hall ar-rumuz
Text: (3.19) I asked God the Exalted the Praised for guidance and I
brought together in this book what had hitherto been dispersed, explaining
it in an unambiguous and clear manner, in order that it might become a
yardstick and a sign for the seekers of this art, and so that other texts can be
compared with it, and one can know from it the thing that is symbolized.
Thus it brings together the name and the thing named, making it easy for the
seeker to understand it, and fathom the secrets of what is symbolized. (5.6)
Here we learn that this Hall ar-rumüz is a summary of what Ibn
Umail was able to collect and understand from the writings of the sages
before him. He promises that «this yardstick and sign» would lead to the
knowledge of the symbolized thing, the stone of the sages.
Text: (5.6) Thus [this book is for] whoever loves this ail passionately
and has patience for the bitterness of the love and the passion, and continues
to think about how he can reach his beloved, and who is not overcome
either by impatience, bitterness, denial or fatigue. He must persevere with
the conditions imposed in order that the Almighty enables him to attain
what He decreed for him in His ancient knowledge. His success would
come after suffering, difficulty, exhaustion and desasters, in order that the
Almighty Creator can make him fully aware of the rank of the secret that
leads to Him. Thus he would embrace it eagerly, carrying out its necessities,
assigning to each one its appropriate degrees and ranks. Then God would
prepare him to be among those favoured with wisdom, recipients of abun­
dant benefit. Through this he would obtain happiness in both worlds (this
world and the next). (5.19)
In an exceptionally clear way, Ibn Umail places love and its painful
inflictions in the centre of the work. The key to success is clearly the love
for the alchemical work, thanks to which the alchemist can reach his
beloved—none other than God. Ibn Umail's al-Qasïda al-mïmïya, also
starts in the same way: «0 you seeker, lover of this science, these are the
roots of the science if you have knowledge.» In his explanation (sarh) to
this line of his poem, Ibn Umail writes: «It is a speech for whoever loves
the work and is passionate about it. Therefore I made its roots clear for
him, in order that he knows well.»127 The inexplicable and bewildering
pain that has to be endured for love’s sake will lead, as he emphasizes, to
Al-Qasïda al-mïmïya, fol.la; C. G. Jung wrote about this love for the work in a similar
127
way in his Mysterium coniunctionis, in the part on Regina, § 208 f.
