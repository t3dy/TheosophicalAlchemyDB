The stone's mysterious power to unite opposites or, in other
words, to attract different substances and bodies to each other, is something
that transcends human understanding. Therefore, this mutual attraction was
or is considered by many civilizations to be a divine power possessed by a
Goddess of Love or by Mother Nature. It is this mysterious attraction of
matter or bodies in whatever form which fascinated the alchemists, so that
they were engrossed in their great work, hoping to find the origin of this
magic attraction, searching like Faust for «was die Welt im Innersten
zusammenhält» (what keeps the world in its core together).
We can better understand Ibn Umail’s treatise about the mysterious
stone in the light of the general evolution of life. Looking specifically at
human evolution, protection and wariness of almighty, nourishing, but also
threatening Mother Nature was crucial for our ancestors. All over the
world, caves, clothes, vessels, settlements, agriculture, and finally towns,
were important steps in the development of human culture, all aiming to
become more and more independent of the whims of Mother Nature. Hand
in hand with this outer liberation there emerged the miracle of the creation
of consciousness, which is an increasing self-awareness on the part of
human beings, centred on the realization that “I am”. This was paired with
a developing will power and an intellectual ability that allowed an individual
to say ‘No’ to Mother Nature. This created a feeling of freedom from what
we today call inner nature, which was, however, not experienced as being
any different from outer nature because those two worlds were not yet
separated. What we today refer to as inner-psychic complexes were seen as
fateful, divine or daemonic powers in a world of which humans were a
part. As a way of consolidating the slowly increasing independence from
Mother Nature, different religious systems emerged, enabling humans to
live at a certain distance from her and in freedom.
In the western world, the culmination of this development can be
seen in the Judaeo-Christian-Islamic religions with their rejection of feminine
attractiveness as devilish seductiveness, which only distracts mankind
from higher cultural development. In Hinduism and Buddhism as well,
the striving for freedom from the opposites for the sake of enlightenment
led to an exaltation of spirituality, with a corresponding rejection of the
entanglements of bodily drives and pleasures. In the western Book
Religions mentioned above, this liberation from Mother Nature culminated

The pivotal point in human evolution was the moment when 427
human beings started to draw a picture or to sculpt a statue of some
“fascinating and threatening other” that was stronger than they were.
Today we refer to this other as daemonic or divine. In this act of creating
a representation of this unknown “fascinating and threatening other”,
humans started to actually contain some of it, and became able to reflect on
its nature. We can call this time the dawn of the human spirit. Over a period
of time such representations became consolidated in religious systems,
permitting individuals and societies to contain and to relate to these
frightening, fateful forces, thus gradually humanizing them. Often the
unity of the many gods as a single manifestation of the “fascinating and
threatening other” was recognized, thereby retaining a balanced paradoxical
truth of “the One” and “the Many”.623 For the purpose of further reinforcing
“the one light principle of consciousness”, the great monotheistic Book
Religions rejected “the dark Many” in the figure of Satan, thereby establishing
a clearly defined and consolidated monotheistic frame. The price for this
pure and clean image of the divine was the exclusion of all its unacceptable
‘dirty’ sides. In the western world this culminated in the collective repression
of the dangerously seductive hell-fire of Satan.

428 Western alchemy has its origin in Hellenistic-Roman times in
Egypt. That was the time when Judaeo-Christian monotheism took root in
this country along with the aforementioned rejection of the lower world
with its carnal desires. The alchemical work began, as we saw, right there
with what was rejected. This was done by accepting and looking at the
reality of this repressed devilish-divine fire that can be experienced, as we
have seen, in an impossible love, or in any enigmatic bodily urge. Ibn
Elmail named this origin or prima materia of the work the black earth, that
is the realm of the rejected devil or feminine principle as it manifests itself
in unorthodox lower carnal desires. Also in the view of Islam this prima
materia is of course dangerously attractive, hence most feared and
accordingly rejected. Yet it is precisely out of this realm of the black earth,
which is a symbol for the individual body of the adept, that the stone of the
sages has to be created. By accepting, containing, and looking at these
bodily cravings, the adept can observe the inner symbolic images that
accompany these drives. The alchemists named these images divine water,
and by his or her continuous pondering over these symbols, the adept was
able to transform this divine water into a beneficial remedy. This process
allowed the preconscious paradoxical androgynous kernel, which consists
of drive and drive-regulation in the depth of the psyche, to be brought to
conscious realization. Every such union of a drive with its immanent
regulating image—which gives it direction and meaning—makes another
part of this inner centre conscious. Modern depth psychology names this
psychic centre the self.

what Ibn Umail described at the end of the Hall ar-rumüz as the image of the

the mysterious unity of the outer and the inner world, or of psyche and matter.

The alchemical work starts by looking at and finally accepting the
prima materia, which is a symbol of the unacceptable “fascinating and
threatening other” that is incompatible with one’s world-view. The encounter
and continuous maintaining of the tension between the inner and the outer
world is the beginning of a process that can make the individual whole
again. Jung recognized in this procedure the process of individuation that he
experienced himself during his mid-life crisis. Only later, many years after
his own encounter with the unconscious, did he discover in the descriptions
of the alchemical work that the old masters were speaking of the same
thing that he had experienced and worked out with painstaking care in his
so-called Red Book. But as he never wanted to publish this personal
book, which he considered a record of his personal encounter with the

seen in the picture series of C. G. Jung’s Red Book. What is in itself whole,
yet not identical with the ego, can also reconcile opposites around it. It will
be up to future historians to assess what it meant for the evolution of
mankind that Jung was able to bring this basic insight of symbolic alchemy
