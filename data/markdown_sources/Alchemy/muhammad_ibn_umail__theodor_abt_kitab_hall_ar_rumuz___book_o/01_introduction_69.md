Whenever something from the deeper or collective layers of the uncon­
scious is constellated, it manifests itself in the human psyche in the form of
symbols. The relationship between symbol and archetype can be illustrated
as seen in figure 22. Symbolic alchemy seeks to express in symbols from
the material world what an individual author has experienced, and what can
be described only by way of symbols. In his Hall ar-rumüz, Ibn Umail
explains various alchemical symbols, such as lead-copper, magnesia, salt,
snail, wool, and ferment. As we read in his text later, they all point to their
central goal, «their hidden stone».115
The misunderstanding of the nature of symbols has prevented many 66
researchers from understanding symbolic-religious alchemy. To give a
recent example, we can read in the book called Alchemie, Lexikon einer
hermetischen Wissenschaft 116 under the keyword symbol: «Ein Symbol
steht generell stellvertretend für einen Gegenstand oder einen Begriff. Ein
Symbol kann ein Zeichen, ein Wort, ein Bild oder ein Gegenstand sein.»117
(Generally a symbol is a substitute for an object or a term. A symbol can
be a sign, a word, a picture or an object.) As long as historians of alchemy
continue to take symbols for signs and vice versa in such an unscientific
way, they will remain unable to understand the value of symbols, and will
continue to try to grasp their meaning in a purely reductive way. In doing
so they simply neglect the repeated advice of all the great authorities of
alchemy to ponder over a symbol which is not just a sign but is rather
pointing—I repeat—to something hitherto really unknown.
Like other alchemists before him, Ibn Umail tries to explain the 67
different symbols ( Arabic: ramz, pi. rumüz) by connecting them to other
symbols, related motifs, or parables, and by referring to the books of the
sages who preceeded him. Abü al-Qâsim al-cIrâqï, called as-SimâwI, a
later alchemist of the 13th century, explains in detail this method of under­
standing «the inner meaning of the symbols and allusions of the sages». As
a central point he declares that we need «necessary associations» (in

the eyes of the seeker. Ibn Umail compares this to a rising sun, symbol for
the creation of a new consciousness that is again in tune with the whole
inner pattern.
The value of this method of collecting «necessary associations»
in order to explain (Arabic: halla) the inner meaning of symbols was
rediscovered by C. G. Jung when he attempted to understand the dreams
of people working with him. He called this the method of amplification.122
He assumed that every dream image points to something unknown. In
order that this unknown might become understandable he collected—in the
way recommended by Abü al-Qäsim—necessary associations and parallel
images around each motif. The ensuing cluster of images points to a
common denominator behind the multiple images, as has been corrobo­
rated in intervening decades of empirical research in dream and picture
interpretation.
