4 The Works of Bereshit and Merkavah
Kabbalah is generally presented as having two main preoccupations: Ma ʾaseh
Bereshit (Work of Creation), based on the exegesis of Genesis 1 and 2, and
Ma ʾaseh Merkavah (Work of the Chariot), visions and reflections concern-
ing the Throne on its Chariot described in the first chapter of Ezekiel, that is,
respectively, the realms of cosmogony and theosophy.26 These two streams of
speculation are seen as complementary, in the belief that ‘to know the stages
of the creative process is also to know the stages of one’s own return to the root
of all existence.’27 Kabbalists, like their more orthodox rabbinic co-religionists,
drew their inspiration from Holy Scripture, although their approach involved
a revolutionary reinterpretation of its content, uncovering new theosophi-
cal and mystical levels of meaning in each verse.28 Every biblical noun in the
Torah became a symbol for one of the emanations of the Godhead and every
verb described their dynamic interplay;29 it being the Kabbalists’ task to dis-
cover interpretations of the Torah and invent keys for the understanding of
its symbolism.30

5 Zohar: Book of Splendour
From the end of the thirteenth century, the Sepher ha-Zohar (Book of Radiance
or Splendour) became the authoritative text of Jewish mysticism.31 The Zohar
was composed or compiled by the Spanish rabbi Moses ben Shem Tov de Leon
(c.1240–1305), who claimed to be drawing from pre-existing manuscript mate-
rial reaching back to the second-century rabbi Shimon bar Yochai.32 Much
more extensive than its predecessors, the Zohar includes such works as the
Sifra diTzniʾuta (Book of the Hidden), Idra Rabba (Great Assembly), Idra
Zuta (Smaller Assembly), Ra ʾaya Meheimna (Faithful Shepherd) and Midrash
haNeʾelam (Hidden Midrash), with commentaries on the Torah ranging from
the nature of God and the structure of the cosmos, the concept of a primor-
dial man, Adam Kadmon, the emanation of the four worlds of Atziluth, Beriah,
Yetzirah, and Assiah, the nature of souls, redemption, forces of evil (kliphoth),
and how the individual practitioner relates to God and creation.33 The Zohar’s
aim is never to simply provide one authoritative interpretation of scripture,
but instead to promote the idea that there are ‘seventy alternative explana-
tions of the Torah’,34 in fact ‘all the words of the Torah … can all bear several
meanings, and all good, and the whole Torah can be expounded in seventy
ways, corresponding to seventy sides and seventy wings.’35
In the Zohar we find further reformulations of notions of the Sephiroth.

6 Ten Sephiroth on the Tree of Life

7 Giovanni Pico della Mirandola and the Genesis of Christian Cabala
When we turn to Christian interest in Jewish doctrines, it is worth bearing
in mind Joseph Dan’s distinction between Christian Hebraism and Christian
Kabbalism: for the former, the acquisition of ‘a systematic knowledge of the
Hebrew language enables one to understand better the Hebrew bible, and
thus gain new insights into the sources of Christianity’; while for the latter
‘non-biblical Hebrew traditions, or oral traditions of biblical interpretation
preserved by the kabbalists, serve as an independent source demonstrating the
truth of Christianity.’ Dan goes further and argues that ‘interest in the kabbalah
was new … an expression of dissatisfaction with the accepted sources and a
quest for new affirmations of the Christian message.’ He sees this openness
to non-Christian sources as another thread in the Renaissance rediscovery of
antiquity, of the prisca theologia (ancient theology), valuing material attrib-
uted to Zoroaster and Hermes, Pythagoras and Orpheus, the Chaldean Oracles
and Egyptian hieroglyphs.56
Various individuals have been proposed as responsible for the birth of a
specifically Christian Cabala.57 The oldest record of a conversion to Christi-
anity due to kabbalistic interpretation of scripture is found in Abulafia’s Gan
Na ʾul (Sealed Garden), where he mentions a group of disciples who converted
to Catholicism.58 Two early candidates are the conversos Raymund Martini
(1220–1285), whose Pugio Fidei (Dagger of Faith) contains Christological spec-
ulations contributing to the notion of a Christian appropriation of Kabbalah,

10 Notarikon/Notariacon
The technique of Notarikon, or the ‘manipulation of letters’ was essentially a
technique making use of acrostics or acronyms, on the assumption that pas-
sages in the Torah could be interpreted by taking single letters from a string
of words to create new words, or, vice versa, that the individual letters of a
single word formed a chain of the initials of different words. The letters con-
cerned were sometimes indicated by a mark placed above them; hence the
term Notarikon ‘from the marks or notaria on the top of each letter,’ similar to
the practice of notaries and actuaries.95 One example of Notarikon popular
in Christian-Cabalist works, including Agrippa’s De Occulta Philosophia, is an

11 Temurah or Tziruf
Temurah, ‘permutation’, ‘commutation’, or ‘transposition’ of letters, also called
Tziruf, ‘combination’ or ‘metathesis,’ is when ‘a letter may be posited to stand
for another letter through the alphabetical circle’, such that ‘one word writ-
ten with one set of letters symbolically designates another word composed of
different letters.’103 This derives from a practice found in the Sepher Yetzirah,
where new alphabets are created from permutations of the standard one. As
there are 22 letters in the Hebrew alphabet, there are consequently 22 varia-
tions or ‘revolutions of the alphabet.’ A great deal of importance was placed
in this technique for the discovery of divine and angelic names. So, for exam-
ple, with the 22nd alphabet permutation known as שבתא (Athbash), where the
first letter of the standard alphabet א (Aleph) is substituted by the last letter
ת (Tau), the second letter ב (Beth), by the penultimate letter ש (Shin) and so
on, the divine name הוהי (YHVH), Yahweh, is transformed into another divine
name, the vox mystica or magica ץפצמ (MSPS), Mazpaz, complete with its own
mystical meanings.104

that his main sources for Christian Cabala are his fellow Christians, Reuchlin,
Agrippa, and Dee. As he quotes from Pico’s Conclusions on the Orphic Hymns

Pico della Mirandola’, it seems reasonable to assume he was also aware of
Pico’s Cabalistic ideas.207 The mention of a ‘Scalichius’ on the same page
