Khunrath’s choice to present this androgynous figure of Adam-Eve here
could have been influenced by ‘one of the most read books in Europe dur-
ing the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries’, Leone Ebreo’s Dialoghi d’amore
(Dialogues of Love), which were included in Pistorius’s cabalistic anthology.680
His third and most elevated dialogue, ‘On the Origin of Love’, is the culmi-
nation of a journey which starts with Aristotle’s physics but ‘ends in a meta-
physics formulated as a Platonic revelation of Holy Scripture’.681 Part of this
dialogue includes a myth shared by Plato and the Bible, that of the androgyne,
with Aristophanes’ account in the Symposium being confirmed in the book
of Genesis.682 Leone depicts a ‘dualistic cosmos governed by a system that is
universally hermaphrodite’, with the heavens male and the earth female.683
In the Zohar, ‘Adam Kadmon reflects the gender bipolarity of the Godhead’
(which includes the female aspect of the Shekhinah). This macrocosmic for-
mulation is likewise reflected in the microcosm of Adam-Eve, although, as
Rossella Pescatori notes, ‘paradoxically, the separation of genders is the ful-
fillment of creation’.684 The two protagonists of Leone’s dialogues, male Philo
and female Sophia, represent the two halves of the androgyne, whereas in
Khunrath’s Amphitheatre we have Christ as Sophia and Adam-Androgyne as
the Theosopher in love with divine wisdom.
Rather than simply focusing on the beginning of the world and the creation
of Adam and Eve, Leah DeVun’s research provides another intriguing possibil-
ity, especially in light of Khunrath’s emphasis on regeneration and rebirth: the
notion, found in some medieval theologians, that all post-resurrection bodies

God is expressed in the Amphitheatre not only in terms of a metaphysics of fire
and light but also in the language of Pythagorean symbolic number, something
that Reuchlin intimately connects with Cabala.725 The most notable instance
of Khunrath’s engagement with such arithmosophy is found precisely in the
Amphitheatre’s engraving of the androgynous Adam-Protoplast. There, in the
outer ring, encompassing the Grades of Cognition and the Ladder of Union,
we read:
LET THE BINARY BE REJECTED, AND THE TERNARY BE REDUCED, BY
MEANS OF THE QUATERNARY, TO THE SIMPLICITY OF THE MONAD.726
This is, in many ways, the main focus of the engraving, with the androgyne a
slightly paradoxical figure, representing at one and the same time the ‘Binarius’
as the duality to be rejected, and the sought-for unity of the ‘Monad’.727
