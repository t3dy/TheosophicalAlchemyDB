48 Conclusion
Looking back at the final stages of the Grades of Cognition, Intellectus and Mens,
it is easy to imagine that Ehregott Daniel Colberg might have had Khunrath’s
engraving in mind when formulating his condemnation of practices aiming
for ‘knowledge of God according to supra-mental unition, that is super-mental
conjunction and super-intellectual union’.1103 In the Amphitheatre’s second cir-
cular figure Khunrath set forth a complex combination of ideas that must have
been a source of inspiration for those interested in developing their own the-
osophical systems of hermetic, cabalistic and magical practice, but were evi-
dently highly problematic for more orthodox religious thinkers like Libavius
and Colberg.
Examining Khunrath’s theosophy we begin to see that he aims to embrace
and subsume multiple philosophical categories for religious ends, not only
proposing theories about the nature of physical and spiritual being but also
promoting practices for the perfection of both, as epitomised by the theosoph-
ical ambition to attain and obtain the Sons of the Micro- and Macro-cosm,
the two Stones, of Wisdom and of the Wise, giving rise not only to the health,
wealth and glory of Wisdom from Proverbs 3:16,1104 but far more importantly
to well-being of the Soul.1105
In the Amphitheatre, Khunrath presents his two ways of Prudence and
Understanding.1106 Ascending on the Ladder of Conjunction and Union, the
Theosopher’s ultimate aim is to unite with the divine. Modelling his life on

49 Ascent and Descent
We have already looked at the seventeenth-century French translation and
commentary on the Amphitheatre by Claude de Bourges, where he wrote of
the necessity of climbing the ladder of conjunction and union and then of
descending by the grades of cognition, with the former taking us to ‘indu-
bitability’ and the latter to ‘perfection’, ‘the one in the oratory, the other in
the laboratory’.1108
De Bourges is a careful, attentive reader, noting that, contrary to expecta-
tions, when writing of the way of understanding, Khunrath does not speak of
ascending through the Grades of Cognition, in the Aristotelian sense of acquir-
ing epistemic knowledge arising from the senses’ awareness of the external
objects of creation, this knowledge then rising from the faculties of the sensi-
tive soul to those of the intellective soul. Instead, Khunrath writes of ‘descend-
ing by the ten grades of Cognition’.1109 This sounds more like the consequences
of Hermes’s encounter with Pimander in the Corpus Hermeticum, when the
human mind experiences gnosis, ‘direct and unmediated, supra-rational, sal-
vational access to the supreme spiritual level of reality’,1110 with the adept then
having the task of climbing down the branches of the Tree of Life, as it were, of
bringing the fruits of this transformed awareness of reality back down to earth
and everyday life. As in the Hermetic Emerald Tablet, there is an ascent from

Notwithstanding Libavius’s disapproval of the Amphitheatre, it is impossible
to ignore the impact that it had on more radical Paracelsian, Weigelian, and
Rosicrucian thinkers in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. As
Gilly quite rightly observes, it is thanks to Khunrath and his books that the
terms ‘Theosophy’ and ‘Theosophers’ spread in such a relatively short period
of time amongst both supporters and opposition.1113
Although Gilly is inclined to dismiss Khunrath’s use of the newly-coined,
hyphenated, compound terms, Christiano-Kabalisticum, Divino-Magicum
and Physico-Chymicum as ‘pompous word combinations’ – rather than as
sincere attempts to convey the specificity of his intentions and the hybridity
of his practice – he nevertheless praises the Amphitheatre for its ‘truly coher-
ent sophiology of God, nature and man, together forming a quintessential
combination of most of the elements of Renaissance Hermetic philosophy,
Christian Kabbalah and Paracelsian-Weigelian world views.’1114 The follow-
ing chapters will now examine in greater detail Khunrath’s three major prac-
tices, his ‘Theosophical Mediators’,1115 ‘Christian Cabala, Divine Magic, and
Physico-Chemistry, whose Triune study is true Catholic THEOSOPHY.’1116

1 The Origins of Christian Cabala
Khunrath’s Christian engagement with the Jewish mystical tradition of
Kabbalah lies at the heart of his theosophy.1 Kabbalistic notions of emana-
tions of the Godhead into the universe and the power contained in divine and
angelic names pervade his philosophical outlook. This chapter begins with
a brief consideration of the main aspects of Jewish Kabbalah and Christian
Cabala preceding Khunrath, most notably in the works of the first Christian
‘phenomenologists of Kabbalah’,2 Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (1463–1494),
Johann Reuchlin (1455–1522) and Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa (1486–1535),
before examining evidence of related ideas in Khunrath’s own works.

The Hebrew term הלבק Kabbalah, which translates as ‘reception’, ‘received
lore’, or ‘tradition’, refers to the Jewish esoteric teaching that emerged in the
High Middle Ages in Provence and Northern Spain,3 though with claims to rev-
elations reaching back to Moses during his encounter with Jehovah on Mount
Sinai, where he received a two-fold revelation, a ‘literal’ written and ‘spiritual’
oral Law, being told ‘These words shalt thou declare, and these shalt thou hide’
(2 Esdras 14:6). The former were the Ten Commandments; the latter secret oral
teachings transmitted from master to disciple over the generations to the elect
few.4 Others believed that God had revealed the names of all things to Adam
in the Garden of Eden, the correct pronunciation of which evoked their true
essence,5 or that Adam had learned Eternal Wisdom from the book held by
Raziel, angel of the highest Mysteries.6
For the Kabbalist, the words of oral and written law formed an enormous
library of mystical symbols speaking of the interaction between the Godhead
as emanator and all that emanated forth. Anticipations of Kabbalah can
be found in earlier material that exerted a profound influence on its devel-
opment, including the Hekhalot or Heavenly Halls literature, dating back to
late antiquity,7 and the earliest extant Hebrew text of speculative thought on
cosmology and cosmogony, the proto-kabbalistic Sepher Yetzirah (Book of

2 Proto-Kabbalah of the Sepher Yetzirah
The most representative of these concepts in the Sepher Yetzirah are the spec-
ulations on the Hebrew alphabet and the Sephiroth.11 The creative powers of
God are seen as being embodied in the 22 Foundation Letters of the Hebrew
alphabet with which the Creator engraved the Divine Names and fashioned the
Universe. Each letter corresponds to a different principle of creation, having its
own distinctive power.12 Each letter and name ‘represents a concentration of
energy and expresses a wealth of meaning which cannot be translated, or not
fully at least, into human language.’13 Language, moreover, is considered not
merely referential, but forms the ‘very fabric of the cosmos; letters, words, and
certain formulations … imbued with creative, transformative, and even soteri-
ological power.’14 By permutating or manipulating these letters, the Kabbalist
can understand and influence created things.15
Connected with this are early references to ten metaphysical principles
called Sephiroth (singular Sephirah) that subsume the archetype of every cre-
ated thing and mediate between God and the universe.16 The term Sephiroth

3 Bahir: Book of Illumination
After the Sepher Yetzirah, the ‘earliest extant text of Kabbala proper’ was the
Sepher ha-Bahir (Book of Illumination or Brilliance),20 first appearing in man-
uscript form in the second half of the twelfth century, composed either by
Isaac the Blind (d. 1235) or one of his school.21 Gershom Scholem considered
it the product of the intermingling of Jewish Gnosticism and Neoplatonism.22
The Bahir presents itself as a series of dialogues between master and disci-
ples, providing commentaries on the first chapters of Genesis, on the hidden
significance of the Hebrew letters, on statements from the Sepher Yetzirah,
and so forth. For the first time, it introduces the concept of the Ilan or divine
tree, with ten branches, a system of emanations, which constitute the divine
pleroma. The Sephiroth had already been introduced in the Sepher Yetzirah,
but it was in the Bahir that they first came to be regarded as divine attributes,
