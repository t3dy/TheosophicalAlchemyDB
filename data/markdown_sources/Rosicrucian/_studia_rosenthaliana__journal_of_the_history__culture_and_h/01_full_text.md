CARLOS GILLY ON SPAIN AND JEWISH BOOKS IN BASLE
EMILE G.L. SCHRIJVER
What happens to a book after it has been sent to a scholarly journal for review? The editor opens the
package and sees whether the book under consideration is in any way fit to be reviewed in his or her
journal. If so, the book is kept for a while in order to find a suitable reviewer, if not so it is ideally sent
back to the publisher. But what happens when a capable reviewer of an extremely learned and
comprehensive book is hard to find? The book remains there were it has been put, well-preserved
between other "difficult" books. But something has to happen, so what does an editor do? After a
few years he decides to give the book another look, and he then gives full freedom to his greed: he
decides to review the book himself, since there is one particular chapter in the book which touches his
area of expertise. This is exactly what happened to the book under review here. It has a chapter
dealing with Jewish bibliography and literature and that is the one I will discuss.
c. GiLLY, Spanien und der Basler Buchdruck bis 1600. Ein Querschnitt durch die spanische Geis tes -
geschichte aus der Sicht einer europäischen Buchdruckerstadt. Basel & Frankfurt am Main, Verlag
Helbing & Lichtenhahn 1985 [= Basler Beiträge zur Geschichtswissenschaft, 151] -xv + 559 p. DM.
95,-. ISBN 3-7190-0909-2.
This book is one of what seems to be a genre. Among others the following works exist (Gilly, p. 2, n.
2): Der Basler Buchdruck als Vermittler italienischen Geistes ; Der Basler Buchdruck und Britannien;
Basle and France in the Sixteenth Century. It is not more than logical that one of the other great
cultures of Late-medieval/Renaissance Europe, Spain, was the next to receive scholarly "Basle"
attention. Dr. Carlos Gilly, who proves himself an incredibly systematic spirit - not particularly
inappropriate for a bibliographer -, did not want to write a bibliography, but a "Querschnitt durch
die spanische Geistesgeschichte aus der Sicht einer europäischen Buchdruckerstadt" and even the
layman can see that he must have succeeded. His book, divided into three main parts (A. Das
Mittelalter: Araber, Juden und Christen; В. Renaissance: С. Reformation), is a real "libro de difícil
clasificación" as N. Fernández Marcos rightly pointed out in his review of the book in Sefarad }
Perhaps it is proper to cite one other review, on which I will, by the way, return at the end of this
review, in order to emphasize once more the fact that this book has a value which reaches beyond the
study of Jewish history and literature. "It is a book of encyclopaedic learning. [. . .] Dr. Gilly's book
gives an extraordinarily good idea of the sixteenth century.
But let us now concentrate on chapter 2, entitled "Die Juden."
The first book the bibliographer would expect to be cited in this chapter is of course the
monumental Die Basler hebräischen Drucke (1492-1866), by J. Prijs (Olten/Freiburgi.B., 1964). But
Gilly is interested in cultural history more than in bibliography, in the technical sense of the word,
and that can be seen immediately. The chapter begins with a general introduction in which Gilly
concentrates on the "Judendekret" of the Basle council of 1434, which included many anti-Jewish
measures. He re-investigates the backgrounds of the anti- Je wish movement in the Basle council,
which is generally considered to be of Spanish origin: two brothers born as Jews, the Spaniards
Alfonso de Cartagena and Gonzalo García de Santa María, adopted in that year the subject of
renewed anti- Jewish measures - converts are commonly considered to be the initiators of late-
medieval anti- Judaism - but in such a way that these really suited the Spanish situation only.
However, according to Gilly there were more obvious reasons for the anti- Je wish decree. He claims

that its motivation simply was one of those informing current questions of the "Reformatio Ec-
clesiae", and that only after the subject had been brought up the Spanish "conversos" managed to
carry through their ideas. But all the same, at least Gonzalo García de Santa Maria's intervention is
indisputable. In 1434 he gave permission to copy in Basle a polemical treatise, Scrutinium scriptura-
rum, written by his father, don Pablo (Paulus) de Santa María, which was directed against the 'Iqarîm
of Joseph Albo. The work in a way attempted to prove the usefulness of converts to Christianity and
the delegates' awareness thereof offers a plausible explanation for the council's interest in the Jewish
question. However, the two Spanish conversos did not succeed: paradoxically Christian anti- Jewish
bellicosity developed into a similarly agressive anti-convert stance.
The cultivation of the Hebrew language in sixteenth-century Basle is Gilly's next subject. As the
most important stimulus for the study of Hebrew among Christians he mentions another work by
Pablo de Santa Maria, 1100 Additiones ad Nicolaum de Lyra , which was a real goldmine for Christian
scholars looking for convincing arguments to convert Jews. Nicolas de Lyra, often called "Rashi's
ape", was a Christian scholar who extensively used Jewish sources for his exegetical works (Rashi's
name is not mentioned by Gilly).
In the light of Gilly's approach, it is perhaps not coincidental that the very first known teacher of
Hebrew in Basle was a Spanish convert, Matthäus Adriani. One of his most important pupils was
Sebastian Münster, an outstanding scholar who, together with Johann Reuchlin, author of e.g. De
rudimentis hebraicis , may be held responsible for the wide-spread dissemination of medieval Jewish
grammatical insights among renaissance and post-renaissance Christian scholars. Real heroes in the
eyes of these scholars were the Spanish grammarians Joseph Qimhi and his two sons Moses and
David. Joseph wrote, among other things, Sefer Zikarôn , in which he introduces the existence of long
and short vowels in Hebrew. Moses' most important work is his Mahalak Sevilê Ha-da'at , which may
be described as a Hebrew textbook avant la lettre - used by Johann Reuchlin, to mention one among
many. David's most important work is of course his Sefer Ha-miklôl , encompassing a grammar
{Heleq Ha-diqdûq) and a dictionary ( Heleq Ha-inyan or Sefer Ha-šoraším). Most of these works
were edited by Münster, who also saw through the press quite a few of Eliah Levita's grammatical
works. The present reviewer is beholden to Gilly for stimulating the foregoing synthesis. One critical
remark, however: the pun mentioned by Gilly (p. 59): "ohne Qimhi keine Tora!" is, for a general
audience, completely incomprehensible without an explanation, which is lacking. The Hebrew
phrase "im ên qimhi ên tôrah " is a clear allusion to Pirqê A vôt 3:17 "/m ên qemah ên tôrah we-f im ên
tôrah ên qemah", "no flour [i.e. bread], no learning; no learning, no flour."
Spanish Jewish Bible commentators were also popular among Christian scholars - suffice it to
mention Moses and David Qimhi and Abraham ibn Ezra - and equally so Spanish Jewish philosop-
hers. As far as the latter are concerned, Gilly concentrates on Johann Buxtorfs (The Younger)
editions and translations of texts written by Maimonides and on the cultural implications of the
so-called Maimonidean controversy: the discussion among Jewish scholars whether it behoved a Jew
to study secular sciences, as Maimonides had in fact always propagated.
Gilly's treatment of talmudic and halakhic literature printed at Basle - his paragraph on this matter
precedes, let it be said, the one on philosophy - is very intriguing. He discusses the influence which
the Italian prohibition of 1559 concerning the printing of the Talmud had on Hebrew printing at
Basle. Plans to print the Talmud in Zürich were already afoot in 1559, directly following the inclusion
of the Talmud in the Italian Index Librorum Prohibitorum , but these never materialized. The many
problems surrounding the printing (by Ambrosius Froben, from 1578 to 1580) of the Basle Talmud
can best be illustrated by citing Prijs's entry (p. 188):
"Der babylonische Talmud ohne den Traktat ' Avôdah Zarah . Mit den Beigaben der Justinia-
nischen Ausgabe (Venedig 1546-51). Unter Zugrundelegung des Texts der Bombergschen
Ausgaben, Venedig 1520-23 und 1526-48 (jedoch mit den von der Zenzur vorgeschriebenen
Änderungen), und unter Benutzung eines Raschi-Manuskripts zum Traktat Pesahîm heraus-
gegeben auf Veranlassung und auf Kosten des Simon Günzburg zur Gemse in Frankfurt a.M.
und seines Onkels Simon Günzburg in Günzburg a.D von Israel Sifroni aus Guastalla."4

published at Basle. Historiographical material is of course well represented there with the first
complete Greek edition (1544) of the works of Flavius Josephus. A rather popular Spanish author
among Basle scholars and printers was Abraham ibn Daud, and his most famous work, Sefer
Ha-qabalah , was printed there several times, both in whole and in part. Another important work
printed at Freiburg im Breisgau by A. Froben of Basle in 1583 was Benjamin of Tudela's famous
itinerary.
In the last part of the chapter here under discussion Gilly says (p. 84): "Wenn wir die Materien-
und Sachregister in J. Prijs' Die Basler hebräischen Drucke durchsehen, so fällt zunächst die fast
vollständige Abwesenheit van naturwissenschaftlichen Werke auf. Nur vier Bücher gehören in
diesen Bereich; [. . .]" He discusses these books, but does not mention the very similar 'vollständige
Abwesenheit' of Hebrew poetry, a pre-eminently Spanish genre.
In his very last paragraph Gilly discusses a few later "Spanish" works which "durch die Basier
Pressen Verbreitung fanden." Among them are Jacob Román's supplement to the second edition of
Buxtorf s Bibliotheca Rabbinica of 1640, and the 1740 reprint of Philippus van Limborch's record of
his written discussion with Isaac Orobio de Castro, entitled De veritate religionis christianae arnica
collatio cum erudito judaeo , originally published at Gouda in 1687. This work is best known because
Van Limborch published the Exemplar humanae vitae of Uriel da Costa as an appendix to this work.5
The Basle re-edition of Van Limborch's work may well be connected with the publication of the
"Opera Omnia" of Hermannus Witsius (by the same printer Johann Rudolph Im-Hoff) and of
Johannes Leusden (printed by the brothers Emanuel & Johann Rudolph (!) Thurneysen; Prijs 291)
at Basle in 1739, both, like Van Limborch, born in the Netherlands.
Four appendices and two indices complete the book: 741 "Spanish editions" printed at Basle
(Anhang 1 ; the table of contents mentions 740) ; 33 editions of which publication at Basle was planned
but did not materialize (Anhang 2) ; 51 editions of works by Spanish Church Fathers (Anhang 3) ; a list
of the manuscripts and archival documents used (Anhang 4 ); an index of abbreviations and a
combined index of names and a bibliographical register. These appendices and indices were prepa-
red, as far as I have been able to control, with great diligence and they give a good insight into the
wide range of subjects treated by Gilly.
In his review, already cited above (n. 2), Alastair Hamilton says: "Its only drawback is its
structure." This is, it has to be admitted, very true as far as the 'Jewish element' is concerned. The
author's unrelenting attention on Spain and its (former) inhabitants has produced a considerable lack
of balance: one simply cannot speak of Nicholas de Lyra without mentioning Rashi, who happens not
to be Spanish. Furthermore, in different vein, one simply cannot make an allusion to Pirqê Avôt
without explaining it as such. "It is a work of encyclopaedic learning", but ass far as the Jewish
element is concerned it is also a work of encyclopaedic limitedness; it is of no use as an overview of
medieval Jewish literature but of course it was never meant as such.
Dr. Carlos Gilly has, however, to an impressive extent, succeeded in placing the Basle editions of
Spanish Jewish authors in a much broader cultural-historical context than has heretofore been done.
Bibliography is often referred to as a science ancillary to historiography. The book here under review
proves that a sound bibliographical basis is a sine qua non for serious historical research. That, to be
sure, is far more important than any niggling criticism.
