Conclusions
Despite the controversial nature of his claims, it can be argued that Grant’s
engagement with elements of Indian spiritual traditions is both substan-
tial and innovative. What needs highlighting is the fact of Grant’s com-
mitment to the principles of esoteric epistemology. More than anything

else, this epistemology is grounded in correlative thinking: it emphasizes
cognitive resonance between the plethora of apparently unrelated phe-
nomena, and in this manner illuminates the concordance between ideo-
logical systems and, ultimately, generates the coherence of the world (cf.
Assmann, 2007: xii). It hardly needs mentioning that this method is nei-
ther scientific nor historical in the conventional and contemporary sense
of these terms, since in its attempt to achieve its cognitive goal it does not
trace the causal chain of relationships between phenomena but instead
strives toward recognition of their mutual links based on analogy, resem-
blance, and sympathy. Grant is aware of the provisional, “constructed,”
nature of this endeavor, and it should be obvious that his understand-
ing of the subject matter is informed by his familiarity with Hindu and
Buddhist teachings that highlight the centrality of consciousness as the
primary factor and substance in the fabric of reality:
A percept, a concept, or a number, any object in fact, has no real rela-
tionship with any other percept, concept, or number. Relationship obtains
only in the consciousness of the perceiver, the consciousness which is the
background upon which all objects appear as images on the screen. There
can therefore be no association of ideas, no correspondences of any kind,
between numbers or ideas which they represent, except in the conscious-
ness of their subject, because no thing exists as an objective entity. (1994:
158)85
The most tenuous of Grant’s comparative statements is the one that argues
the connection between Indian Tantra and the supposed Draconian tra-
dition with its alleged roots in predynastic Egypt. The weakness consists
in what appears as Grant’s attempt to explain this connection in histori-
cal terms. If this is indeed the intention behind his assertions, it must be
stated unequivocally that they are at best highly speculative, as they lack
any solid historical evidence that would support them. One has to keep
in mind, however, that esoteric discourse differs in its methodology, and
in its truth claims, from academic and scientific discourse, and for that
reason Grant’s claims are best taken with a grain of salt and not literally.
Grant’s engagement with the subjects of nonduality and emptiness,
understood as characteristics of ultimate reality, is original. Grant sug-
gests that the impulse emanating from the void heart of reality mani-
fests in the guise of destructive and repulsive gods. The force responsible
for this masquerading lies with the defense mechanism of the ego in its
attempt to protect itself from the forces that are not destructive as such
but only of this same ego’s misdirected grip on the illusion of a separate

individual existence. There is a close parallel between Grant’s interpreta-
tion of this type of dynamics and the Vajrayāna Buddhist teaching that
explains the reasons for the wrathful appearance of some Buddhas and
Bodhisattvas, and the Hindu notions behind the furious imagery asso-

frightening is so only to the deluded and egotistic perception. Similarly,
in his interpretation of the states of consciousness, Grant remains true
to the traditional Indian teachings, and at the same time he is applying

The physical manifestation of Consciousness is Light. Its metaphysical
reality is the LVX of the Gnostics, the Jnâna of the Advaitins, the Buddha
of the Madhyamikas, the Ain Soph Aur of the Qabalists. (1994: 187)
In Grant’s exposition of Tantra and sexual magick, two elements deserve
to be singled out. First of all, there is a line of mutual correspondence
between the theory and practice related to the consummation of the sex-
ual fluids as portrayed by Grant and as described in some of the impor-
tant Hindu and Buddhist tantras.86 In that respect, his exposition of these
doctrines may be considered to be informed by, and aligned with, the
genuine currents of Tantra. And secondly, by his privileging of the role
of woman, her body, and the properties of her sexual fluids, and espe-
cially by glorifying what traditional patriarchal cultures often regarded
as impurity, namely the fact of woman’s menstruation, Grant may be
considered a contributor toward the transformation of the hegemonic
masculinity87 and as a consequence, and if only for that, he deserves
credit, just as Western Tantra itself “deserves to be taken seriously as
a cultural development within Western society” (Samuel, 2008: 231).
In conclusion, Grant is interesting and important because he has con-
structed and projected an alternative model of the nature and purpose
of human e xistence—including alternative notions about the human
body and sexuality—and in framing that model he was to a significant
degree informed and inspired by his understanding of Eastern, primarily
Indian, spiritual traditions.

The English Paramahamsa
His Holiness Shri Gurudev Dadaji Mahendranath (1911–1991), the
“English Paramahamsa,” was born in London as Lawrence Amos
Miles.1 Much later, as an old man, he reflected on and summarized
the vagaries of his unusual fate with a measure of stoical detachment:
“I was born to this life and my birth [I Ching] hexagram is The Wan-
derer . . . A pagan magician but with never any home to call my own; for
such is the life of one who beholds the cosmos in motion” (Mahendra-
nath, n.d.: 134). While growing up, the imaginative boy came to believe
that his domestic garden was populated by supernatural beings, “gob-
lins, fairies, gnomes, trolls, pixeys and elves” (40) whom he preferred
as playing companions to mechanical toys. When he was 11 years old,
he received his first “pagan initiation” from his great-great-aunt; the
ceremony took place in her cottage in Sussex (4). It is reasonable to
assume that such early experiences predisposed Miles to search for the
meaning of life through alternative paths.
Miles claims to have met and befriended the aging Aleister Crowley
in the midst of the latter’s court trial against the artist Nina Hamnett,
whom he unsuccessfully sued for libel in 1934. According to his later
reminiscences, Miles was as much disillusioned in the English justice,
“and how easily it could turn round and display its rear end” (Mahen-
dranath, n.d.: 39), as he was impressed by the Mage: “Aleister Crowley

was probably the most far out and advanced thinker at the time but as
well as being a natural born magician he possessed a knowledge of yoga
and I Ching which was superior to that of any other European” (40).
Crowley suggested to him that he should study the wisdom of the East in
situ. However, it took some time before Miles finally managed to arrange
his affairs and move to India. In the meantime, he became a member
of the International Brigade fighting against Franco’s fascist regime in
Spain and later, during World War II, as a soldier in Cairo, Egypt. There
he met with a group of French occultists, one of whom was the famous
Traditionalist René Guénon, who obviously did not leave a commend-
able impression on Miles: “I knew this at the time and remember won-
dering why he did not change his surname as it was shared by a species
of grizzly-furred monkeys” (18).
In 1953, Miles finally reached India, arriving in Bombay (now
Mumbai) on a passenger steamer. Immediately upon his arrival, he
met his first guru, Shri Lokanatha Avadhoot Maharaj. In the eve-
ning of the same day, Miles was given initiation into the Ādi Nāth
Sampradāya, a subsect of the Order of the Nāth panth yogis (102).
“The succession Rites of the Adi-Nath Sampradaya have been passed
on to Sir Michael Magee who was given the initiation name of Shri
Lokanath Maharaj in honour of [Dadaji’s] first Indian guru” (4). At a
later date, he was initiated into the tantric Uttara Kaula tradition by
Shri Pagala Baba of Ranchi, Bihar, who was at the time “recognized as
the last surviving Kaula and authentic Tantrik Guru in India” (4). This
line of succession was passed on to John Power, also known as Shri
Vilasanath Maharaj, who “now has the leadership of the Tantrik Order
in the West and anywhere else it wishes to take root” (4). This was
deemed necessary because India was at the present historical moment
perceived to be “dominated by Puritan concepts” (4). Besides these,
Miles also received other initiations, “but which are regarded as being
of lesser importance,” into Vajrayāna, Soto Zen, Taoism, Ch’an, and
Theravāda Buddhism (5).
Mahendranath Dadaji—as Miles came to be known—synthesized
and reformulated these, mostly Indian, teachings with the intention of
spreading them to the West, “as a spiritual path for the western house-
holder”2 of either sex. As a result of this effort, several groups consist-
ing of his Western disciples eventually emerged, the most important
being the International Nath Order (INO) and the Arcane and Magickal
Order of the Knights of Shambhala (AMOOKOS). A distinctive feature
of both Mahendranath’s teachings and of the discourse employed by his

Western disciples consists of the visible tendency toward syncretism.3
What is significant to observe is that this syncretic discourse assumes
a strong analogy and affinity between Indian and Western esotericism,
between Yoga and magick.4 In what follows, my intention is to explore
the implications of this assumed affinity.
Essential Teachings and Practices
The historical Nāths were yogis with tantric connections, originally estab-
lished in the early medieval North India (see Chapter 3). They are primar-
ily credited with the development of Hatha Yoga, based on the regime
of bodily practice, the goal of which was the attainment of miraculous
powers (siddhis)5 and the acquisition of the status of “a second Śiva.” In
his own teachings, and in accordance with traditional recommendations,
Dadaji similarly focused on the importance of proper posture (āsana),
controlled breathing (prānāyāma), and concentration (dhāranā), all of
which were often done in association with mantra chanting, visualiza-
tion, and work on the cakras. He accordingly gave instructions (here in
poetic form) to “sit cross-legged, comfortable and relaxed. . . . / Keep the
mind awake . . . / In the preliminary and early exploratory mind wan-
derings, / Concentrate by counting the breaths, and much pranayama”
(Mahendranath, n.d.: 124). These instructions accord with a widely held
assumption in Indian spirituality, which is that by regulating one’s pos-
ture and breathing the mind becomes calm and thus able to focus. This
leads to accomplishing the goal of Yoga, which has been traditionally
defined by Patañjali as “the cessation of the wanderings of the mind.”6 In
addition, Mahendranath wrote a number of poetic compositions, which
were intended to serve the role both of invocatory hymns and guided
meditation instructions.
As already remarked, the hallmark of Mahendranath’s spiritual system
lies in its syncretic nature: he freely and frequently blends the elements
of Tantra, I Ching, Zen, and Western occult teachings. Significantly, he
argued that the successful pursuit of the path toward liberation implies
the transcendence, or even reversal, of customary morality. This is a
recurring element in the ethos of Western occultists studied in this book,
and it is something that provides a strong link between their endeavors
and what in the Indian tradition is called the “practice of reversal” (ultā
sādhana). In Mahendranath’s (n.d.: 41) formulation: “The highest path of
Indian and Tibetan Yoga is the Varma Marg of Tantrika. Like our English
world of faery it teaches attainment by the complete reversal-inversion

of society morality, taboos and relationships.” As a consequence, Mahen-
dranath characterized the way of the magician as “amoral,” “neither
moral nor immoral . . . free from normal conditioning, for this is based
on religious, national and established interests” (102).
Mahendranath’s contacts with a growing number of Western dis-
ciples eventually resulted in the transmission of teachings through
initiations and the formation of the Orders of the “Western Nāths.”7
There were some important innovations in the process. The intention
was that the “International Nath Order would have to be for Western
people and adapt itself to the Western patterns, especially while in its
infancy.”8 The social aspect of this adaptation was that unlike its Indian
predecessors, the Western Nāths were to be primarily householders.
Ideologically, the result was that the conceptual vocabulary intended
to disseminate the teachings assumed some of the recognizable fea-
tures of Western esoteric discourse. Yogic and tantric concepts were
represented and understood as magick and the occult, which was par-
ticularly the case with AMOOKOS. Mahendranath stated the goal suc-
cinctly: “I devised the concept of a western Nath Order as a suitable
vehicle to extract the magick and occult concepts, which were more
advanced in the East, to the West, where they were little known.”9 Sev-
eral examples should illustrate how this “extraction of the magick and
occult concepts” was done.
Yoga of Magick; Magick of Yoga
Mahendranath (n.d.: 12) represents the historical Nāth Yogis as magi-
cians: “The Nathas and Aghoris were both associated with magic powers,
but while the Nathas were regarded as being the distributors of good luck
and blessings; the Aghoris were associated with Black Siddhis, or Black
Magic.”10 He defines Yoga as magick alchemy: “Yoga is the process, and
the goal is return to the One Absolute. / It is the spiritual Magick Alchemy,
independent of religions. . . . / Yoga is the dynamic power potential of
all modern Magick.”11 In a similar vein, meditation is understood as the
root of magick power: “The practice of meditation is, in itself, one of the
simplest processes, and it is also essential in the growth and expansion of
magick power and harmony. . . . Meditation . . . is the gateway to magick
and magick powers.”12 The magical philosophy of Crowley, the law of
Thelema summarized in the injunction “Do what thou wilt shall be the
whole of the Law,” is interpreted as harmonious with the essence of Indian
radical spirituality:

Mahendranath’s text, which, as is typical for his style, incorporates a good
deal of neologisms, “Sinistroversus: The Anti-dexter Varma, Turn-about

idea that the path of Tantra embraces both bhukti and mukti, liberation
and enjoyment. In arguing that this “path is not for everyone,” Mahen-
dranath engages in a discourse that shows close resemblance to Crowley’s
contrasting (via G. W. F. Hegel and Friedrich Nietzsche) between masters
and slaves: “Slaves must serve [cf. AL II: 58] and not enjoy; they must
worship and not attain” (n.d.: 87). As opposed to that, the way of the
Gods and of the masters implies “a way whereby one tastes the Micro-
cosm in all its aspects” (87). The paradigmatic area where one tastes the
totality of microcosmic aspects is eroticism, and Mahendranath elabo-
rates on several ritual examples that implement such an attitude toward
the world. These ritual acts consecrate the male and female partner as
an embodiment of the divine pair and their genital organs as appropri-
ate symbols of the goddess and god (see Mahendranath, n.d.: 93–4). The
ensuing “tantric sex” (to invoke the bête noire) in Mahendranath’s expo-
sition represents “The Magickal Way to create a New Aeon / The world
of the future and won by our Wonder” (94). In addition, suggestions for
other forms of sexual ritual acts are also given, incorporating Hindu ter-
minology and what may reasonably be called the worldview of Tantra.
Nevertheless, the concluding remarks are again couched in a formula-
tion that contains Thelemic overtones: “For the Cosmos upon one rule is
built / And the only Law is to do as you wilt” (98).

Francis Dashwood, and “Saint” Aleister Crowley on the one hand and
“the amoral philosophy of the Nathas, Tantriks and Siddha saints and

avadhūta, and correlated with the injunction “Do what you will.” The
overall point is that, according to Mahendranath (111), Rabelais, Dash-
wood, and Crowley practiced and taught an equivalent of the highest
spiritual traditions of the East.
Interpretations of Mahendranath’s Case: The Nature
of Esotericism and the Role of Syncretism
How are we to understand and interpret this free interplay between
the conceptual worlds of Indian Yoga and Tantra on the one hand, and
the Western occult and esoteric traditions on the other hand? What are

[Page 124]
WHEN yOgA BECOmEs mAgICK 117
the underlying assumptions that made it possible for Mahendranath
and his disciples (see below) to represent yogic meditation as a form of
magick? Is this a case of cultural misinterpretation? Is it an imposition of
a Western, and thus alien, universe of discourse onto what is specifically
an Indian religious category? Alternatively, if on the basis of conceptual
vocabulary we approach Mahendranath’s teachings as a form of Western
esotericism, how are we to explain the presence of yogic and tantric ele-
ments in his system?
Mahendranath had stated that his goal was to adapt to Western pat-
terns. In order to transmit the teachings, he consequently engaged in
what Stanley Tambiah has designated as the “translation of cultures.”15
My interpretation of his work rests on several, mutually reinforcing and
interrelated, assumptions. I suggest, here as elsewhere in this study, that
the “translation” of Yoga and Tantra as the occult and magick implies that
there is a “family resemblance” between these categories. An important
aspect of this resemblance lies in the tendency toward syncretism, which
again relies on the common esoteric notion of analogy or correspon-
dence, or, to use the Sanskrit term, the bandhu.16 In addition, the fact that
the Western Nāths incorporate Western esoteric notions into their dis-
course should not be seen as an anomaly, but rather as a typical activity
within the context of syncretic religiosity. In the East also, the ideas and
practices of Yoga and Tantra often transcend the doctrinal boundaries
between religious denominations, just as they cross the borders between
countries (see below). In the process, they exchange and reformulate
freely a significant amount of knowledge as well as conceptual vocabu-
lary. And finally, what this exchange also suggests is that esotericism and
the occult represent valid and important means of the transmission of
religious ideas and practices across ideological and geographic boundar-
ies.17 Let me at this juncture elaborate on these assertions.
As already argued on several occasions, perhaps the most characteris-
tic feature of esotericism consists of correlative thinking, that is, the belief
in correspondences18 between apparently discrete aspects of reality. An
unbroken chain of analogical relations connects the natural, human, and
spiritual worlds.19 This leads to the notion that the human microcosm
corresponds to the divine macrocosm, which notion—and the spiritual
practice based on it—has been of fundamental importance in Western
Esotericism20 as well as in Indian Yoga and Tantra. Richard Davis (1995:
41) draws attention to this crucial idea with the observation that “Tant-
rics view the human body as a microcosm of the universe, and focus on it
as the only vehicle for attaining powers and liberation.”21 Mahendranath

stresses the same principle when, in a gesture of syncretism, he makes
reference to both Indian religious philosophy as the source of the idea,
and to magick as its field of application:
The basic thought in the Nath Order is that a conscious thinking power
(Purusha) permeates the entire infinite universe or cosmos. . . . From this
follows the conclusion that all human beings on earth must also contain
a particle of this Supreme Spirit. . . . Therefore our magick becomes pos-

manifest and use the power of the Cosmic Spirit.22
An aspect of correlative thinking lies in the postulation that, aside from
the physical, human beings also posses a corresponding subtle body, the
knowledge of which is of great importance in one’s spiritual quest. This is
again a common supposition in Western Esotericism, in which the con-
cept of the “astral body” looms large, as in Yoga and Tantra, in which
the fundamental notions of the occult centers of energy, the cakras, and
the gnosis-imparting “serpent power,” the kundalinī, refer precisely to the
realities of the subtle body.23 What is also common to these traditions is
the assumption that it is the praxis, sādhana, and not faith or doctrine,
which is of primary importance. Similarly, it is a shared belief among the
religious groups under discussion that human beings are perfectible, that
a transformation of an ordinary person into an enlightened, immortal,
or divine being is possible. Antoine Faivre has asserted transmutation as
one of the defining characteristics of Western Esotericism.24 Similarly,
Shashibhushan Dasgupta (1969: 251), writing about the historical prede-
cessors of Mahendranath and the Western Nāths, declares: “The Sādhanā
of the Nāth Siddhas is essentially a Sādhanā of transubstantiation and
transfiguration.” For all these reasons, it appears acceptable and oppor-
tune to approach and understand Indian yogic and tantric traditions as
forms of esotericism, which share a family resemblance with their West-
ern counterparts.
Esoteric traditions commonly and typically display a tendency
toward syncretism.25 Syncretism is a gesture of inclusion, and as such
it naturally stems from the belief in the mutual interconnectedness of
all phenomena, which is the basic philosophy behind the notion of cor-
respondences. We have seen that the teachings of Mahendranath Dadaji
intended for the Western Nāths display a significant degree of syncre-
tism. This is, however, nothing uncommon within the context of Indian

[Page 126]

or even pan-Asian religiosity. The historical Nāth yogis, for example, are
well known for their connections with both Buddhist and Islamic eso-
teric groups. Historically and ideologically, they are related to medieval
Buddhist Sahajiyās and Mahasiddhas.26 David Gordon White (1995:
402) claims that “perhaps no Hindu religious sect has interacted on as
profound and sustained a level as have the Nāth Siddhas with exponents
of that mystic branch of Islam known as Sufism.”27 Even more to the
point are the tantric traditions in general. Tantric forms of theory and
practice are, or have been, observable in Indian Buddhism, Hinduism,
Jainism,28 and, as already mentioned, even Islam. Tantric teachings have
crossed the boundaries of India to other countries of Asia, and they
have often mingled there with the local traditions: in Tibet with Bön,
in China with Daoism, in Japan with Shintō, in Indonesia with Islam.29
There is therefore nothing particularly unusual and incongruous in
either Mahendranath’s syncretism per se or in his incorporation of the
local, Western esoteric, idiom.
Geographically, Tantra is, or has been, practiced in India, Nepal,
Tibet, Bhutan, Mongolia, China, Japan, and Southeast Asia. In the 20th
century, tantric teachings also arrived in the West.30 Dissemination of the
teachings across such a vast area is facilitated by its inclusive and flexible
nature, which is also a characteristic feature of esotericism. As already
mentioned, esoteric traditions emphasize the practical dimensions of
teachings rather than a doctrinal fidelity, and as such they align them-
selves easily with diverse religious denominations. An alternative way
of stating the same would be to suggest that esotericism is a valid and
important means of cultural transfer. Western esotericists were much
more instrumental in the acculturation of non-European spiritual tradi-
tions than has generally been acknowledged. This was possible because,
as already argued, there exists an important similarity, a “correspon-
dence,” between various manifestations of esoteric theory and practice.
Ritually transmitted teachings about hidden aspects of reality accessed
through the awakening of the occult centers of power within the human
body and mind are as common in Indian Yoga and Tantra as they are in
Western occultism and magick. And it is precisely this commonality, this
shared cultural space, that justifies and explains the syncretic character
of Mahendranath’s and the Western Nāths’ reformulation of their Indian
spiritual heritage. In the remainder of this chapter, this reformulation
will be illustrated through short surveys of the work of some of Mahen-
dranath’s major (whether direct or indirect) disciples.

Mike Magee and the Arcane and Magickal Order of
the Knights of Shambhala (AMOOKOS)
Mike Magee is a Scotland-born British journalist currently based in
Oxford. While still a member of Kenneth Grant’s Typhonian Ordo Tem-
pli Orientis (now simply Typhonian Order), he came into contact with
Dadaji Mahendranath in India and became his disciple. As a result of
this collaboration, in 1978 Dadaji provided Magee with a charter to cre-
ate an East-West order, which was to disseminate the teachings of the
Nāths to the occidental aspirants. The name of the order is significant for
its blending of Eastern and Western (particularly Thelemic) esotericism:
The Arcane and Magickal Order of the Knights of Shambhala (AMOO-
KOS; the Thelemic component is related to the spelling of magick with
“k,” although in Crowley’s practice the “k” was dropped in adjectival
usage). At a later point, Dadaji recanted this appointment, and chose his
American pupil Kapilnath, associated with the International Nath Order
(INO), as his successor.31 The issue of the validity of either succession is
beyond the scope of my intentions with this study: for a historian of reli-
gions, both factions are of interest as examples of contemporary spiritual-
ity. The fact that I am focusing on AMOOKOS is not in and of itself an
endorsement, but a result of methodological preference that is grounded
in geographical and formal expediency relevant to this study: AMOO-
KOS is a predominantly British group, and they tend to emphasize the
confluence between Indian and Western occult ideas and practices, while
INO appears to have a stronger presence in North America and leans
toward the more traditional style of its Indian origins.
It is of great interest to observe the conceptual vocabulary contained
in Dadaji’s charter given to Magee (dated January 1, 1978). Its rhetorical

Mogg Morgan is well known in the occult community as a writer and
the proprietor of the publishing enterprise Mandrake of Oxford. His
interests, reflected also in the thematic spectrum of Mandrake’s numer-

to emphasize Morgan’s assertion about the eclectic nature of contempo-

Morgan (2008a: 90) describes his entry into the world of esoteric
spirituality in a manner that underscores its syncretic nature, which
is otherwise characteristic of what Faivre calls “the practice of con-

underpinnings of his position, “at a crossroad between East and West,”

West approach lies at the heart of Theosophy, the Hermetic Order of
the Golden Dawn [for which see my comments below, G.D.], the OTO
and so on. Some call this current Typhonian, I call it Tankhem” (90).

magical Orders owe to India and Egypt, which otherwise occupy the
foundational place in the imaginary of Western esotericism: “All these
magical styles’ work are crossover between the ideas of late Egyptian
and early ‘tantrik’ magick” (90).
In the introduction to his Sexual Magick, written under the pen
name Katon Shual, Morgan reflects on the issue of syncretism, particu-
larly within the context of the British magical tradition, in the following
manner:
Western magick as it is practiced now is eclectic in its approach. That is to say
it borrows ideas and techniques from a wide range of global traditions. This
symbiosis had been going on for so long it is almost impossible to point to a
so-called native British tradition, should one want to. Even if one were to go
back to the Celtic/shamanistic roots one finds links with the cultures of India
and Mediterranean. Contrary to the beliefs of some present day occultists, it is
this long standing symbiosis which is the strength of modern magick. (Shual,
1995: vi)
Generally speaking, Morgan is consistent in his contention that there is a
fundamental communality between Indian Yoga and Tantra and Western
occultism. He explicitly states (Morgan, 2008b: 18) that what “is called

not only from the preceding quotes but also from the general tenor of

Magick.” Due to this fundamental correspondence, because of this “fam-

is both feasible and fertile. What from a certain perspective appears as a
process of inventing a tradition, from an alternative position (adopted by
Morgan and others studied in this book) constitutes an exploration in the
common field. To illustrate this point, we may concentrate on Morgan’s
passing remark that “some of the late Victorian grimoires, such as P. B.
Randolph’s Magia Sexualis, came through the melting pot of colonials’
tantric collections” (Shual, 1995: 10). In this instance, it is useful to recall

lineage. Versluis focuses on Western traditions in which such breaks his-

cal conditions reinforced by religious intolerance toward nonorthodox
believes and practices; however, there is no inherent reason why such
an initiatory role could not be extended to books and art representing
Eastern traditions.
Morgan seems to be on more speculative terrain in his assertion that
there have been some tantric influences on the Hermetic Order of the
Golden Dawn and that “tantrik concepts and practices were widely used
by initiates of the TS [Theosophical Society] and GD [Golden Dawn]”

and that the “[h]igher adepts of both these early magical orders practiced
some kind of sexual magick, itself derived from secret teachers of the
then despised tantrik sects” (10). What makes this a more speculative
claim is that unlike his earlier-mentioned statement on the inspirative
value of textual resources, which relies on the fundamental importance
of imagination in esoteric disciplines, this latter contention implies an
actual historical link, the reality of which remains undocumented and as
such and to that degree doubtful.

Andrew Chumbley and the Sabbatic Tantra
Andrew D. Chumbley (1967–2004) was a writer, artist, scholar, and prac-
titioner of what he designated as the “Sabbatic Craft,” a form of magic
influenced for the most part by the theory and practice of European, par-
ticularly British, “cunning-craft” traditions. By the time of his untimely
death on his thirty-seventh birthday, Chumbley was engaged in a doc-
toral program at the University of London, working on the subject of
initiatic dreams in history.33 He was also for a while involved in Grant’s
Typhonian Order and he had, among other influences, received initiation
from Mahendranath Dadaji’s disciple John Powers, also known as Vila-
sanath (Schulke, 2010). He explains the syncretic character of his own
path by acknowledging contacts with initiates of various traditions, and
states: “Such contacts have led me to active involvement with the Paths of
Uttara Kaula Tantrikas and, upon a subtle level, with the Sufi Tradition of
Ovaysiyya” (Chumbley, 2011: 79).34 However, Chumbley was most inti-
mately involved, in the capacity of its Magister, with the previously name-
less order, which he came to call the Cultus Sabbati.35
The Cultus Sabbati, which defines itself as an order of traditional
witchcraft, was led by Chumbley from 1991 to 2004, and since then by
Daniel A. Schulke. The order is also associated with two publishing ven-
tures, Xoanon Publishing, specializing in sabbatic lore, and Three Hands
Press. Describing the organizational structure of the order, Schulke (2010)
explains that, among others, “the convergent streams of Tantric teaching,
mysticism and praxis . . . have [also] entered” into the Cultus. Schulke
(2010) nevertheless emphasizes: “Though there are considerable Tant-
ric influences in currency within our order, the entirety of our work is
driven by the Sabbatic recensions of British witch-lore which are exclu-
sively passed orally from Master to Prentice, and not written down.” There
exists, however, a cell within the order that focuses primarily on the Sab-
batic recension and expansion of traditional tantric material. This internal
cell is designated as Ku-Trishula, or The Order of the Negative Trident.
According to Schulke (2010), while the Ku-Trishula “preserves many
‘traditional’ aspects of these [tantric] teachings in their raw forms, these
have also acted as seed-crystals for the augmentation and development of
sorcerous practices within the Sabbatic continuum.” The theoretical and
practical aspects of these teachings consists of the following elements:
i. asceticism as a foundation of witchcraft praxis (various)
ii. knowledge of so-called “psycho-sexual anatomy”

In the as yet unpublished The Dragon-Book of Essex (1998), otherwise
privately distributed among select initiates of the Cultus Sabbati, Chum-
bley provides much more elaborate meditative and ritual instructions for
the performance of “The Wreath of the Seven Laughing Skulls,” apparently
based on the teachings received in dream. I have not seen the full descrip-
tion of the practice, but was generously allowed to examine a few excerpts
from the text.40 The “Preparatory Comment” establishes the objective of the
endeavor and suggests that the union of “Purity and Perfidy,” the desired
outcome based on the transcendence and transmutation of both “poisons
and nectars of the Serpent,”41 brings about the “elixirs of wisdom” and the
attainment of gnosis and magical fearlessness. The modus operandi con-
sists, among other things, in the cutting through of dualism inherent in the
functioning of the nonemancipated ordinary consciousness.
The section of the text entitled “The Secret Ritual Domain: The Trian-
gular Field of Sorcerous Ferocity” lays out technical details related to the
place of operation. The place is triangular: in his mind, the practitioner is
to imagine an immense charnel-ground marked by three “colossal swords
of black fire” between which lightning bolts crackle. In practical terms, an
area demarcated by three nails or staves mutually connected with strands
of colored thread will suffice. This area is imaginally and conceptually
identified with the female pubic region, a yoni of the goddess, on which
the seated or standing practitioner is to be assumed to signify a divine
phallus, a lingam. Chumbley (1998) states that the dream-given name
for this enclosed area was “The Charnel-ground of the Black Goat” and
that consequently the ritual space is to be imagined to symbolically rep-
resent the severed head of the animal on which the practitioner sits, as

In order to proceed with the practice in such an enclosure, it is neces-
sary for the practitioner to enter into some “strange and terrifying place,”
whether imaginal or actual. Once this is accomplished, the ritual is to
continue along eight major consecutive steps. The first one of these, and
the only one I have had access to, incorporates an enchantment that
invokes the union of “purity and perfidy.” While doing so, the seeker
should first imagine that he sits on the skeletal remains of his parents
in sexual embrace. Next, he should imagine that the bones are of those
belonging to the Corpse-king and Corpse-queen. Finally, he should per-
ceive the bones on which he is supposed to be sitting as being his own
and of his own ritual consort. Chumbley (1998) concludes: “Upon the
Throne of Mortal Desire’s Transcendence the body of the practitioner
should be realised as the divine cerulean body of Qayin La-Azhaka.”
We see in the above a pattern that is woven out of several notional
threads with important tantric associations. All the major strands—
power, gnosis, fearlessness, transcendence of death, and sexuality, of
Eros and Thanatos, of life and death, through the recognition of their
equivalence, and the final union of “purity and perfidy”—have their par-
allels in the conceptual and ritual world that is characteristic of Tantra.
Simultaneously, we should not lose sight of the fact that the context of
the above-described ritual is a solitary practice that is incorporated into
the curriculum of the Western Order of “traditional witchcraft” and that
its most explicit referent is that it is contained in the “Dragon-Book of
Essex.” This is a fine example of not only syncretism, which is in and of
itself an important ingredient of Western—and Eastern!—esotericism,
but also of the translation of cultures. It is also a fine example of Western
Tantra, which in its occultist rendition (to use an awkward expression)
has nothing to do with the New Age phenomenon of misrepresenting
Tantra as a form of improved sex. Sexuality is important in Tantra, but
this is a form of sex in which one embraces carnally one’s mortality amid
the wreath of the laughing skulls.
