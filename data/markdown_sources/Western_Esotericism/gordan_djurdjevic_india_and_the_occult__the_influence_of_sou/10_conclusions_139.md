As I have already indicated in the introductory chapter, it is similarly
incongruent to evaluate the occultist engagement with Yoga and Tan-
tra in accordance with academic conventions of research. To provide
an example, Agehananda Bharati (1962) wrote that he would turn away
from his office those spiritual seekers who wanted to study Yoga or Zen
but were disinclined to study Sanskrit or Chinese (and not only Japa-
nese, in the case of Zen, as he himself pointed out)! And while this is an
admirable position to take, it is simultaneously far from realistic, and if
applied, this precept would preclude the majority of people from being
practitioners of their respective religious traditions. The vast majority of
Christians throughout history could not read any of the three classical
biblical languages (Hebrew, Greek, and Latin), just as the vast majority of
Buddhists could not read Sanskrit or Pāli. Religious practices are influ-
enced but not determined by their historical, cultural, and social context.
I concur with Jeffrey Kripal here, who argued that “cultural differences
and local knowledges are socially and politically important but not onto-
logically ultimate” (2006: 94).
If we give credence to the speculations of Indian yogis and tāntrikas,
if we are respectful toward their traditional claims, then we have to
acknowledge that these claims are not, and cannot be, socially and cul-
turally limited in their assumed properties. Either cakras and kundalinī
may exist, in which case they cannot be confined to Indian, Buddhist,
Hindu, or any particular body, but must needs be potentially accessi-
ble by everyone, or they do not exist, in which case Asian tāntrikas are
as deluded as the Western occultists. (This holds true also if we accept

not exist as such, but have to be imaginatively created.) That being said,
it is simultaneously true that local traditions frame the perception and
expression of such subtle realities in a culture-specific manner, so that,
for example, in Hindu Tantra the kundalinī is approached and concep-
tualized in theomorphic registers, as a goddess. In the Western habitat,
the original cultural imaginings and conceptualizations may be lost, for-
gotten, overlooked, misunderstood, or simply omitted, so that instead
of being approached as the goddess, kundalinī, as Olav Hammer criti-
cally observes, is approached “merely” as an energy. This may well be so,
and I will return to this issue in a moment, but if our mental constructs
are crafted after the fact, if the experience is the primary and its repre-
sentation the secondary fact, then both “goddess” and “energy” are sec-
ond-order constructs signifying a common experience of extraordinary
power to those, and according to those, who claim to have had it. There
is a complex relationship between cultural diversity and biological uni-
formity underlying all of these experiences, but there is no inherent and
essential reason why the conceptual constructs expressed in one cultural
code must override those expressed differently. As Kripal (2007: 177)
argues: “The ‘inner serpent’ of kundalini yoga is simply a South Asian
construction of universal neurobiology.”
But let us return to representations of the kundalinī. Even in India,
there are varieties and differences in this respect so that while some tra-
ditions speak of it as a goddess, in an androcentric discourse of the Nāth
yogis, we find an alterative expression, the “fire of brahman” (brahmāgni),
referring to the same phenomenon.2 So when Crowley approaches the
subject, for example in his essay “Energised Enthusiasm,” it is under-
standable and to be expected that his framing of the issue is expressed in
a different vocabulary, in a different cultural code. Nevertheless, it is clear
that what he describes, and what appears to show a deep and meaningful
similarity with the experience of kundalinī, is approached as something
extraordinarily significant, as a divine inspiration and a source of the
genius, so that, despite the culture-specific discourse, its general tenor is
