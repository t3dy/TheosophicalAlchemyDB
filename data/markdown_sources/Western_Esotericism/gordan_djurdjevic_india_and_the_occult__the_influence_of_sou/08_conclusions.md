for the purpose of conceptualizing the nature of esotericism as a reli-
gious and cultural phenomenon. Faivre has advised against an attempt
to construct a category of universal esotericism, arguing instead for the
cultural and historical specificity, even uniqueness, of Western esoteric
traditions.43 Without denying fully the veracity of the latter assertion, I
am continuously proposing an alternative view. It is both logical and his-
torically observable, that exoteric forms of religious thought and practice
have their esoteric dimension. It is undeniable that there exists (at least as
a feasible category that makes it possible to theorize about) esoteric Bud-
dhism, esoteric Hinduism, and esoteric Islam. This statement should not
be construed as if it were implying an identity between various forms of
esotericism, nor as if it were suggesting that esotericism is an essentialist
category, unaffected by its cultural environment and the force of histori-
cal change.
What emerges as obvious from the writings of Mahendranath,
Magee, Morgan, and Chumbley is the conviction about the compatibility
between regional forms of esotericism, or, to express it with reference to
two paradigmatic categories, between Yoga and magic. It also appears
obvious that this conviction is based on personal experience and engage-
ment with these two particular forms of religious thought and practice.
I suggest that the principal reason behind the notion of compatibility
between regional forms of esotericism, which leads to practices of a syn-
cretic character, lies in the lived experience that justifies it and renders
it meaningful. In practice, these various methods of spiritual cultiva-
tion apparently work. It is erroneous to maintain that one needs to be
immersed in a native culture from which a particular practice emerged
in order to have some results and benefits stemming from the practice.
Engagement with one’s body and mind in their occult aspects appar-
ently and arguably yields some results that can be overlaid with syncretic
concepts and worldviews that to the practitioners not only do not seem
incongruous but provide explanatory expediency and semantic richness.
Here as elsewhere, an attitude toward sex as a perceived component
of tantric teachings calls for a comment. I have argued throughout this
study that while such a perception among Western occultists, including
Mahendranath’s direct and indirect disciples, does indeed exist, it should
not be confused or conflated with the New Age theories about Tantra as
a form of enhanced sexual technique that leads to orgasmic bliss as its
principal raison d’être. The attitude toward sex is emphatically different.
A short document entitled “Tantra, Sex and the New Age,” composed by
Consistory Council of AMOOKOS, addresses the issue explicitly; it also

Tantra as encouraged by AMOOKOS is not related to new age tantra with

Instead, AMOOKOS affirms that genuine tantra is an occult tradition.
It has its own worldview, values, methods, practices and stories. It is chal-
lenging and can be confronting. It is also not without risk. It is experiential
and practical and it transcends religious and scientific worldviews.
That is not to say that any of the secrets of tantra are not discoverable
by any human being equipped with five senses. It is more that peoples
[sic] lives most often move them away from what is natural and towards
what is artificial. And what is artificial is, by definition, shaped by societal
conditioning.
Indian and Western esotericism share some fundamental suppositions,
such as the belief in correspondences and the occult aspects of the
human body, and are they similar not only for the practice of concor-
dance, or syncretism, and for the ritual transmission of an (often secret)
body of knowledge. These two religious traditions have also in common
a long-standing and deep engagement with a number of specific esoteric
disciplines, such as magic or abhicara, alchemy or rasāyana, and astrol-
ogy or jyotiśa.44 Each of these disciplines is both similar to and different
from its counterpart in its respective cultural expression and historical
contingency. For this reason, and in way of conclusion, in order to under-
stand religious teachers such as Mahendranath and groups such as the
Western Nāths, what appears as a scholarly desiderative is not a construc-
tion of the category of universal and unchanging esotericism, but instead
an alertness to and engagement with what is best described as a com-
parative esotericism. Such an academic approach would be as sensitive to
the common features between various esoteric traditions, which I have
attempted to emphasize here, as it should be aware of the specific cultural
and historical singularity of the particular forms of esoteric theory and
practice.
