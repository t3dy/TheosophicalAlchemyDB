## Section: Conclusions: Eastern Methods and Western Culture

Conclusions: Eastern Methods and Western Culture
Fortune’s position within the context of Western esoteric tradition is well
established, and her books continue to be reprinted. The Society of Inner
Light is still active. One of the dominant ideological positions to which
Fortune subscribed and which she consistently promoted was that, while
various local traditions may be of equal value, they should not mix, and
as a consequence, she argued that Eastern methods are fundamentally
unsuited for Westerners. This may be an appropriate place to devote addi-
tional attention to this issue and to interrogate some of its implications.
At first glance, Fortune’s position is problematic on several grounds.
The contemporary reader is immediately and uneasily alerted to what
appears as a strong cultural essentialism and even racial prejudice in her
writings. Fortune ties culture, race, and religion together so strongly that
their mutual codependency seems determined. It merits remembering,
however, that the presence and frequency of racial arguments were much
more a part of everyday discourse at the time of her writing than is the
case now. Fortune’s insistence on remaining faithful to one’s own cul-
ture does reflect similar considerations often voiced in cultural studies,
where the argument is that the understanding of cultural norms is locally
contingent. Thus, the New Age style of thought is frequently criticized

[Page 95]

precisely for its tendency to uncritically blur specific regional identities
of spiritual notions and practices and to lump together diverse system
incongruously. But even if one agrees to a certain validity of Fortune’s
argument, it nevertheless seems that she is wrong in suggesting that Yoga
is unsuitable for Westerners, given its current wide popularity in North
America and Europe.
In her book What Is Occultism? (2001b; originally published in 1929
as Sane Occultism), Fortune attempts to provide a scientific explanation
for her exclusivist views. Here she expounds the same basic attitude that
she will later continue with in The Circuit of Force, praising Eastern wis-
dom as such and recommending the theoretical engagement with its tra-
ditions, but advising against the practical application of its methods. As
far as the practice is concerned, Fortune is convinced that “it is best for a
man to follow the line of his own racial evolution. It is very seldom that
a European, living in Europe, is successfully trained by Eastern meth-
ods” (161). The reason for this relates to the principal notion that occult
practices affect the functioning of the cakras, which Fortune here cor-
relates with endocrine glands and their role in the organism (163).11 She
thus argues that Eastern theory is confirmed by Western anatomy, but the
problem, according to Fortune, lies in the fact that “endocrine balance in
different races differs profoundly” (164).12 In order to remedy this situa-
tion, “the Great White Lodge gives to each race the religion suited to its
needs” (166).
It would be relatively easy to accuse Fortune of promoting racial
ideology, but her arguments do not privilege any particular race or creed
over another. It may be of interest to observe, while still on this subject,
that she approaches the issue of the diversity among religious and initi-
atic systems in a manner that is exactly opposite to Crowley’s views on
the matter. She essentializes the diversity by arguing the fundamental
connection between locality and its respective culture: one determines
the other. Crowley relativizes the diversity, seeing it only as something
accidental, as shades produced by local conditions that are cast over the
fundamental unity. Consider, for example, his statement in Eight Lectures
on Yoga: “The methods all over the world were identical; this was masked
by religious prejudice and local custom. But in their quiddity— identical!”
(Crowley, 1991a: 24). However, the issue is more complex because, as we
have seen, Fortune herself acknowledges the fundamental unity underly-
ing diverse systems. She writes in The Training and Work of an Initiate: “If
we have the key to one symbol-system we can readily equate it with all the
others, for fundamentally they are the same” (92). Thus she accepts the

Jung brings in the difference in psychological structure. In a lecture given
in 1932, he proposes that “if you get into the Hindu mentality, you are
just upside-down, you are all wrong. They have the unconscious above,
we have it below. Everything is just the opposite” (1996: 16).
The recent popularity of Yoga and other forms of Eastern spiritual-
ity apparently contradicts both Fortune and Jung, as well as those who,
like them, argued against the possibility of adopting and pursuing for-
eign religious and cultural practices. But the subject remains contentious.
Critics often point out the incongruous nature of syncretism, made up
of improperly understood and insufficiently defined notions that, for
example, end up decontextualized in the melting pot of New Age spiritu-
ality. Such uncritical borrowings from other cultures often amount to just
another form of exploitation, to a contemporary type of neocolonialism.
The underlying binary mentality observed in such instances, the preva-
lence given to the opposition between the materialistic West and the spir-
itual East, is similarly criticized as simplistic, incorrect, and supportive of
the status quo. And perhaps the most glaring contradiction refers to the
fact that engagement in the spiritual culture of the East becomes another
possession, a kind of “spiritual materialism,” in its Western form. Yoga,
with its origin in the culture of world renouncers and ascetics, becomes
the technique of fitness and health and a status symbol. And when
śramanas turn into yuppies, the question becomes: is this still Yoga?
This question must remain open. The underlying issue, the subject
of sameness and difference between diverse cultural systems of thought
and practice, is a mercurial one. Societies, cultures, ideas, and practices
continually change. Three world religions, Buddhism, Christianity, and
Islam, spread and developed by migration, and in the process interacted
with and often adopted, in varying degrees, elements of indigenous
cultural practices, and in consequence they also changed. This process
is perhaps most clearly observed in Buddhism, in which there is the

somewhat from these religions in that they are often considered as tech-

more prone to adoption by respective larger denominational environ-

of Yoga and that it will be based on Christianity (see Jung, 1996: xxx).
Fortune and other occultists treated in this study, often implicitly and
sometimes explicitly, argued that the proper model for the adoption of
Eastern spirituality lies in the Western esoteric tradition, which in itself
in their opinion stands for the Yoga of the West.
Before closing this chapter, brief mention should be made of the fact
that Fortune also wrote fictional works on occult subjects. She consid-
ered this a serious matter and as another avenue for the dissemination
of her teachings. Of great interest is her admission that “[t]he ‘Mysti-
cal Qabalah’ [i.e., her nonfictional opus] gives the theory, but the novels
give the practice” (Fortune, 2003: xiii). Grant suggests that her novels,
which typically present strong feminine characters who serve the role of
the initiatrix for previously sexually and in other ways frustrated male
partners, are at variance with her doctrinal texts that give preference
to Western esoteric tradition (see Grant, 1993: ix). He argues: “In her
novels . . . we obtain frequent glimpses of a startlingly unorthodox and
skillful occultist who succeeded in gaining access to other worlds, other
dimensions, through an adaptation of Eastern techniques usually associ-
ated with Tantric yoga” (ix). Katon Shual (also known as Mogg Morgan)
similarly claims (1995: 26) that “An undercurrent of sexual magick and
tantra permeates Dion Fortune’s entire magical career, the best examples
of which are found in her novels.” The strongest statement on the issue
is contained in Grant’s letter to the American biographer of Fortune, in
which he declares,
It was obvious to me then, and the conviction grows stronger each time

although Dion was far from fulfilling this role in quite the same way that
most of Aleister’s shaktis fulfilled it, she was, I believe, fully conscious of

Kenneth Grant (1924–2011) was a British writer on occult subjects,
who was primarily known for developing an idiosyncratic
interpretation of the work of Aleister Crowley (1875–1947) and his
magico-religious doctrine of Thelema.1 Grant’s oeuvre has generated a
small but devoted following and, in addition to his literary activities, he
was also recognized as the Head of the magical fraternity known as the
Typhonian Order.2 In his body of work, Grant has created an unlikely
mélange comprised of thematic threads that include both Eastern and
Western esoteric traditions, in addition to consistent references to artistic
and literary works infused with the aroma of the mysterious, fantastic,
and uncanny, with a dominant place assigned to the fictional output of
H. P. Lovecraft and the visionary creations of Austin O. Spare. Grant’s
literary style is notorious for being hermeneutically opaque—those who
are not put off by it maintain (for example, Bogdan, 2003: viii) that the
immersion in its verbal and conceptual labyrinths amounts to a form of
lesemysterien. In this chapter, the focus will be on the influence of Indian
spiritual traditions on Grant’s opus and on his interpretation of their
similarity with Western occultism.
Although he already had some prior occult experiences, Grant’s
magical career started in earnest with his apprenticeship under the aged
Crowley in 1945.3 It is of some significance in the present context that
Grant (1980: 90, n.5) describes his initial mystical practices, commenced
several years before he met with Crowley, as “certain yogic exercises

involving prolonged attentiveness to the flow of mental images.” Despite
the fact that his secretarial appointment with Crowley lasted only sev-
eral months, the experience proved to be of a life-long impact. Grant’s
earliest writings, however, addressed mostly Hindu spiritual traditions
and appeared in Indian magazines such as The Call Divine (Bombay)
and Ananda Varta (Banaras) in the early 1950s.4 In these writings, per-
haps surprisingly to those familiar with his later output, Grant comes
across primarily as a devotee. The bulk of his writing in this period
is strongly influenced by the life and thought of the twentieth-century
mystic Ramana Maharshi. In particular, Grant seems to be impressed
by the spiritual practice of ātma vicāra, in other words, internal self-
reflection intended to eventually lead to the discovery of the “true self”
through the process of overcoming the mental chatter by the consistent
checking of the real identity of the perceiver of its content: “Who am
I?”5 It could be argued that the process of ātma vicāra, coupled with his
interest in ontological nondualism (advaita), eventually led Grant to
the elaboration of an extremely complex magical universe, understood
as a projection of singular consciousness, which in itself is but an illu-
sion, glamour, māyā. This issue will be elaborated in greater detail at a
later point.6

relevance that already in an article published in 1954, Grant drew a cor-

he made a connection between four levels of consciousness as analyzed

same phenomena, given in accordance with the glyph of the Tree of Life
(as it is employed in Western occult tradition).8 In Grant’s view, the two

Two elements in this quotation merit further comments. One concerns
the explicit correlation between Eastern and Western metaphysical ideas
in a manner that shares significant similarity with the doctrinal position of
Traditionalism or Perennialism.10 According to this view, there exists one
primordial Tradition that finds its more or less pristine (or rather, more or
less corrupt) expressions in the local, typically esoteric, systems of spiri-
tual theory and praxis.11 In the same essay, Grant expresses this worldview
in an unequivocal manner. Referring to the already cited paragraph, he
states that
to the writer’s mind no greater help of soldering the love of East for West,
and vice versa may be given than the demonstration that differences appear
only upon the surface of the two traditions and not at the Heart, where all
is One and the Same. (2006 [1954]: 48)
The difference between Grant and other Traditionalist authors, such as
René Guénon or Ananda Coomaraswamy, lies in his positive evalua-
tion of the Western occult/magical tradition (what the latter would no
doubt consider a counter-Tradition).12 By this gesture, Grant evidently
acknowledges the validity of the occult current, and it is paradigmatic
that his legitimizing strategy relies on the perceived respectability of
Eastern metaphysics. The correlation that Grant establishes thus simulta-
neously serves two mutually reinforcing perspectives: it is an example of
positive Orientalism13 just as it is an example of the respectful treatment
of Western occultism.
Another issue that is implicit in the correlation between the four states
of consciousness as conceptualized in Indian metaphysics and the West-
ern symbolic model of the Tree of Life concerns Grant’s privileging of
consciousness as the primary stuff of which reality is made.14 It hardly
needs mentioning that this is a common presupposition in Indian meta-
physics, both Hindu and Buddhist. It is, however, somewhat atypical for
the proponent of Western occultism to stress this view. To a large degree,
it is arguable that Grant was in this respect influenced not only by his
study of Oriental philosophy but also by the teachings of Crowley, who
similarly considered the mind as fons et origo of magical phenomena and
the fundamental blueprint of reality. In Grant’s case, this is evident in his
above-quoted correlation, in which the analogy focuses solely on modi-
fications of consciousness, and not on the mapping of both the internal

and external universe as is habitually the case when the model of the Tree
of Life is employed.15
The perennialist position with the occultist slant is also maintained
and evident in the series of ten essays that Grant and his wife and col-
laborator, Steffi Grant, who deserves a study of her own, originally issued
between 1959 and 1963 under the title Carfax Monographs.16 The first
essay in the series (Grant and Grant, 2006: 2–4), written by Steffi but in
full accord with her husband’s ideas, again concerns the glyph of the Tree
of Life. The essay is introduced by the statement (Grant and Grant, 2006:
3; emphasis added) that it is a “note on the modifications of One Con-
sciousness” represented “with a coloured diagram of the Qabalistic Tree of
Life.” Significantly, the diagram is compared to Śrī Yantra,17 which “repre-
sents Devi [Goddess] in geometric form” and it is interpreted as “an eido-
lon of Shakti, the manifest and active aspect of the Godhead” (Grant and
Grant, 2006: 3). As in the previous example, several mutually reinforcing
legitimizing strategies are observable in these statements, the purpose
of which, broadly speaking, is to validate the similarity between Indian
spiritual tradition and Western occultism. This approach, controversial
though it may be, arguably represents an original avenue of intercultural
interpretation and conceptual cross-pollination. Once again, and without
denigrating the value of the Grants’ contribution, it needs emphasizing
that the seeds of elucidating Indian metaphysics through the lenses of the
Western occult tradition, by mapping the former on the model of the Tree
of Life, were already cast by Crowley in several of his important writings.
Typhonian Trilogies
Grant’s first published monograph was Magical Revival in 1972 (reissued
in 1991 and in 2010). In fact, the manuscript he submitted was deemed
too bulky by the publisher, and the text was split into two separate vol-
umes. The second part was thus published in 1973 as Aleister Crowley
and the Hidden God. In 1975, Grant published Cults of the Shadow, and
the three books, due to the consistency of their running themes, came
to be thought of as a trilogy. Grant continued to develop his ideas (or at
least, the exposition of his ideas) in subsequent volumes, which some-
times appeared after a significant hiatus, and they eventually materialized
as the triple trilogy, customarily referred to as the Typhonian Trilogies. In
addition, the Typhonian subjects were also treated in fictional form, and
in particular the short novel Against the Light (1997) is considered as a
narrative supplement to the final volume of the Trilogies, The Ninth Arch

[Page 102]

(2002). The novella Dance, Doll, Dance! (2003a) engages tantric subjects.
Grant also published his short reminiscences of Crowley and, with Steffi
Grant, of Austin Osman Spare (Grant and Grant, 1998), in addition to his
monograph on the latter (Grant, 1975b). And while Grant’s main themes
consistently appear in all his published works, the main focus here will be
on the Typhonian Trilogies.18
It would be nearly impossible, and it would certainly exceed the scope
of present considerations, to provide a comprehensive account of the ideo-
logical universe treated in the Trilogies. Instead, the emphasis here will be
on the presence and treatment of Indological material. The subject matter
is mutually interrelated and forms a complex whole, but for analytical
reasons I propose to single out and treat in more detail the following five
subjects: (1) the underlying theme of the fundamental similarity between
the mysteries of the East and of the West, which is a commonly held view
in the rhetoric of Western esotericism, but elucidated with Grant’s par-
ticular idiosyncrasy; (2) the consistent presentation of an advaitan (non-
dual) worldview with an accompanying emphasis on consciousness as the
only real substance of the universe; (3) the correlation that Grant draws
between the ontological teaching on emptiness as expounded in Eastern
metaphysics and Western magical and kabbalistic elucidation of the same
subject; (4) the notion of the Left-Hand Path, its correlation with the doc-
trine of reversal, and its misrepresentation as black magic; and finally, (5)
Grant’s interpretation of the Tantra and its relation to the sexual magick
associated with Crowley and his Ordo Templi Orientis (OTO), of which
Grant considered himself, erroneously,19 to be the leader.
East-West Similarity and the Draconian Tradition
One of the fundamental elements in what he perceives as the similarity
between esoteric traditions of the East and the West is predicated upon
Grant’s understanding of the idea of tradition. As already mentioned, it
is possible to discern both interesting parallels and significant differences
between the Grantian and, for example, the Guénonian approach to this
notion. Grant’s thinking on the subject is deeply rooted in the occultist
imaginary, and it shares in its appreciation of India and the East a good
deal of similarity with the Theosophy, from which it however differs due
to his positive valorization of magick and the “Left-Hand” path.
Grant is a proponent of what he designates as the Typhonian or Dra-
conian tradition.20 A major influence on Grant in this respect is the
British speculative Egyptologist Gerald Massey (1828–1907). In Grant’s

exposition, the Draconian tradition, “the most ancient in the world”
(1973: 109),21 which had its historical roots in Africa and which the
Egyptians formally elaborated into a system, “flowered later in the tantras
of India, Mongolia, China, and Tibet” (1972: 37). According to Grant,
this tradition focused on the worship of stellar, lunar, and feminine divin-
ities.22 “The Sabean Goddess Typhon with her brood of seven souls or
stars preceded the lunar mysteries” (1975a: 52), which were at the cen-
ter of the cult.23 This was met with vicious hostility by the followers of
later, solar and patriarchal cults.24 As part of their hostile propaganda,
the Osirians or Solarites (as Grant calls them) painted the Draconian tra-
dition in morally negative terms, as evil, corrupt, and debauched, and
as a consequence they regarded these older mysteries with horror. The
Typhonian monuments “were mutilated and scarified by the adherents
of the later Solar cults who abhorred all reminders of the sabean [i.e.,
Draconian/Typhonian] origins of their theology” (Grant, 1972: 73). The
tradition reappeared, after its long decline, “in obscure alchemical writ-
ings in the West, and in Oriental occult tantras” (Grant, 1994: 1).
The distinctive trait of the thus conceived Draconian tradition is the
worship of feminine divinities. The main reason for that, aside from
the stellar metaphysical speculations,25 in other words, when related to
earthly women, is of sexual and physiological (biochemical) nature.26
The tradition as such engages sex as a method of spiritual achievement.27
It emerges very clearly from Grant’s opus that the primary reason for
this orientation does not relate to the hedonistic aspect of sex or the
pursuit of orgasmic bliss.28 The main purpose behind sexual magick is,
properly speaking, alchemical—if by that designation we understand the
transmutation of material substances into their spiritual equivalents. Its
rationale rests on the following set of ideas. The true secrets of magick
are bodily.29 These secrets relate to sexual secretions. The most important
secretion is the menstrual blood, which is either ambrosial in itself or it
becomes such when commingled with the male sperm. Thus the links,
which relate the esotericism of the East with that of the West, according
to Grant, have their roots in the Draconian tradition of sexual magick. I
propose to return to the elucidation of this subject at a later point, when
discussing Grant’s particular approach to Tantra and sex magick.
Grant and Advaita
Grant is a consistent proponent of the advaitan worldview. As already
mentioned, a major influence on him in this regard was the famous

[Page 104]
sECrETs Of THE TyPHONIAN TANTrA 97
twentieth-century South Indian mystic Ramana Maharshi,30 who was
a subject of several early articles by Grant. The philosophy of advaita
argues that only the Self [that is, ātman, identical with brahman] truly
exists, everything else being simply a world play, which is another way
of saying that everything else is a duality (dvaita), and from that vantage
point not ontologically real. The essence of that truly and solely exist-
ing Self is equated with consciousness, of which the phenomenal world
is a projection.31 In accordance with the general trend of Indian meta-
physics, Grant considers the unsatisfactory nature of human existence,
the “thralldom of existence,” to be a result of the epistemological error
committed by identifying with the projections or modifications of that
One Consciousness. “The cause of the mystery, glamour, or ignorance as
the Buddhist call it, is the initial and mistaken identification of the Self
with its objects” (Grant, 1977: 24). Expressed alternatively, human bond-
age and consequent sufferings are due to the bewitching power that the
external universe exerts over us.
It would appear, from Grant’s writings, that he understands the mysti-
cal path, or meditation on the subject-as-consciousness, associated with
the god Śiva, as the typically Eastern form of inquiry that leads to enlight-
enment, while the Western, magical method, consists of the meditation
on the world of forms associated with Śakti. In an important passage,
Grant explains the rationale, according to which this latter method effec-
tively leads to liberation, by stating that
by “doing the true will,” i.e. letting all parts of the picture fulfill themselves
evenly without let or hindrance, we come to see the illusoriness of the pic-
ture and its mirage-like beauty cast in the shining waters of the Unmoving
Shiva. For only by withdrawing to a coign of vantage without the picture
itself, and refusing to identify the Self with the ego playing therein, can
the picture be seen in its entirety and as a limited and separate and wholly
illusory representation of the thoughts and desires which form a perpetual
river of unhindered dream. (2006: 68–9)
The world is thus, in Grant’s understanding, a projection of the Self.
As a projection, lacking an inherent ontological grounding, being only
an appearance, the world is also a magical illusion. Nevertheless, due to
its dependency on the real Self as the source of the projection, to study
this magical world is thus paradoxically tantamount to the process of
self-inquiry.32 The elaborate mapping of the phenomenal world, includ-
ing its occult, subtle, and what Grant would habitually designate as
“transmundane” or “extraterrestrial” aspects, thus amounts to a complex

[Page 105]

act of self-discovery.33 By mastering the world through the mastering of
magick, one becomes liberated, realizing that only the Self exists, while
its projections are, by the same token and in that sense, unreal.34 The jus-
tification for an engagement with the world, understood as a projection,
for the purpose of discovering the Self is, according to Grant (2006: 13),
contained in the conviction that unless and “before we understand and
identify the true nature of this Self, in both its dreaming and its waking
states, we are merely carried along by the seemingly chance experiences
that arise in our consciousness.”
In an important passage, while discussing the relationship between
the true will and false will, Grant argues that there exists in fact no divi-
sion in the will, which is unitary and exists beyond the phenomenal uni-
verse. The phenomenal will is compared to the reflection of the face in
the mirror: only one face truly exists, but the reflected image, although
illusory in itself, is still a reflection of something real. Grant suggests that
the true nature of this reflection can be found only by tracing it back to
its source, and that this can be done only by realizing the identity of the
being that is reflected in the mirror. This process, the inquiry into the
nature of the Self through the process of ātma vicāra, is the only method,
according to Grant (1994: 107), that leads to discovering the nature of the
true will, in other words, the nature of Thelema.
If the ontological truth of existence lies in its unity and oneness, the
experience of duality constitutes a problem that must be effectively dealt
with. The well-known advaitan position associated with the philosophy
.
of Ādi Śankara (788–820 CE) suggests that the perception of reality as
consisting of both the subject and the object rests on the misapprehen-
sion engendered by the force of māyā, for only the subject truly exists.
Grant’s position is not consistently articulated in the same manner, but it
frequently aligns itself with the tantric attitude, which homologizes the
dual aspect of existence with the divine pair of Śiva and Śakti, whose
erotic embrace provides the means of ontological union (what may be,
with a grain of salt, compared to the Western principle of coincidentia
oppositorum). In Grant’s (1972: 151) formulation, the sexual act consti-
tutes a sacrament possessing a “peculiar virtue since it effects a transfor-
mation of consciousness through the annihilation of apparent duality.”
It may be added that in Indian thought this notion—the analogy of the
sexual embrace and its associated rapture with the experience of the onto-
logical unity that is the essential attribute of brahman, the true ground of
existence—is very ancient, with its locus classicus in the Vedic Upanisads
(Brhadāranyaka Upanisad 4:3:21).

The fundamental aspect of Grant’s advaitic position concerns his
privileging of consciousness as the only true reality. The world is onto-
logically nondual because in its ultimate nature it consists of a singular
consciousness. However, although essentially one, consciousness mani-
fests through several modes or states, each possessing its own set of oper-
ative laws, and each appearing as real in itself, within its own domain.
These are the state of waking consciousness, the state of dreams, and the
state of deep sleep without dreams. Grant is very consistent in relating
these states to their Sanskrit designations (jāgrat, svapna, and susupti
respectively). (He is less consistent in his references to the fourth, tran-
scendental, state of consciousness, turīya, so that he occasionally con-
flates its properties with those of the state of deep sleep without dreams.)
His treatment of this subject is a good indicator of Grant’s general modus
operandi, which is to say that while he remains generally correct in his
referencing and understanding of the source material, he at the same
time displays often controversial inventiveness in the application and
elaboration of this same material, as will be shown in what follows.
The account of the four states of consciousness is already found in

The Notion of Emptiness in Eastern Metaphysical and
Western Kabbalistic and Magical Representations
In addition to his nondualist view of reality, Grant also consistently argues
that the unmanifest, the void, and the formless stand for the true source
of phenomena, and that, paradoxical though it may sound, nonbeing is
in fact the real Being. As might be expected, in order to provide doctrinal
support for this thesis, Grant often makes references to Buddhist teach-
ings, where the notion of emptiness or void (śūnyatā) is elaborated at
great length, being one of the hallmarks of the system. As elsewhere in
his opus, Grant brings Eastern teachings into correlations with what he
perceives to be their Western magical and kabbalistic counterparts, while
the whole is forged so as to fit his own idiosyncratic vision.
Stripped down to its ultimate essential nature, the one truly exist-
ing reality is the reality of consciousness, which in its own form is no
form, beyond form, void.41 This void is sometimes represented by the
goddess Kālī, who is considered to correspond to the Thelemic goddess
of space, Nuit.42 Nuit, conceived of as the infinite space, is also absolute
consciousness. The symbol of Nuit is a circle; the circle is zero; thus Nuit
stands for void, the sole reality.43 “Void . . . exists at the heart of Matter,
beyond Time and Space and the delusions of individuality” (Grant,
1980: 175). Nonbeing, the negative, the noumenal is the Reality under-
lying the p henomenal world (Grant, 1977: 27–8). Reality is Nonbeing
(Grant, 1977: 26).
The phenomenal world is the world of illusion, characterized by the
split between the subject and the object, dominated by the ego, “the
source of the conceptual universe” (Grant, 1977: 49).44 To attain the true
reality thus implies the dissolution of the ego, and the formula to achieve
this is “the formula of initiation into the Real, i.e. the anti-universe or
world of non-being, the negative, the Ain” (49).45 Naturally, the ego
dreads such a prospect, and in its attempt to circumvent its dissolution,
it portrays the realm of nonbeing in morally and aesthetically negative
terms. Grant (142) claims that the original mythmakers misunderstood
the nature of nonbeing, and for that reason they represented it as evil.
This is an important assertion, arguing as it does that there is a disparity
between the experience of the ontological void and its subsequent (mis)

[Page 108]

representation in moralistic language. Anchoring his position in these
observations, Grant (243–4) makes a bold and controversial step in argu-
ing that “black magic” in fact relates to the world of nonbeing, and as
such is not evil but only appears so to the constricted (and constructed)
sense of individuality.
One major exception to this negative portrayal of ontological empti-
ness (śūnyatā) and the lack of individual substantiality (anātman/anatta)
is found, as already indicated, in Buddhist teachings. “The Buddhist
doctrine of Anatta, alone of the ‘old aeon’ doctrines, expresses . . . total
transcendence of duality. Nagarjuna elaborated its implications in the
Madhyamaka philosophy” (Grant, 1994: 63).46 Grant insists that Crow-
ley himself failed to properly understand the full implication of these
doctrines, but he also adds (73) that one has to keep in mind that when
Crowley wrote on Buddhism (which was rather early in his career), “cer-
tain vital Mahayana texts in English translation were not available. I refer
particularly to the texts of the Prajnaparamita school.” In accord with
the general esoteric tendency toward the practice of concordance, Grant
(1992: 256) suggests that Mādhyamaka represents not only the most pro-
found form of Buddhist teaching but also “the highest Advaitic doctrines
of India and China.”
The ontological void, as the noumenal world and the source and
matrix of the phenomenal world, is also analogically related to woman
and her sexual organ. We have already come across statements that god-
desses such as Kālī and Nuit symbolize absolute consciousness, which is
void. Grant also claims that the Scarlet Woman, one of the officers of the
New Aeon in the doctrine of Thelema, being an earthly representative of
Nuit, provides the gateway to the void.47 More specifically, it is the female
sexual organ that represents this gateway. “The vagina is . . . the Vessel of
the Void that conveys the Word—to Man—from the Voice of the Silence”
(Grant, 1980: 247). Perhaps the key example that illustrates Grant’s train
of thought on this subject is the one in which he correlates the “flower-
strewn yoni” (i.e., vagina) of the woman participating in tantric worship
with the śmāśanam, the cremation ground where the Left-Hand Path
(vāmācara) rites are performed.48 “This is the burning-ground of desire,
the place in which desires are consumed, extinguished. Hence, Kali is
said to grant K aivalya (Liberation) in the Void (Yoni)” (Grant, 1972: 149).
The consummation of desire that takes place in the vagina, the eradica-
tion of interest in the phenomenal world that follows the experience of
total orgasm, opens up the gateway to the void and its voice of silence.49
Grant (150) expresses this view in his inimitable style: “The desires of the

An additional conceptual thread that is of relevance to Grant’s treat-
ment of the void nature of reality concerns the already discussed state
of deep sleep without dreams that stands as a final frontier before the
ultimate, the fourth and transcendental state of consciousness, which
Indian tradition designates by the name turīya. In the already quoted
paragraph from his 1954 essay, Grant defines this state as “The Void =
The Sole Reality = The Self = The Limitless Light of the Western Tradi-
tion.”51 In an earlier essay, published in 1953,52 this empty state of pure
consciousness bereft of all mentation is equated with brahman, which is
in accord with the traditional Hindu understanding of the nature of the
state of turīya. Much less traditional is Grant’s later exegesis in which
he suggests that the Self’s innate powers, seen with the concept-ridden
mind, appear theomorphically as “Old Ones,” “Outer Ones,” and “Deep
Ones.”53 They appear hideous, horrendous, and unacceptable to the mind
in its waking state “because of the conscious mind’s inability to conceive
immense and formless energy in any but terrible configurations” (1999:
29). Seen from this perspective, Grant’s interest in the Left-Hand Path
and “dark” doctrines is justified as an effort to penetrate into the state of
pure and void consciousness that lies beyond the veil of monstrosity. The
only means that leads to this state involves the reversal of the traditional
magical method of invocation.54 In order to explore this subject more
fully, we turn our attention to the mysteries of “black magic,” the reversal,
and the Left-Hand Path.
Left-Hand Path
The concept of the Left-Hand Path with its associated range of practices
constitutes a leitmotif in Grant’s work to such a degree that he claimed
that the restoration of this form of magick and mysticism represents one
of his primary concerns.55 In Western esoteric traditions, the term itself
is regularly used in a derogatory sense, especially by H. P. Blavatsky and
Theosophists but also by Crowley, and in these accounts it semantically
amounts to an alternative designation for “black magic” and similar
deviations from the Right-Hand Path, which is supposed to lead to the
rightful goal of spiritual life. Grant’s handling of these two terms typically
makes reference to their conventional use in Indian tantric parlance, so
that he often uses the Sanskrit terms vāma mārga and daksina mārga,
and emphasizes that these fundamentally refer to nothing more than

[Page 110]
sECrETs Of THE TyPHONIAN TANTrA 103
two diverse modes of practice without inherent moral validity attached
to either of them. That being said, it should be pointed out that Grant
acknowledges that even in India the Left-Hand Path tends to be confused
with black magic.
According to his own admission, Grant was in 1946 admitted into an
occult Order, in which he received “an initiated interpretation of tant-
ric methods relating to the Vama Marg, or Left-Hand Path, which deals
with the highly secret worship of the Primal Goddess, or Devi” (1972: 2).
Throughout the Typhonian Trilogies, he makes frequent allusions to the
Kaula branch of vāmācārins (that is, followers of the Left-Hand Path),
and he places an enormous emphasis on “an invaluable contemporary
Kaula Comment on ancient Tantric rites” (Grant, 1973: 2), so that it
is a safe conjecture that the above-mentioned Order is of Kaula prov-
enance.56 Grant does not provide documentary evidence of his admis-
sion into this Order, which is to be expected and in itself in no way
precludes the authenticity of his assertion. It is significant, however, that
the instigation of the Kaulas in India is attributed to the legendary guru
Matsyendranāth, who indeed placed the worship of female deities at the
center of the cult.57 Grant similarly highlights the worship of the female
deities as the essential feature of the Left-Hand Path, just as was the case
with the Draconian tradition,58 and by the same token, as the principal
reason of its denigration. According to him,
the main cause of the vilification of the Left Hand Path by the adherents of
the solar and later cults—even to the present day—is due to its connection
with the feminine aspect of the Creative Principle. It is the sex-magical use
of Woman in the rites of the Left hand Path that has made it universally
suspect. (1975a: 2)
I propose to return to the subject of the “sex-magical use of Woman”
in the following section and to focus here on some other aspects of the
Left-Hand Path. Grant’s manner of distinguishing between the two Paths
is worthy of note. He suggests that the Left-Hand Path represents the eso-
teric aspect of tantric practice, the Right-Hand Path being exoteric in its
mode of worship.59 In accordance with the general tendency of his writ-
ing, Grant connects the esoteric with a focus on the body and its myster-
ies. He thus claims that representatives of the Left-Hand Path worship
the Goddess at the level of the lowest, the mūlādhāra cakra, otherwise
associated with the element of earth and the material plane,60 while those
of the Right-Hand Path utilize the “current of light” (that is, kundalinī) at
the level of the highest cakra (sahasrāra, the position of which is in fact

above and outside the body).61 It follows, by extension, that the former
mode of practice is also typical of the path of magick, and the latter of the
path of mysticism.62
In the Left-Hand Path practice the actual woman is worshipped, and in
the Right-Hand Path a symbolic representation in the form of a śrī cakra
diagram; in the former the kundalinī is awakened by physical means, and
in the latter by mental means.63 Left-Hand Path initiates use actual kalās

Right-Hand Path initiates use the substitutes.64 More examples could be
given, but the foregoing should suffice to make Grant’s position clear: he
gives preference to the Left-Hand Path on the basis of its privileging of
the occult power inherent in the subtle properties of the human body.
It needs underscoring that this agrees with our current understanding of
the Kaula school of medieval India, which similarly privileged the human
body and the powers of actual sexual fluids used in their rituals, a process
that later became superseded by the incorporation of symbolical substi-
tutes and which David Gordon White calls the “semanticization” of sex
in tantric practice.65
On several occasions, Grant translates the term vāma (i.e., left) as
“thrown out” and “unclean.” Quoting from the already mentioned con-
temporary Kaula comment, he writes that the Left-Hand Path refers to
“the use of things generally considered unclean, namely the excretions
of the human body; the use of things that provoke disgust.”66 This meth-
odological option, the handling of substances that are ordinarily “con-
sidered unclean” and that “provoke disgust,” is a well-known feature of
tantric practice. This particular style of practice is in its turn but an aspect
of the wider ideological orientation, which is often glossed as a “practice
of reversal,” or, in the parlance of the Nāth Yogis, the ultā sādhana. From
this point of view, a yogi, and in particular a Left-Hand Path tāntrika,
goes “against the grain,” in the opposite direction from the rest of the
world. For the general tendency of the world is toward its own decay
and death, followed by endless rounds of equally useless existence, ad
nauseam. To liberate oneself from such a fate, an activity oriented in the
opposite direction is required, and this finds its expression—as one pos-
sible methodological option—in doing those things that are ordinarily
rejected, feared, considered unclean, and so forth.
Grant habitually glosses this practice of reversal by the Sanskrit term
viparīta karani. He (1973: 232) defines the term as the “total reversion
of the senses by presenting them with things and ideas from which,
under ordinary circumstances, they would recoil.” In a manner that is

tantric concept with what he considers the Western magical representa-
tion of the process of reversal as illustrated on the Tarot card the Hanged
Man, which portrays a man hanging upside-down with one foot tied to
a branch of a tree or a similar object.68 Grant likewise considers that the
Egyptian goddess Nut—designated as Nuit in The Book of the Law—and
in the form represented in the so-called Stelé of Revealing,69 also depicts
the viparītamudrā (the posture of reversal) with her arched body, and
both her feet and hands touching the ground.70 Following Crowley, he
considers this representation of the overarched Goddess as an indication
of the sexual position in which the woman is on top of her partner, and
which Grant deems a suitable posture in order for the man to partake of
her sexual fluids.71
The most radical elaboration of the practice of reversal is arguably rep-
resented by Grant’s explorations of the negative or averse side of the Tree
of Life. The subject is most prominently, although by no means exclu-
sively, treated in Nightside of Eden (Grant, 1977). Traditional occultism
utilizes the glyph of the Tree of Life, consisting of 10 circles or sephiroth
(associated with numbers one through ten) and 22 paths (associated with
the letters of the Hebrew alphabet), to represent the totality of macro-
cosmic and microcosmic phenomena. Outside the Tree proper, repre-
sented as situated below the lowest sephira, are the so-called qliphoth, the
“shells” associated with negative, or even demonic, forces. Grant (1977:
1) argues that a full magical initiation necessitates the exploration of both
the light and the darkness and that initiation is consequently “not possi-
ble without an understanding of the so-called qlipothic paths which are,
in practice, as real as any object illumined by the sun.” These explorations
of the averse side of the Tree of Life eventually led to the proposition that
they in fact constitute the proper object of the Left-Hand Path, which
as such stands ontologically prior to its Right-Hand counterpart. Grant’s
argument for this is that the proper sphere of the Left-Hand Path is the
noumenal world of the true being, which is paradoxically nonbeing. The
Right-Hand Path thus deals with the world of phenomena, which is ulti-
mately illusory. “There is no objective reality, but there is the manifesta-
tion of non-manifestation; the shadow of being that is cast by non-being”
(Grant, 1977: 52–3).
The curious and rather original implication of the above position con-
sists of the suggestion—which Grant does not state explicitly but which
follows inherently from his arguments—that the Left-Hand Path, the
worship of the feminine creative principle, the handling of forbidden and

Tantra and Sex Magick
The subject of Tantra forms a consistent motif in Grant’s writings.
As already mentioned, by his own admittance (1972: 2) he was initiated in
1946 into an “occult Order where [he] received an initiated interpretation
of tantric methods relating to the Vama Marg, or Left Hand Path, which
deals with the highly secret worship of the Primal Godess, or Devi.” Grant
(1973: 3) attaches a great significance to “an invaluable contemporary
Kaula Comment on ancient Tantric rites,” elsewhere (1999: xi) identified
as “a contemporary Comment on an ancient Tantric work, Anandalahari,”
.
which is traditionally attributed to Ādi Śankara (788–820 CE). We have
already seen that according to Grant’s view, the Asian Tantric systems rep-
resent a branch and continuation of the Draconian tradition, of which the
Thelemic magick instigated by Crowley is the most recent manifestation.
It is thus significant that already in the initial volume of the Typhonian
Trilogies, Grant (1972: 7) refers to the founding document of Thelema,
The Book of the Law,72 as “the New Gnosis, the latest Tantra, the most
complex Grimoire.” Of equal significance is also the statement (Grant,
1972: 131) that “[t]he revival of tantric elements in The Book of the Law
may be evidence of a positive move on the part of Aiwaz73 to forge a link
between Western and Oriental systems of magick.”74 In order to elucidate
Grant’s rationale for the correlation he draws between Tantra, Thelema,
and the Draconian tradition, an elaboration is in order.
At the most obvious level, and in particular with his constant refer-
ences to Crowley and his Ordo Templi Orientis (OTO)75 teachings, it is
clear that Grant connects Tantra with sexual magick. At first glance, it
would appear that Grant is thus perpetuating a common Western mis-
identification between Tantra and sexuality. The matter is, however, more
complex than that. To begin with, Grant’s primary interest (as Crowley’s)

is not in the sexual act itself but in the ambrosial and occult properties
of, particularly female, sexual fluids. This is as consonant with (especially
early) tantric practices76 as it is dissimilar from the typical Western New
Age pursuit of the “orgasmic bliss.”77 Grant’s insistence that the corporeal,
and not the mental, is of primary interest in magick squares with the
typical Tantric privileging of the human body and its occult powers.78
And finally, a very important link between Tantric and Western magical
systems concerns the already discussed notion of the Left-Hand Path,
which also includes the practice of reversal (viparīta karani) as a method
of attainment, and which, as we have seen, in Grant’s case becomes asso-
ciated with speculations about the reverse side of the Tree of Life and
related issues.
In his emphasis on the superior value of female sexual secretions,
which he typically glosses as kalas (Skt. kalā),79 Grant parts ways with
Crowley. Grant maintains that Crowley’s understanding of the tantric
practice was incomplete and that he in consequence erroneously equated
the bindu—the “drop” of sexual fluid possessing ambrosial qualities—
with the male seed.80 Grant on the contrary suggests (1973: 45) that the
priestess in the tantric cult “is the sole source of the supreme Elixir, the
virgin whore of heaven who sheds her star-light without direct sexual
contact with the priest or any other male member of the Circle.” (I inter-
pret the term “star-light” as a gloss for either menstrual blood or vaginal
secretion.) It should be borne in mind that this position is in accordance
with a particularly early phase of Hindu and Buddhist Tantric practice,
in which
the role of the female consort is vital, because the clan fluid (kula-dra-
vya) or clan nectar (kulāmrta), vulval essence (yoni-tattva), or thought of

womb. Because she is herself the embodiment of the energy of the god-
head (or Wisdom, the complement to male Skill in Means), her sexual or
menstrual discharge is considered to be the germ plasm of the godhead or
enlightened consciousness itself. (White, 2000: 16)

An important further clarification is in order. Similar to Crowley’s
insistence that both mystical (meditative) and magical (ritual) aspects of
the Great Work need to be equally pursued as the two sides of the same
coin, Grant relates the operations of sexual magick to two ends. In the
instance of mystical orientation, the physical contact between the Priest-
ess and the Priest is not established and the result of the ritual “manifests
in, and as, formless states of consciousness which lead on to the ultimate
goal of Advaita” (Grant, 1973: 44). Alternatively, if the operation is con-
ducted with the purpose of effecting material manifestation of its desired
intent, in other words, if desire is brought into the equation—since
“desire can culminate in the state of freedom, or desirelessness, only after
it has discharged its potential energy in the form of its own image”—the
outcome will be the projection of the Will (desire) in the world of form:
“magical as distinct from mystical, light or perceptible as opposed to dark
or imperceptible” (Grant, 1977: 227).
Grant’s manner of dealing with the symbolism of light is interesting:
darkness stands for the state of nonduality because there is no “other”—no
object as distinct from subject—that can be perceived. In a similar man-
ner, Giuseppe Tucci (1961: 18), commenting on Vasubandhu’s Trimśikā,
notes that “when there is something perceptible, there is a perceiver, but
when the perceptible is not there . . . there is implicit the non-existence
of perceiver, not only of the perception.” It should be noted that Grant
(1973: 44) also relates the aforementioned type of darkness to the per-
ception of “the interplay of Hadit and Nuit (or Shiva and Shakti), whose
explosive union is so blinding in its brilliance that it appears as ‘thick
darkness’ and ‘sudden death.’” Crowley has in a similar vein interpreted
the symbolic properties of the color black and its association with both
the Thelemic goddess Babalon and her correlation with the Hindu god-
