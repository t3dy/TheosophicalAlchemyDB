The current popularity of Asian spiritual traditions in the West is a com-
plex phenomenon that cannot be reduced to any one specific factor. Never-
theless, it is generally assumed that despite its many faults and confusions,
the Theosophical movement significantly helped make this phenomenon
possible.3 Stated more generally, the transmission of Buddhism, Yoga,
Tantra, and other Asian spiritual systems was, at least to a degree, facili-
tated by Western esoteric traditions. An important segment of these tra-
ditions is constituted by the occult groups that inherited and developed
further the systematized model of ritual magic from the Hermetic Order
of Golden Dawn, of which Crowley’s Thelema and his syncretic system
of magick are the prime examples. The significant influence of Crow-
ley, Fortune, Grant, and other individuals studied in this book upon the
twentieth century and contemporary Western esotericism on the one
hand, and the influence of esotericism on popular culture on the other
hand, suggests that some of the ideas about Yoga and Tantra entered into
the popular consciousness through these particular channels.4 Western
occultists welcomed Asian spirituality, incorporated it into their models
of thought and practice, and in the process transformed it. They both
generated and sustained an interest in the religious traditions of the East
and thus served a contributing role in the phenomenon of the “trans-
lation of cultures.” After all and arguably, the antinomian heart, which
lies at the core of the renunciatory and “against-the-grain” traditions of
South Asian spirituality, beats more fully in its occultist forms studied
here—with its representatives who more or less all occupy a marginal
and countercultural space, and who often either flout social norms or
advocate their transcendence—than in the numerous Yoga studios and
tantric workshops that mushroom throughout the cities of the consum-
erist West.
