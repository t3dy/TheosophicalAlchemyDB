# Gordan Djurdjevic India and the Occult_ The Influence of South Asian Spirituality on Modern Western Occultism Palgrave Macm

## Section: Introduction

Introduction
There is a great deal of difference between the theory and practice of
Indian Tantra and the sexual magick associated with Aleister Crowley.1
Hugh Urban has suggested on several occasions that a curious outcome
of Crowley’s influence on Western esoteric traditions lies precisely in
the (re)interpretation of tantric teachings as being centered on the ritual
use of sex. Contemporary Western “neo-Tantra” seems to be even more
emphatically oriented toward the pursuit of prolonged orgasmic plea-
sure, an attitude that often provokes outright dismissals of this type of
spirituality as inauthentic and based on misunderstanding. Nevertheless,
there is an interesting parallel, a sort of mirroring, between ideas about
the properties of human seed in certain traditions of tantric Yoga and in
Crowley’s teachings. In both cases, the male seed is considered to have
great magical power and, in the final instance, ambrosial attributes. But
the reflected image is upside down: while the yogis attempt to sublimate
the sperm by forcing it to traverse upwards from the genitals to the head,
where it turns into elixir of immortality, in Crowley’s case the sperm

moves downward, it is ejected, and is made use of as elixir in its material
form.
In other words, the yogis—and I have in mind particularly the Nāth
Siddha yogis (vide infra)—are predominantly engaged in the transfor-
mation of the semen from the gross into the subtle form. The method
thus consists of the spiritualization of the material: in Western alchemi-
cal terms, and with respect to the famous formula of solve et coagula, this
process may be designated as solve. It may also be considered as the path
of the serpent, which may be related to the “serpent power,” kundalinī,
which—when “awakened”—“rises” from the lowest cakra at the base of
the spine to the top of the head. In Crowley’s case, conversely, the mate-
rial form of the semen is sanctified as a form of Eucharist and taken,
among other things, as a symbolic representation of the natural human
body that has become divine.2 We may interpret this other method as
the materialization of the spiritual, or, in alchemical terms, coagula. This
may also be designated as the path of the dove, which may be related to
the descending dove represented on the seal of Crowley’s magical order
Ordo Templi Orientis (OTO). In what follows, my intention is to explore
formal characteristics and implications of this thematic parallel and these
methodological differences.
Solve: The Nāth Siddhas and the Path of the Serpent
The order (sampradāya) of North Indian hatha yogis (with tantric con-
nections) known as the Nāth Siddhas was founded in the early medieval
period. Their lineage particularly venerates two early and semilegendary
adepts, Matsyendranāth (who possibly lived in the eighth or the ninth
century) and Gorakhnāth (who probably lived in the twelfth century).
An important aspect of their teaching lies in the notion that the human
seed originates in the cranial vault, at which place it exists in the subtle
form of the elixir of immortality, amrta. Unfortunately, this precious
liquid drips continually down the spinal column and eventually gets
destroyed in gastric fire or through ejaculation.3 The Nāth yogis believe
that this process is the principal cause of disease, old age, and dying. Thus
we read in The Sayings of Gorakh, a collection of vernacular poetry attrib-
uted to Gorakhnāth, the following description of the consequences to
which the dissipation of the semen leads: “The legs become shaky, the
stomach loose / And the [hair on the] head [white] like a heron’s wings.”4
The first necessary step to counteract this process lies in the adoption of

a celibate lifestyle. As a consequence, the Nāths embrace an antisexual
and sometimes outright misogynic attitude, as evidenced in the follow-
ing statements:
The vagina is a vampire. The vagina is a vampire.
Without teeth, she has devoured the whole world.
The wise have saved their head with wisdom;
The [ordinary] people have forgotten their own selves.5
Celibacy, however, is not in and of itself the final goal of the Nāth yogis,
but only its preliminary requirement. The most important work consists
of the process of reversal, ultā sādhana, by which the sperm, or bindu,
needs to return to its place of origin in the head. This is achieved through
the regime of Yoga, consisting of postures, āsanas, breathing exercises,
prānāyāma, muscular contractions, bandhas, the chanting of mantras,
and the practice of meditation, dhyāna.6 The successful completion of
the process of reversal results in the transformation of the sperm back
into the elixir of immortality, which causes the yogi to become godlike,
“a second Śiva.” His body becomes divine, divya deha, and he obtains
immortality, amrtatva, and miraculous powers, siddhis, which render
him an accomplished adept, siddha. As Dasgupta (1969: 246) has stated,
“[I]t has been emphatically declared in all texts of yoga that he, who has
been able to give an upward flow to the [seminal] fluid is a god, and not
a man.”
As has been observed by several scholars, the basic operative prin-
ciple of the Yoga associated with the Nāth Siddhas represents an appli-
cation and internalization of the Indian alchemy, rasāyana. Indian
tradition relates mercury and sulfur, the two major (al)chemical sub-
stances comparable to the Western materia prima, to the divine cou-
ple Śiva and Śakti. When internalized, these chemical elements are
identified with the sperm, bindu, which corresponds to Śiva, and the
menstrual blood, rajas, corresponding to Śakti.7 Śiva’s dwelling place,
within the esoteric geography of the human body, is situated in the
head, or to be more precise, in the sahasrāra cakra, the topmost cen-
ter of occult energy. Śakti lies dormant in the mūlādhāra cakra, at the
base of the spine. The principle task of the yogi is to make these two
divinities unite, which attainment results in the liberation from funda-
mental duality, dvandva,8 which is understood as the cause of suffer-
ing, duhkha, which is based on ignorance, avidya. Thus Gorakhnāth
urges his disciples: “Take Śiva and Śakti and make them meet,”9 while

he expresses his own achievement by stating, “Joining Śiva and Śakti, I
have remained within my own house.”10 Similarly, we read in an already
quoted Sanskrit text,11 also attributed to Gorakhnāth:

Semen is associated with the Moon, and menstrual blood is associated

the knower of Yoga.12
Based on the above attributions, it would appear that a natural method
of uniting Śiva with Śakti lies in the commingling of the semen with
menstrual blood. It is quite possible that this methodological orienta-
tion was characteristic of the early phase of Tantra, associated with the
cult of the divine yoginīs and the Kaula tradition of Matsyendranāth.13
In fact, as David Gordon White has persuasively argued, this early phase
of Tantra14 was based on the lineage tradition that involved, as the core
element of initiation, the consummation of the “clan nectar,” kulāmrta,
issuing from the nether “mouth” of the female yoginī.15 The later Nāth
tradition, however, is predominantly androcentric and misogynic.16 The
focus of attention becomes the male seed, bindu,17 and its transforma-
tion into the elixir. The bindu is invested with miraculous powers: it is
considered the root of the body, the essence of Yoga, and the provider of
immortality—but only, and this is an important qualification, if it is not
wasted, if it is not ejaculated in the act of sex. This is so because bindu is
the elixir only if brought back from the genitals to the top of the head.
For, once it has trickled down to the genitals, it has lost its ambrosial
value.
The properties and transformations of the bindu are closely related to
other constituents of the yogi’s subtle body (sūksma śarīra), such as the
kundalinī18 and the cakras. The latter, as is well known, are the subtle cen-
ters of occult energy, situated along the spinal column, usually consid-
ered to be four, five, or seven in number.19 Each cakra is associated with
a cluster of corresponding properties and, among other things, each one
carries an elementary attribution. The lowest cakra is associated with the
element earth, the one above it with water, then follows fire, and finally
air. Above these, in the region of the “third eye” is the cakra correspond-
ing with the space or ether, ākāśa. The highest cakra is the dwelling place
of Śiva. As the cakras rise up along the spine, their properties become
more and more subtle. The spiritual progress of a yogi follows precisely

this path of “involution,”20 or sublimation, so that the typical method of
practice starts with the yogi meditating on and thus identifying with the
lowest cakra, while the ultimate goal is to reach and “awaken” the highest
one, and in this manner to acquire a divine body and become equal to the
god Śiva. In other words, the yogi initially identifies with the bindu21 by
focusing attention at the level corresponding to the material realm, and
from there the direction is to proceed upwards and toward more subtle
levels of existence and identity. From earthly body toward divine, spiri-
tual body: the method is solve.
The Nāth yogis consistently glorify the powers of the bindu. “Bindu is
verily Yoga,” claims Gorakhnāth. “The one who knows the mystery of this
bindu, / He is creator, he is god.”22 But the typical leitmotif is that the flow
of semen has to be reversed. Consequently, Gorakh advises that “The
lower Ganges should be brought up to the [top of the] world,”23 a meta-
phorical rendition of the principle of seminal reversal. On the “top of the
world,” in the “circle of the sky,” in other words, in the highest cakra, the
bindu resides in the pool of nectar, which the adept yogi internally drinks
and thus obtains magical powers, including immortality. “In the circle
of the sky is an upside-down well. / There is the residence of nectar. / He
who has a guru can drink it all.”24 The yogi who has successfully accom-
plished this feat transcends in his status even the gods, or as Gorakhnāth
puts it: “Even Visnu washes the feet / Of the yogi who holds above what
[otherwise] goes below.”25
According to the well-known legend of the Nāth lore, Gorakhnāth’s
guru, Matsyendranāth, temporarily lost his yogic way of life by suc-
cumbing to the powers of the queen of the “land of women.”26 As a
result, Matsyendranāth, a “fallen yogi,” is often depicted as an old man,
the inevitable outcome of his waste of semen in the embrace of a woman.
Contrary to that, Gorakhnāth is typically represented as a youth, a
“child Gorakh,” Gorakh bālak. In fact, one of the most significant verses
in the Sayings of Gorakh alludes to Gorakhnāth as a child who delivers
yogic sermons from the “summit of the sky,” in other words, from the
sahāsrāra cakra.27 Thus, in a certain sense, the “child Gorakh” dwells
esoterically at the highest level of human mind-body complex, which
is otherwise accessed by, and after a manner identical with, the semen
that has returned to its place of origin, and to its condition of elixir,
at the “summit of the sky.”28 In other words, it is possible in at least
one metaphoric level to interpret the “child Gorakh” as an anthropo-
morphic image of the human seed in its highest, divine aspect. We will
subsequently see that Crowley also considers the human sperm in both

anthropomorphic and theomorphic registers. It hardly merits mention-
ing that the operating mechanism, which makes possible this chain of
associations, rests on the esoteric notion of correspondence (in Sanskrit,

the bindu.
To summarize: the Nāth Siddhas attribute miraculous powers to the
male seed, but only under the condition that it has been sublimated,
returned to the top of the head, and transformed from its gross mate-
rial condition into the subtle form of the elixir. Thus, in addition to gen-
der disparity, evidenced in the devaluation of women and the ambrosial
potentials of their bodily fluids, the ideology of the Nāth yogis incorpo-
rates the hierarchical distinction between pure, that is to say the upper,
parts of the human body and impure, the lower parts of the body, those
that are below the navel. Similarly, aspects of reality are considered more
important when they exist in their subtle form (such as the seed in the
aspect of elixir), while the material manifestations of the same (such as
the physical seed) are devalued.
Coagula: Aleister Crowley and the Path of the Dove
It is not completely clear through which historical channels Crowley
arrived at and synthesized his version of sex magick, which supposedly
informs the highest teaching of his magical order the OTO. (Crowley
became the head of the OTO in 1922.) The strongest link points in the
direction of Paschal Beverly Randolph and the Hermetic Brotherhood of
Light.29 Another major influence seems to be Clément de Saint-Marcq.30
There is no doubt, however, that Crowley has amplified the teaching
transmitted through the original OTO by references to other related
techniques that blend spirituality with sex, and it seems clear that he was
familiar with some tantric and hatha yogic ideas on the subject, as argued
in the previous chapter. This being said, it is emphatically not my inten-
tion to claim a historical link, but rather to investigate formal similarities
between Crowley’s sex magick and the Yoga of the Nāth Siddhas.
It calls for immediate mention that the line of connection between
sexual aspects of the early phase of Tantra (vide supra) and Crowley’s
teachings is simultaneously the line of demarcation that separates him
from the contemporary New Age neo-tāntrikas. Unlike the latter, Crow-
ley does not appear to be primarily interested in prolonged orgasmic
bliss; instead, his main preoccupation seems to lie in the ambrosial
potential of sexual fluids. In the same way as the Nāth Siddhas, Crowley

[Page 74]
SOLVE ET COAGULA 67
is sexist31 in assigning the superior value to the male seed; in other words,
he too tends “to fetishize manhood as a gateway to immortality.”32 What
distinguishes his method is the preference for the material form of the
semen, which is ejaculated, commingled with vaginal fluids, and either
consumed as a magical form of Eucharist or employed in order to con-
secrate talismans.
It is significant that in one of the earliest essays on the subject, Crowley
places emphasis on the physiological, that is, material aspects of sexual
magick, while simultaneously establishing a connection between sexual
activity and artistic creativity. He writes:
The divine consciousness which is reflected and refracted in the works of
Genius feeds upon a certain secretion, as I believe. This secretion is analo-
gous to semen, but not identical with it. . . . So closely is this secretion con-
nected with the sexual economy that it appears to me at times as if it might
be a by-product of that process which generates semen.33
An important assertion in the above quotation is that it is vigorous
sexual activity, and not the ascetic denial of the same, which leads to
creative outbursts: the one seems to parallel the other. Crowley will
eventually arrive at the position that the sexual (and especially male)
fluids represent crystallization of the highest magical power. His magical
diaries, the most representative of which is his “Rex de Arte Regia,”34 are
consequently filled with continual references to sexual rites conducted
with specific aims in mind, at the conclusion of which he would partake
of the “Eucharist,” consisting of the bindu mixed with vaginal fluids or
menstrual blood. Thus, in contradistinction to the method of the Nāth
yogis, Crowley commences his practice at the mental or ideal level, by
concentrating the attention on the goal of practice and by remaining
focused on it up to, and through, the moment of orgasm and ejaculation.
In doing so, he in effect “fixes the immobile” and earths, or draws down,
the spiritual or ideal (in the Platonic sense) form of semen, originally
impressed by the image of the goal meditated upon, which now becomes
material and turns into a sacrament. His view of the value of this sort
of a sacrament is neatly summarized in the following passage from his
magnum opus, Magick in Theory and Practice:35
The Magician becomes filled with God, fed upon God, intoxicated with
God. Little by little his body will become purified by the internal lustration
of God; day by day his mortal frame, shedding its earthly elements, will
become in very truth the Temple of the Holy Ghost. Day by day the matter

is replaced by Spirit, the human by the divine; ultimately the change will be
complete: God manifest in flesh will be his name. (1997: 269)
Keeping in mind the fact that Crowley’s method of sexual magick has
already received a significant amount of scholarly treatment, most nota-
bly in the work of Hugh Urban, I will refrain from going into the details of
its principles and technicalities, and focus instead on some less explored
areas. In particular, my intention is to investigate a set of ideas that relate
to the notion that the human seed represents an essential identity of the
person, a form of a deity, or an aspect of one’s Holy Guardian Angel. This
investigation will eventually lead to a comparison with the related ideas
implied in the system of Yoga as practiced by the Nāth Siddhas.
The implied divine nature of sexual fluids is apparent in the previous
quotation, where Crowley refers to the consummation of the “Eucharist”
as resulting in the Magician’s being “filled with God, fed upon God, intox-
icated with God.” Just as the Christian theology understands the bread
and wine used in the ritual of the Mass to be equivalent to the real body
and blood of Jesus Christ, Crowley assumes his version of the “Eucharist”
to be literally the material incarnation of the god.36 This is also evident in
the lines from one of his “holy” or inspired texts, “Liber Stellae Rubeae,”
which describe the ejaculation of the sperm in a sexual rite under the dis-
turbing metaphor of child sacrifice: “Also he shall slay a young child upon
the altar, and the blood shall cover the altar with perfume as of roses.
Then shall the master appear as He should appear—in His glory.”37 Who
is this master? It would appear that it is the sperm understood in its meta-
physical aspect. The same idea is entertained, among other things, in the
chapter from The Book of Lies, called “Dewdrops,” in which the sperm is
equated with the “Dew of Immortality” and described as “the Quintes-
sence and Elixir of [man’s] being,” with the following admonition: “Let
this go free, even as It will; thou art not its master, but the vehicle of It.”38
There are scattered allusions in Crowley’s writings to the effect that
the human sexual secretions, in their religious connotation, constitute
the secret or “silent” Self of the person. This secret Self is occasionally
equated with the “Babe in the Egg of Blue” and identified as the god of
silence, Hoor-Pa-Kraat (Harpocrates), one of the major Thelemic dei-
ties. The “egg” in question is “not only Akasha, but the original egg in
the biological sense. This egg issues from the lotus, which is the symbol
of the Yoni [vagina].”39 Despite the fact that Crowley here recognizes the
role of a female “egg” in its generative function, it is more consonant with
his general ideological position to give precedence to the inherent virtues

the context of present considerations.
“The Hermit” is associated with the Hebrew letter yod, which by its
shape resembles the seed. It is also the first letter of the Tetragramma-
ton (YHWH), and thus it “symbolizes the Father, who is Wisdom; he is
the highest form of Mercury, and the Logos, the Creator of all worlds.
Accordingly, his representative in physical life is spermatozoon; this is
why the card is called The Hermit.”40 (Note that both Crowley and the
Nāth yogis relate the sperm to Mercury.) In addition this card, and its
associated set of correspondences, is considered to illustrate “the entire
mystery of Life in its most secret workings.”41 Perhaps the most impor-
tant aspect of the card, however, lies in the notion that it represents “the
general doctrine that the climax of the Descent into Matter is the signal for
the reintegration by Spirit. It is . . . the mode of fulfillment of the Great
Work.”42 And it is precisely this ideological position, the notion that the
descent into material form is a necessary prerequisite for spiritual reinte-
gration, that on the one hand justifies and explains Crowley’s valorization
of the material form of the seed (and a cluster of related ideas and prac-
tices), while on the other hand distances his mode of approaching the
potentials of sexual secretions from the method of the Nāth Siddhas. The
ultimate result may be the same, but the two methodological orientations
reflect divergent views about the relationship between the material and
the spiritual realms of existence.
Interpretations and Conclusions
The Nāth Siddhas occupy a somewhat ambiguous position with respect
to the tantric traditions of India. In general, they share tantric interest in
the human body as an instrument of liberation and a microcosm infused
with hidden powers and properties. But with respect to the liberating
potentials of sexuality, they appear rather puritanical. They do not draw
the tantric equation between Yoga and bhoga, between meditation and
(this-worldly and sexual) pleasure. As a consequence, their ultimate goal
seems to be embedded in the traditional Indian model of withdrawal
from the world and material existence. This is also reflected and apparent

in their attitude toward the ambrosial potential of the human seed: it is
considered an elixir of immortality, but only if withdrawn from sexual
organs (associated with the element of Earth) and brought up to the top
of the head (associated with emptiness, śūnya, or Heaven, gagana). In
a similar vein, guru Matsyendranāth, who marries and has children, is
considered a “fallen” (patita) yogi, in contrast to Gorakhnāth, the celibate
and thus (from the perspective of the Nāths) eternally young yogi, the
one who has preserved his seed.
The sexual magick of Crowley is anchored in a different ideological
perspective. Crowley considered his religious/magical philosophy of
Thelema to be representative of what he termed the White School of
Magick, understood in its special meaning of a doctrine that accepts and
celebrates material existence.43 As a consequence, the ultimate goal of
the Thelemic adept consists not in withdrawal from the world but in the
deliberate intention “[t]o do [one’s] pleasure on the earth [a]mong the
legions of the living.”44 An important aspect of this doctrinal attitude
lies in the positive orientation toward the human body and sexuality. By
extension, and as a practical implementation of this theoretical position
with respect to the ambrosial potentials of the human seed, Crowley
attributes magical significance to the material form of the semen. In
doing so, he gives precedence to the path of the dove over the path of the
serpent, to coagula over solve.
The closest resemblance between the Yoga of the Nāth Siddhas and the
sexual magick of Crowley lies in the theophanic understanding of sexual
fluids. The Nāths imply that the semen and menstrual blood are forms
of Śiva and Śakti, and Crowley holds a similar view. They all also attach
great importance to the symbol of the child, and from a certain perspec-
tive, this “child” is identical to the human sperm. In the case of the Nāths,
this is implied through the correspondence between the semen, Śiva, and
Gorakhnāth (who is occasionally deified as a “second Śiva”). Gorakhnāth
is also the child who is giving the speech from the “summit of heaven,”
in other words, from the highest cakra, the ultimate destination of the
bindu, which here also assumes the form of the elixir. Gorakhnāth is Śiva,
who is the semen, which is the eternal divine child. Similarly, Crowley
(1981: 11) refers to the theogony of The Book of the Law under the rubric
of Kteis (vagina), Phallos, and Sperma: the Mother, the Father, and the
Child. This “child,” the Lord of the Aeon of Thelema, is thus, from at least
one point of view, correspondent with the spiritual aspect of the sperm.45
Both Crowley and the Nāths draw a connection, on several lev-
els, between the properties of the human seed and the condition of

immortality. A common feature of the yogic stories is their ability to
“trick Death” (kāla vañcana). The implication is that those who control
their bindu thereby escape the clutches of mortality. This is comparable
to Crowley’s (1974a: 89) suggestion that the sperm is able to tame the
three-headed hound of Hell, Cerberus. It is a biological fact that the sex-
ual secretions are the “secret” of the physical continuation, or “immor-
tality,” of the human species. Immortality is the condition of the gods,
who are “not mortal” (amar), a Sanskrit word related to amrta (elixir of
immortality), which is again an aspect or property of sexual fluids. The
connection between these ideas should be apparent. And the view that the
human sperm might be considered a form of the god should also appear
less strange if approached from the perspective of the identity between the
microcosm and macrocosm. This view is usually taken in the sense that
the human being analogically resembles the universe, but it is equally pos-
sible to analogically identify the macrocosm as the human being and the
microcosm as the human seed. Consequently, at least in some of their the-
ories, both the Nāth Siddhas and Crowley ascribe to the view that imputes
divine properties to the potentials of the human sexual secretions.
There remains the question of historical connections between sexual
practices of the yogis and Crowley’s magick. His mode of practice is, by
his own admission, transmitted to him through the channel of the Ordo
Templi Orientis (OTO). One of the founders of this Order was Karl Kell-
ner, who was supposedly initiated in these teachings by three “Oriental
Adepts.”46 Two of these three were genuine yogis: Bheema Sena Pratapa
and Agamya Guru Paramahansa, whose works on yogic traditions were
published in English and known by Crowley.47 Kellner’s successor, The-
odor Reuss, was similarly interested in Yoga and wrote on the subject,
as Kellner also did.48 It is conceivable that some sort of genuine trans-
mission was effected along these lines. In addition, Crowley was familiar
with some literature on the topic—such as Hatha Yoga Pradīpikā and
Śiva Samhitā—which treat of the cakras, kundalinī, and the practice of
urethral suction (vajrolī mudrā) by which the yogi withdraws commin-
gled semen and vaginal fluids.
It is also almost certain that some relevant information reached the
OTO through the teachings of Randolph and particularly through the
writings of Saint-Marcq on the subject of spermatophagy. An often-
overlooked possible historical influence might lie in some of the practices
of Count Cagliostro and his “Rite of Mizraim.”49 According to Massimo
Introvigne, certain French and German occult schools draw a distinc-
tion between “dry” and “wet” ways of alchemical practice. The “wet” way,

alternatively designated as “inner” alchemy, consists of “eating, in diverse
ways, the semen.”50 Interestingly enough, the ultimate goal of inner
alchemy was again the obtainment of immortality.51 Christopher Macin-
tosh also noted the employment of semen for the purpose of the produc-
tion of elixir in inner alchemy.52 But it would be an oversimplification to
assume that the ideas of the mystical properties of human sexual secre-
tions were limited to the above sources. They in fact seem to be wide-
spread. I suggest that it is feasible to treat these notions as an example of
an “ideal” or “logical object,” as proposed, among others, by Ioan Cou-
liano (1992) and more recently Leon Marvell (2007). Let me elaborate.
In Couliano’s interpretation, an ideal or logical object originates in the
operations of the human mind, which is thus the locus of the genesis of
ideas, unconstrained by the necessity of historical transmission. As Mar-
vell (2007: 45) explains, “The concept of ideal objects implies that a non-
linear approach to the history of ideas is possible. . . . The logical space
represented by the ideal object is not restricted to the ‘arrow of time.’”53
The ideas of sanctity related to human sexual secretions thus do not
need to originate among Gnostics (as reported by Epiphanius) or among
Hindu tāntrikas: they logically follow on the basis of the convergence of
several factors. Association between sexuality and the generation of life
is obvious. The flow of energy between the head and genitals along the
spinal column during an orgasm is empirically observable. That the male
semen has an origin in the head, to which it needs to return, is attested
belief in ancient India as well as in Greece54 and China55; however, the
notion of logical object implies that this belief does not necessarily result
from the historical contacts and transmissions: it is, instead, based on
observation and the combination of associated notions. The idea that the
semen might be the essence of the person is not that odd if we recall
that the origin of something is often viewed as its essential form. As
T. S. Eliot (1974), admittedly in another context, wrote in “East Coker,”
“In my beginning is my end.” Thus I suggest that Crowley’s ideas on the
subject are much more complex in their origin than a simple and straight
line of historical transmission would allow for. He was exposed to these
ideas and practices through several channels, and he elaborated on them
through research and experimentation. In the end, despite all the com-
motion that the subject still provokes, in its essentials it is something
rather natural. The generations of humanity bestow life to their descen-
dants through the medium of sexual fluids, and if there is any biological
candidate for an elixir of life, these same fluids seem a logical choice.

Esoteric Discourse, Cultural Constraints,
and the Initiatic Hermeneutics
Dion Fortune commences her series of essays on the subject of subtle
force and its role in occult practice by pointing out the initial difficulty
that a Westerner experiences in an attempt to penetrate into a differ-
ent cultural code, in this case, trying to understand the principles of
Yoga and Tantra. Generally speaking, the difficulty is of hermeneutical
nature: esoteric literature is couched in a specific mode of discourse in
such a manner that the intended meaning is not discoverable by the
act of simple translation, and the dictionary meaning is insufficient
to unlock the true import that lies hidden in such texts (Fortune and
Knight, 1998: 11). This is a valid remark, particularly in light of the
common presence of the “twilight speech” (sandhya bhāsā) in tantric
and yogic literature.4 The larger epistemological issue, the question
pertaining to valid means of obtaining knowledge, and the related dis-
tinction between emic and etic approaches toward that goal, similarly
inhere in the problem that Fortune is pointing out with her remark.
Additional difficulty consists of cultural and social differences, which
similarly constrain efforts at mutual understanding, for Fortune firmly
believes that the general attitude toward life in the East and the West is
“entirely d ifferent” (1998: 11).
Having thus briefly sketched the initial problem, the solution that For-
tune offers to it lies in the condition of initiation: an initiate immersed
in the Western esoteric tradition is singularly equipped to surmount

[Page 84]

the above-stated difficulty, because such a person possesses the key
that opens the gate of understanding another, that is, foreign esoteric
tradition(s). To a certain extent, this suggestion implies that the emic
overrides the etic approach, but even more importantly, the implica-
tion—at a later point explicitly stated—is that analogical or correlative
thinking, the style of thought that operates on the basis of assumed corre-
spondences between phenomena, provides a valid means of penetration
into alien systems of ideas and symbols. Correlative thinking was much
more appreciated and widespread in the classical antiquity, and for that
reason Fortune, otherwise deeply impressed by the work of Jane Har-

no difficulty in understanding each other” (1998: 12). More specifically,
she claims that those who are initiated into Western esoteric tradition
“will have no difficulty in understanding much in Eastern thought that is
obscure or even obscene to the ordinary student” (Fortune and Knight,
1998: 13), and she argues that the key toward unification of Eastern and
Western esoteric systems lies in the kabbalistic model of the Tree of Life.
Before proceeding to take a closer look at Fortune’s elaboration of the
methods that employ the Western hermetic Kabbalah as an interpreta-
tive tool, it may be opportune here to interrogate the implications of her
position. According to Fortune, being an insider of one mystery tradition
facilitates understanding of the inner meaning of another tradition. This
is a common presupposition among esoteric writers and it is implicitly
present in what Antoine Faivre refers to as a practice of concordance,
and which Faivre and Voss define as “a consistent tendency to attempt
to establish common denominators among two or more different tra-
ditions, or even among all traditions, in hopes of obtaining a gnosis of
superior quality” (1995: 61). Fortune insists that her training in Western
initiatic systems entitles her to comment on Yoga (1998: 13) and enables
her to surpass the smokescreen of the “twilight language”: “I can take
eastern teachings and methods, and comparing them with what I know
of the western methods, say: ‘This is what you really mean, quite apart
from what you choose to say’” (1998: 53).5 This perspective both implies
an epistemological advantage of correlative thinking and assumes that
there exists a family resemblance between Eastern and Western esoteric
traditions. And finally, the implication is that the personal engagement in
a praxis is a sine qua non of comparative esotericism. Crowley similarly
suggests, in his commentary to Blavatsky’s The Voice of Silence (Crowley

is practical experience.

Subtle Body, Sexuality, and Tantra
It has become commonplace to associate Western misunderstanding of
Tantra with the subject of sex. Fortune is here again emphasizing this
association.7 However, her reasons for making this connection deserve
another look. What is at stake here is not only the “translation” of Indian
metaphysical theories into Western occult vocabulary and cognitive pat-

the relationship between sexuality and the spiritual quest without falling
into the extremes of either sensual hedonism on the one hand or puri-

taking Fortune’s writings on this subject as the case in point.

the sephirothic Tree, as used in the hermetic Kabbalah. In particular,

that this cakra corresponds with the sephira Yesod, associated with the

But Yesod is also the place of the “very personal sex force” (vide supra).

sephira Yesod) centers. But this is an important consideration: this oppo-
sition is functional and not moral or ontological. As a functional opposi-
tion, the relationship between the lowest and the highest—between sex
organs and the brain, between eroticism and spirituality—serves the nec-
essary role of a catalyst that sets the current in motion. But the current
is one.

Rituals and the Awakening of the “Sleeping Serpent”
The basic postulate of Fortune’s comparison between Indian and Western
esotericism concerns the reciprocity between the system of the cakras in
the former and the Tree of Life in the latter current of thought and prac-
tice. The basis of this reciprocity lies in the presumed shared properties
of the subtle body and its centers of energy. If we are to acknowledge and
accept the validity of her statements on the subject, the further basis of the
mutual correlation between the two systems is established experimentally
and experientially: Fortune claims that she has studied both methods and
that her exposition of the subject matter is based on her ability to bypass
the double entendre of the written sources, and the related ability to align
the disparate notions, by taking advantage of correlative thinking and the
system of correspondences based on the model of the Tree of Life. There
remains one more important element in this matter that deserves further
comment, and this refers to the “translation” of Eastern meditations into
Western rituals and their mutual correspondence.
Once again, in order to properly evaluate and understand Fortune’s
position, it needs emphasizing that she privileges the subtle or “etheric”
body as the proper vehicle of the occult practice and that the develop-
ment of this body and its powers depends on the principle of polarity and
circuit of the “force.” In this regard, she is in general agreement with the
basic thrust of tantric and related yogic teachings. Fortune argues that
the Western methods of engagement with these subjects are predomi-
nantly ceremonial, and as a historic precedent and example she suggests
“the Mystery rites of such centres such as Eleusis and the Memphis,” to
which she adds, at first glance somewhat unexpectedly, that “the same

As far as the statement about ballroom dancing, as a contemporary
Western method for raising the equivalent of the “serpent power,” is con-
cerned, it is helpful to situate it within the larger context of the mutual
relationship between religion and dance. In the simplest possible terms,
dance is conducive to ecstasy. Crowley (1913: 17–46) has made some
cogent remarks on the subject in his essay on “Energised Enthusiasm.” In
the recent past, a number of studies have focused on the phenomenon of
rave dancing and its relationship with altered states of consciousness and
the experience of the sacred. As Tim Olaveson (2004: 90) observes: “Rav-
ers describe ‘the vibe’ as a kind of energy or pulse . . . which can only be
physically experienced. As an amplified feeling or emotional state, the
rave ‘vibe’ mirrors the ‘exaltation’ and ‘enthusiasm’ described by [Émile]
Durkheim.” Even more pertinent to the present discussion, Erik Davis
(2004: 262) notes in relation to the experiences of the dancers at the pop-
ular location Goa in India that their goal “revolves around ‘raising the
kundalini serpent energy in the body’s chakra system’” and that in the
music “these highly directed vibrations serve as immediate, functional
analogs for the secret life of shakti.”
The most probable reasons for Fortune’s suggestion that ballroom
dancing represents a Western analogue to the Indian methods of raising
the energies of the “sleeping serpent” lie in the following two elements. As
M. Carole Pistole (2003: 235) notes, “[i]n ballroom dancing, the partners’
positions are inherently gendered,” and this observation ties neatly with
Fortune’s insistence on the importance of the “magnetic polarity” for the
purpose of activating the subtle energies of the body. Another reason, I
suggest, concerns the “charged” emotional and erotic state into which
the dancers inevitably enter. This is in agreement with Fortune’s remark
that, while the methods of Yoga deal with the energies of the subtle body
directly, “the occult method uses ceremonial to energise the imagina-
tion so as to arouse the emotions, thus causing the subconscious energies
and controls to come into action” (Fortune and Knight, 1998: 107). And
while ballroom dancing is not exactly ceremonial, although it does have
its “ritual” structure, the important thing to emphasize is the role of the
energized imagination and emotions that are involved in the process.10

As mentioned above, another Western method that in Fortune’s opin-
ion resembles Indian techniques of awakening the cakras and the related
energies of the subtle body is that of the spiritualists’ circle. To be more
precise, she is of the opinion that there are three strands that comprise
the effective model of the engagement with the subject under discus-
sion: these are Hatha Yoga, spiritualism, and ceremonial magic. We are
already familiar with the statement that “Hatha Yoga and spiritualism
between them reveal the lost secrets of western occultism” (Fortune and
Knight, 1998: 205). The reason for this somewhat unexpected appraisal
of spiritualism is again of a primarily pragmatic nature: Fortune is not
suggesting that spiritualism provides a valid metaphysical position vis-
à-vis the nature of reality, but that its method works. And the method
works, according to Fortune’s general thesis, because spiritualists sit in
a circle, typically in a gendered arrangement, alternating between the
sexes, holding hands: in other words, generating a circuit of force. This
seating arrangement is, by the way, also typical of the tantric ritual circle,
a fact that Fortune does not mention explicitly, although it may be safely
assumed that she was familiar with it from Avalon’s writings.
