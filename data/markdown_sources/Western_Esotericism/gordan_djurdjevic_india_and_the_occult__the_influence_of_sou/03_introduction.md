There is a network of related notions, representations, and attitudes
toward reality, whose characteristics and mutual relationships
in varying degrees overlap, intertwine, and resemble each other, and
which among others include categories such as myth, fiction, construct,
imagination, and māyā.1 From what may be defined as a conventional,
historicist, or (more pejoratively) literalist position, any worldview
aspiring to veracity that is anchored in the above-mentioned categories is
considered invalid, flawed, erroneous, and fake. But from an alternative
perspective, the presence of imaginative construction or fiction is
an inescapable condition in the formulation of any account about
reality. Reality is structured as fiction (Lacan). There are no facts, only
interpretations (Nietzsche). The priest, the poet, the scientist, and the
historian are each and every one, from this other point of view, in the
final instance all storytellers, and only their respective genres, with their
associated discursive conventions and methodological rules, mutually
differ. It follows, from this line of thought, that fiction (the map) is an
indispensable and perhaps in the final reckoning the only possible model
for engagement with reality (the territory).
Positioning itself in such a perspective, this book looks into the imag-
ined India and her spiritual heritage, in particular the theory and prac-
tice of Yoga and Tantra, as constructed, interpreted, appropriated, and
reformulated by selected representative figures associated with modern
Western occultism. More specifically, the geographic focus of the study is
the British occult scene, and its temporal framework is for the most part

new religious movements and groups, in particular those that inhere in

To start with, it would be appropriate to clarify my conceptual vocab-
ulary. Throughout this book, I am intentionally using interchangeably
and far from consistently several associated terms, the most important
of which are esotericism, the occult, and magic. I am rather hesitant to
define these terms with precision, particularly if such defining should
suggest that these designations refer to sharply distinguished areas and
aspects of religious thought and practice, just as I am aware of the fact
that some would even object to the inclusion of these subjects into the
very category of religion as such. Nevertheless, since I subscribe to
Mircea Eliade’s proposal that religion refers to the human response to
what they perceive as the sacred or the real,2 I feel justified in referring
to esotericism, the occult, and magic as forms of religion.
Esotericism is the broadest and the most general category of these three,
and it refers to both ideas and associated activities that operate with hid-
den or secret knowledge, representing inner aspects of normative religion,
often fully accessible only to a select circle of initiates and expressed in a
particular, frequently coded, mode of discourse. In its Western regional
form—and I should emphasize again that I consider esotericism as a con-
ceptual category that is applicable cross-culturally—this includes a vast
array of textual and performative traditions in a historical trajectory from
Alexandrian Hermeticism in Late Antiquity to the contemporary explo-
sion of interest in alternative religiosity.3 More than anything else, eso-
tericism as a form of thought relies on analogical or correlative thinking,
which both generates the coherence of the world (Assmann, 2007) and
provides a justification for practical engagement with a variety of objec-
tives in mind, often through a ritual activity based upon the fundamental
principle that “like attracts like.” Esotericism typically assumes that the

human being represents a mirror of the universe (a microcosm reflecting
the macrocosm) and that human nature is perfectible and, in its ultimate
potential, divine. In esotericism, the intermediary world—what Paul Mus
called mesocosm and Henry Corbin mundus imaginalis—is understood
to be of significant importance, as is the value of the cultivated faculty
of imagination. Typically, experiential and intuitive suprarational knowl-
edge, sometimes referred to as gnosis, is considered more important than
faith, although the value of the latter is not rejected.
The adjective “occult” and the related substantive “occultism” usually
refer to a more specific and narrower category. The term is sometimes
associated with the “occult philosophy” famously propounded by
Cornelius Agrippa in his influential De Occulta Philosophia Libri Tres
(first published in 1533). Dealing with the hidden properties of things
and their deliberate manipulation for typically personal ends, the
so-called “occult sciences” include disciplines such as alchemy, astrol-
ogy, divination, and ritual magic. In the nineteenth century, in the wake
of a renewed and widespread interest in the subject occasioned by the
writings of Eliphas Lévi (1810–75), the term occultism came into popu-
lar usage. To a degree, I maintain the distinction, suggested by the soci-
ologist Edward Tiryakian (1974), between esotericism as a theoretical
formation and occultism as a form of practice based upon it, although I
recognize that this is a heuristic division more than anything else.
By the end of the nineteenth century a seminal group, the Hermetic
Order of the Golden Dawn (est. 1888), synthesized a good deal of West-
ern occult traditions and for the most part associated them with the the-
ory and practice of ritual magic. In simplified terms, the Golden Dawn
understood magic as the means to achieving union with one’s higher self,
or Genius, and becoming godlike in the process (see Regardie, 1971: 214).
This particular understanding of magic looms large in the present context
because this study treats of the figures influenced to varying degrees by
the teachings of the Golden Dawn, just as it focuses on their interest in
and embrace of Eastern, for the most part Indian, religiosity. And since
the Western occultists’ interest in South Asian spirituality mainly, though
by no means exclusively, concerns the traditions of Yoga and Tantra, it
would be appropriate also to provide some basic definitions of these two
important and mutually related categories.4
There is no scholarly consensus as to the precise origins of Yoga, which
according to some predates Hinduism and has its roots in the ancient,
non-Aryan, Indus Valley Civilization. The earliest records documenting

two classical Sanskrit epics, the Rāmāyana and Mahābhārata, particu-

“textbook” of Yoga philosophy, the Yoga Sūtras, attributed to Patañjali,
which may be approximately dated to the first centuries of the Common
Era, but which without doubt represents the codification and synthesis of
(much?) older traditions. The crux of Patañjali’s system lies in the notion
that the goal of Yoga necessitates the stopping of the fluctuations of the
mind, which makes possible the correct insight into the true nature of
reality and one’s own identity. As a result of this insight, one is able to
“isolate” one’s true Self (purusa) from misidentification with various
aspects and “permutations” of what Sāmkhya, the Indian philosophi-
cal tradition closely aligned with Yoga, calls “Nature” (prakrti) and thus
to liberate oneself from the world of suffering and illusion (samsāra). It
is of some interest in the present context that Patañjali enlarges signifi-
cantly on the concept of “magical powers” (vibhūtis, siddhis) that arise as
a side product of yogic practice. This element of classical Yoga is some-
what controversial: while the siddhis are a sign of successfully performed
stages of advanced Yoga, they are not to be enjoyed as such, for to do so
would be to lose sight of the true goal and purpose of the practice, which
is the transcendence of and the liberation from the world.
At a later point, a different style of Yoga enters the stage. Several dis-
tinctive elements mark its characteristic features. Instead of being focused
on the mental process and its gradual stilling, this other form, often
called hatha (“forceful,” “intense”) Yoga, engages primarily the body,
both in its physical and metaphysical registers. A great deal of attention
is given to the practice of various postures (āsanas), muscular contrac-
tions (b andhas), and the practice of controlled breathing (prānāyāma).
In addition, particularly in its more tantric aspects, the notion of the
subtle body (sūksma śarīra) becomes increasingly relevant, involving the
more or less complex esoteric anatomy, consisting of various centers or
“wheels” (cakras) of energy, the subtle conduits or “tubes” (nādis) through
which it flows, and the notion of the primal “force” (śakti) metaphorically
associated with the “crooked” or “coiled” (kundalinī) snake “asleep” at the
base of the spine. Unlike classical Yoga, intent on liberation, this other
major style of Yoga, often associated with Tantra, is primarily interested
in power, which fundamentally and ultimately involves the notion and
goal of the divinization of the practitioner.

It is notoriously difficult to define what is meant by Tantra, even if this
were a unified phenomenon, which it is not. First of all, there is a class of
literature called “tantras,” but they do not all necessarily reflect the ideas
and practices habitually associated with Tantra as a particular phenom-
enon, which may be observed to a lesser or greater degree in every major
form of religion in India (although the Hindu and Buddhist varieties are
the most developed). Tantra typically refers to a type of ritual activity,
involving the use of special words of power (mantras), often chanted in
conjunction with various symbolic hand gestures (mudrās), and using
specific designs, whether actually drawn or visualized, called yantras and
mandalas, representing in geometric form either deities or models of an
enlightened universe, which are used for meditation or, in some cases, in
the rituals of initiation (usually drawn on the ground, signifying a sacred
space into which the initiate is admitted). The tantric ideological stance
calls for the acceptance of the world, which, in Hindu Tantra, is often
understood as the body of the goddess. Tantra suggests the mastery of
the world through engagement with it, rather than the withdrawal from
it, and homeopathically suggests that the poison can become the cure,
making a famous declaration that there is no distinction between Yoga
and bhoga (pleasure). Tantra is associated with magic, the occult, and
alchemy.5 And in some of their teachings, certain tantric groups, such
as Kaulas and Sahajiyās, suggest that sexuality could be utilized as an
instrument for the achievement of the supreme goal: the attainment of
immortality and becoming a god or a Buddha.
One interpretative model that could be utilized in order to understand
the incorporation of the above summarized Indian traditions into
certain strands of Western occultism is the model of the “translation
of cultures” proposed by Stanley Tambiah (1990). This process of the
translation of South Asian spiritual heritage into the arena of Western
occultism brought into the foreground several fundamental notions,
which in their turn call for pertinent hermeneutical engagement. Per-
haps the most important, and certainly the most obvious result of the
occultist encounter with Indian religions, lies in the acknowledgment
of what they perceive as the mutual similarity between Yoga and Tantra
on the one hand and Western esotericism—to use the general term—
on the other. “Their Yoga is our magic”—that is the typical postulation,
sometimes explicitly stated, at other times tacitly implied.6 To para-
phrase Tambiah, who on his part paraphrased Ludwig W ittgenstein
(1953), this correlation between Yoga and magic rests on the perception

Indian, and Western esoteric traditions is simultaneously also one of

The emic perception of similarity between Indian and Western eso-
tericism may also be corroborated by etic arguments. In simplified terms,
these arguments relate to meaningful formal resemblances and actual
historical contacts between these two traditions. In light of the explana-
tory model famously proposed by James G. Frazer in The Golden Bough
(1st ed. 1890), the “magic” of the relationship between Indian and West-
ern esotericism may be considered as both homeopathic (based on for-
mal, “sympathetic” resemblances) and contagious (based on mutual
contacts). As Tambiah, among others, has noticed, these two models of
magic correspond and may be condensed to two basic linguistic tropes:
metaphor and metonymy. Metaphorical and metonymic relationships,
from that vantage point, illuminate the dynamics between Indian and
Western regional varieties of esoteric theory and practice. My empha-
sis, to clarify again, will be on emic, formal, metaphorical, and imagined
connections between Indian Yoga and Western occultism. I stress, as her-
meneutical tools and conceptual models, Wittgenstein’s notion of family
resemblance, Ioan Couliano’s use of the notion of the “ideal object” (vide
infra), the esoteric/occult principle of correspondence (analogy, sympa-
thy), and the positive, active, and creative use of imagination. I imply that
these notions are either congenial to the occultists’ epistemology or heu-
ristically valid as interpretative tools in an attempt to understand their
worldview—or both.

may be, and sometimes has been, designated as “Western Tantra.” This

academia tends to dismiss those segments of Western cultural practice

At the outset, I would like to make it clear that my chosen subjects, my
case studies, do not belong to that category that is often designated as
the “New Age Tantra.” There are significant differences between the ideas
about Tantra as entertained by Aleister Crowley, Kenneth Grant, and
the initiates of the AMOOKOS (the Arcane and Magickal Order of the
Knights of Shambhala) on the one hand and those of Margo Anand and
the “orgasmic sex” variety of Tantra, typical of the New Age approach,
on the other.8 But even after this bracketing, it needs to be said that the
subjects of the present study are nevertheless similarly reproached for
misunderstanding what genuine Tantra is all about. This whole book is
one long argument for acknowledging that Western occult engagement
with Yoga and Tantra, as any other segment of cultural and religious life,
is deserving of scholarly attention and elucidation. At this point, I would
like to present some of the main reasons for my particular position in
this regard.
My principal methodological assumption, which I take as self-
evident, is that any subject of inquiry pertaining to human culture allows
for multiple interpretations. I thus do not refute that Crowley, Grant, et
al., from one point of view, and in varying degrees, display an imperfect
knowledge and understanding of India, of her classical languages, of her
religious and philosophical literature, and so on. But a common pre-
supposition behind a good deal of scholarly criticism is in my opinion

mistaken: it consists in treating these occultists as if they were Indolo-
gists, which they were not. They were not scholars nor did they pre-
tend to have been. A related implied misapprehension is that yogis and
tāntrikas in India are all on their part fully conversant with the tradi-

practice, which is far from truth. Instead of applying the criteria that
are appropriate to the academic study of Yoga and Tantra, it would be
methodologically more fruitful to consider the Western occultists’ rep-
resentations of these Indian disciplines within the context of their own
particular worldview. If the twentieth-century British occultists treated
here were approached on their own, as a distinct cultural and religious
formation with specific discursive conventions and intellectual assump-
tions, many of their attitudes and assertions would appear, within the
horizon of their own particular project, meaningful and self-consistent.
To contextualize these remarks with a pertinent example, the suggested
approach would demonstrate that their typical claim regarding a simi-
larity between Indian Yoga and Western magic rests on the basis of a
particular style of thought that is fundamental to the occultists’ episte-
mology: analogical thinking.
Analogical or correlative thinking, in other words the attempt to orga-
nize the comprehension of reality on the basis of perceived similarity or
correspondence between what ordinarily appears as an unrelated plural-
ity of phenomena, is at the root not only of Western occultism but also
of other traditional systems of thought. In India, this type of thinking,
the perception of hidden cosmic connections and the spiritual practice
predicated upon it, brought about what may be termed a radical revolu-
tion by which the original Vedic emphasis on the central role of religious
sacrifice (yajña) was translated into its inner or esoteric correlate. The last
phase of Vedic culture was dominated by the worldview of the Upanisads
and one of their fundamental notions was that the actual ceremony of
sacrifice was less important than its internalized form, in the context of
which the meditation replaced and superseded the ritual. The Upanisads
emphasized the correlation between sacrifice, cosmos, and the human
body and mind, and asserted that knowledge of these hidden relations
possesses salvific power. The elaborate structure of correspondences also
plays a significant role in Tantra, where the phonic and photic correlates
of metaphysical referents figure prominently in the theory and practice
of yantra, mandala, and mantra. In a similar manner, analogical think-
ing is one of the fundamental building blocks of Western esoteric tradi-
tions (see, inter alia, Faivre, 1994). Writing on the foundational subject of

ancient Hermeticism, Jan Assmann (2007: xii) asserts that “on the basis
of thinking in terms of references and analogies, rather than causal rela-
tionships, Hermeticism developed a theory of the coherence of the world
that could be put to practical use.” My contention is that Western occult-
ists, treated in this study, approach India’s spirituality and declare its sim-
ilarity with their magic and the occult in accordance with the principles
of analogical thinking that lie at the root of their own epistemology.
From another, though related, point of view it could be asserted that
the Western occult perception of Indian Yoga and Tantra is a result of
imagination. This imagined phenomenon is then brought into relation
with Western esotericism, and the mutual similarity between the two
is declared. To the degree that this is a correct evaluation of the matter
at hand and to the degree that this procedure is considered erroneous
and problematic, the possible counterargument is that on the one hand
everything is in a sense imagined, and on the other hand, the exercise of
imaginative faculties again lies at the root of the occult epistemology and
is as such indispensable to its project. Needless to say, that everything is
imagined does not imply that everything is equal. That reality is a fiction
does not mean that every fiction is of identical value. There are boring
fictions as well as there are dangerous fictions. My argument is that the
Western occult fiction of Indian Yoga and Tantra is a cultural phenom-
enon that is inherently interesting as such and that it has its own definite
positive values, which facts further justify its research and interpretation.
The values I have in mind are primarily the following:
● Western occultists were instrumental in the accommodation and
translation of Eastern, particularly Indian, culture and spirituality
to a larger degree than is customarily acknowledged.
● By incorporating elements of Eastern, particularly Indian, culture
and spirituality into the arena of Western esotericism, Western
occultists modified and broadened the scope of its theoretical dis-
course and practical application.
● By incorporating elements of Eastern, particularly Indian, spiritu-
ality into the arena of Western esotericism—in itself an important
though often unacknowledged segment of Western culture—West-
ern occultists influenced the more substantial reconfiguration of
habitual ideas regarding the human body and its subtle and occult
powers, the human mind and its hidden capabilities, and finally,
the nature and potential of human sexuality as an instrument of
spiritual liberation.

● Western occultists, to a varying degree, utilized some of the Eastern

It could be observed that this study provides a sort of a sequel to Joscelyn
Godwin’s erudite book The Theosophical Enlightenment (1994), the final
chapter of which focuses on the subject of “The Parting of East and West.”
As Godwin (1994: 379) himself suggested, this parting—occasioned for the
most part by what was perceived as the Theosophical Society’s unbalanced
glorification of the wisdom of the East, to which some groups responded by
the opposite reaction of the exclusive focus on the Western esotericism—
was not final: “Together with the Western occult tradition, the Theos-
ophists have provided almost all the underpinnings of the ‘New Age’
movement, their exoteric reflection, in which there is definitely no
parting of the hemispheres.” A difference between Godwin’s work and
mine lies in the fact that I have limited myself to the exploration of the
exclusively British occult scene through a selective engagement with sev-
eral case studies considered as representative of the matter at hand. In
addition to this, my efforts are for the most part of a hermeneutical and
expository nature. My explicit aim is to argue the validity of the Western
occultists’ incorporation of the elements of Indian spiritual traditions
into their conceptual universe and to suggest that the “Western Tantra” is
a subject deserving of scholarly consideration.
The Scottish historian James Webb has suggested in his classic work
The Occult Underground (1974: 2) that “[t]he dichotomy of Under-
ground and Establishment is one of the most important concepts to
have emerged from recent social changes.” Remarking on the exclu-
sion of the occult from the normative culture’s consensus regarding
what is “relevant,” Webb has argued (1974: 2) that “it is the very nature
of the occult that it cannot exist except in opposition to and interrela-
tion with that critical Establishment,” and in consequence he proposed
that “the occult is ‘important’ and ‘relevant’ to the aspirations of man-
kind; further, that it is worth study in its own right.” The occultists’
importation of South Asian spirituality, in particular Yoga and Tantra,
similarly occupies and constitutes the space of the Western cultural
Underground, while the academic study of this same spirituality argu-
ably represents the Establishment. If we think of these two cultural and

In his reevaluation of the work of Mircea Eliade, Bryan Rennie (1996:
116) proposes that a “positive implication of Eliade’s thought is that
truth, reality, power, significance, in short his ‘sacred,’ are freed from
historical actuality and ontological independence.” For something to be
perceived as sacred or real (which amounts to the same, according to the
Romanian scholar), it is not necessary to have historical or ontological
actuality. Rennie illustrates this idea with two pertinent references. In his
Tales of the Sacred and Supernatural, Eliade (1981) states that religious
systems of thought can help in integrating one’s grasp of reality irrespec-
tive of the historical accuracy of their content. Similarly, Eliade (1976: 3)
argued: “One of the fascinating aspects of the ‘cultural fashion’ is that it
does not matter whether the facts in question and their interpretation are
true or not.” With a grain of salt, these remarks may be applied to West-
ern occultists’ perception of India and her spiritual heritage. In order for
this to make sense, we have to approach and understand Western occult
systems of thought and practice as exemplars of “cultural fashions” or,
alternatively but equally appropriately, as imaginative and creative reli-
gious systems (again, understanding religion in Eliadean fashion as the
human response to what they perceive as the sacred or real).
Bradford Verter (1997) argues that the most important formative
forces in the process of the emergence of modern occultism amount
to the influence of comparative studies,9 the rise of mass media,10 the
allure of the Bohemian subculture,11 and—what is significant in the
present context—the ascending presence and popularity of Eastern, in
particular Indian, spirituality.12 It was specifically the impact of Hindu
and Buddhist teachings that led to the rise of what Verter calls a “new
occult paradigm.” While traditional occultism defined its cultural hori-
zons by reference to various strands of essentially Western esoteric tra-
ditions (such as Neoplatonism, Hermeticism, and the Kabbalah), the
new paradigm extended its boundaries to encompass Eastern spiritual
ideas, disciplines, sacred texts, and gurus.13 The importance of this
change lies in the fact that together “[w]ith the shift in personnel came
a shift in the message. . . . Acknowledging the validity of so many inter-
changeable spirits had the peculiar effect of redirecting the attention

These assertions call for serious consideration. If the general argu-
ment is that colonialism changed—or created, as Said (1978) or Inden
(1990) might argue—not only the East but also the West (and I am aware
of the contested and constructed nature of both designations),14 and if we
want properly to understand the characteristics of this change, we can-
not do this fully unless we also take into account the study of the occult
as an area of Western culture that was both influenced and transformed
by the ideas and practices of Eastern provenance. To continue to insist
that the Western occult interpretation of Indian Yoga and Tantra (and
other forms of Asian religiosity) is flawed, improper, mistaken, and even
imperialistic is not exactly and not completely incorrect, but the inherent
limitation of this approach is to focus on the issue of legitimacy and the
supremacy of origins to the point of imbalance.
It will be obvious from the case studies that follow that one of the domi-
nant models of Western occultist reception and (re)interpretation of
Indian spirituality consists of their employment of the kabbalistic Tree of
Life and related concepts, in order to explain and classify Eastern ideas.
To preclude a possible misunderstanding, I should clarify that the Kab-
balah in this case does not exactly or primarily refer to the system of Jew-
ish mystical tradition based on the interpretation of the Torah, but instead

To return to the occult Kabbalah in the tradition of the Golden Dawn:
its most characteristic feature, as already suggested, lies in the use of
the model of the Tree of Life, consisting of ten spheres associated with
the first ten numbers and twenty-two paths associated with the letters
of the Hebrew alphabet. This symbolic structure is then used as a model
of the universe and what may be called a filing cabinet for a potentially
limitless system of correspondences. In this manner, the Tree of Life and
its principal set of elemental, planetary, and zodiacal attributions pro-
vide an organizing and structural model that governs and systematizes
operations of analogical thinking. This theoretical knowledge has its
practical application in the construction of various magical rituals, where
the corresponding sets of images, ideas, sounds, perfumes, colors, pre-
cious stones, and other elements comprise the constitutional framework
of specific rites. The following needs reemphasizing: that the Western
occultists associate and inscribe yogic and tantric concepts and other ele-
ments of Indian thought onto the kabalistic Tree of Life is not so much an
instance of an incongruous lumping together of disparate religious and
philosophical ideas but rather the principal element, the sine qua non, of
their particular epistemology and of their modus operandi.
The occult epistemology, based on correlative thinking, engenders
the coherence of the world by uncovering and emphasizing an assumed
analogy or sympathy between phenomena. In a certain sense and to
some degree, this is in principle similar to Wittgenstein’s notion of fam-
ily resemblance. To provide an example relevant to this notion, we may
consider the cosmogonic idea of emanation: according to this view, the
highest and purest form of spiritual reality emanates through several
successive stages until it reaches the material level of manifestation. In
Neoplatonic teachings, this scenario becomes entwined with astrological
considerations: the soul, on its downward journey toward embodiment,

nation of the objective universe and its related but reversed trajectory of
spiritual development in Indian traditions. Here the attributes of vari-

is associated with the material world and the physical body, while the
highest cakra, the goal of the yogic “journey,” stands for the pure spirit.

the similarity between these Neoplatonic, yogic, kabbalistic, and occultist
notions, for as soon as one conceives of the idea that reality is not uniform
but consists of a gradual series of layers between the ideal and the actual,
between the spiritual and the material, between God and humankind, the
related idea that it is possible to climb back from the lowest rung on the
scale to its highest end appears almost by necessity. This instance of family
resemblance is thus also an example of an “ideal object” that “exists in its
own synchronical and logical dimension” (Couliano, 1992: 8) and as such

Permeable boundaries generate anxiety.1 Academic disciplines and
scholarly categories tend to aspire toward clear marks of delineation
and mutual distinction. This is particularly the case with newly established
areas of research. It is thus not surprising that Antoine Faivre, one of
the founders of the academic study of Western esotericism, in several
instances argued against the inclination to make a claim for a “universal
esotericism.”2 According to this view, which is not isolated, esotericism
should be seen as a specifically Western cultural phenomenon. The
reasons for this position appear sound: there is a historic continuity
among Western esoteric currents, and in the West there exists a specific
esoteric universe of discourse that is closely related to its own exoteric
wing, represented by the normative Abrahamic religions, in particular
Christianity. Closer analysis, however, will demonstrate that the
above conceptualization of esotericism lies principally in its heuristic
expediency: it does make sense, and it is appropriate to study esotericism
as a Western phenomenon for the reasons mentioned above, but there is
no inherent rationale to adopt this orientation as the only valid approach.
Like any other cultural notion, the category of esotericism is a theoretical
construct3—a discursive formation—and as such it may be used as
a tool with which to approach what appear to be reasonably similar
manifestations of human thought and behavior in other cultures. What
follows is a suggestion of how this may be done with respect to Indian
(South Asian) traditions, with an implicit assumption that something
similar may be done relative to the other area studies.

A note of caution against the attribution of the category of esotericism
to the Hindu tradition was also voiced early on by a Traditionalist author,
René Guénon. According to his assessment, there is no strict opposition
between the exoteric and esoteric teachings in Hinduism; instead, there
is only a progressive deepening of esotericism.4 As a matter of fact, a
similar view was already propounded much earlier. In the middle of the
eighteenth century, John Zephaniah Holwell, in the second volume of
his Interesting Historical Events, Relative to the Provinces of Bengal, and
the Empire of Indostan, albeit with a somewhat different agenda in mind,
wrote that
It is worthy notice that the Metempsychosis as well as the three grand prin-
ciples taught in the greater Eleusinian mysteries; namely the unity of god-
head, his general providence over all creation, and a future state of reward
and punishment . . . were preached by the Bramins, from time immemorial
to this day, throughout Indostan: not as mysteries, but as religious tenets,
publicly known and received; by every Gentoo, of the meanest capacity.
(1767: 25; emphasis in the original.)
Broadly speaking, Guénon’s argument has reasonable merit, since it is
true that in Hinduism the divine is typically not construed as existing
solely outside of the believer—this is a fairly common view. But there
are many shades of meaning to this assertion. There are, in fact, Indian
traditions that internalize their doctrines in a more restrictive, exclu-
sive, and secretive manner so that access to teachings and practices of
this kind is possible only with guidance from a guru, which typically
presupposes the necessity of initiation (dīksā, abhiseka), transmission
of secret knowledge, employment of coded discourse (sandhyā bhāsā),
and a claim of absolute knowledge and supernatural powers (siddhis).
All these elements inhere in the category of esotericism. But even if we
accept Guénon’s interpretation, this does not have to mean that we must
necessarily and completely abandon the use of the category of esoteri-
cism when studying Hinduism. Instead, we need to acknowledge that
there exists more than one form of esotericism and that the particularity
of the Western branch lies in the sharp distinction between it and nor-
mative, exoteric religion, while in India the distinction tends to be one
of degree rather than kind.
Historically, the view of India as a repository of occult knowledge
is quite ancient, captivating the Western imagination subsequent to
Alexander the Great’s military conquest of the northwestern region of
the country. The archetype of the Indian sage was encapsulated into

included under the umbrella terms of esotericism, magic, and the occult,

specific forms of Indian religiosity in more detail, it is appropriate to
make some remarks of a general nature. In order to avoid possible misun-
derstandings, and fully aware that “seemingly innocuous terminological
conventions are often the reflection of hidden or implicit ideological agen-

as magic or the occult, my intention is not to represent aspects of Indian

Burchett is justifiably critical of the negative conceptual baggage attached

scholars. His explicit aim is to deconstruct the thusly conceived notion
of magic and demonstrate why it is inappropriate to include it as a descrip-

though the argument has wider connotations). Stated succinctly, to call an
aspect of Indian religion “magic” typically implies an attitude of suprem-

Burchett’s analysis of the prevalent academic attitude toward magic and
find his arguments cogent, I am nevertheless adopting an alternative
strategy. I continue to operate with notions of magic, esotericism, and
the occult, but I am reversing their qualitative connotations. I take them
seriously. In this way I am attempting to perform the Derridean strategy
of “destroying the old machinery” (Burchett, 2008: 837) of social dispar-
ity that associates “establishment” with religion and science, and “under-
ground” with magic.

If esotericism, in the most general (and etymological) meaning of the

The conceptual patterning of reality along the system of correspon-

the fundamental statement at the root of such thinking is given in the
famous Emerald Tablet (Tabula Smaragdina) in the form of “As above,
so below.”18 In Indian alchemy, the analogous principle is expressed
through the phrase “As in metal, so in the body” (yathā lohe, tathā dehe).

inner are one” (bāhar bhītar yekamkār; see also Djurdjevic, 2008: 32). As
Wayman explains, the most important aspect of analogical thinking in
Vajrayāna relates to the well-known principle of “affiliating” one’s own
body, speech, and mind with the body, speech, and mind of the Buddha
through corporeal ritual gestures (mudrās), vocal incantations (mantras),
and deep mental concentration (samādhi).20 We may again simplify the
complexity of Vajrayāna Buddhism for the purpose of making an argu-
ment and suggest that its ultimate goal, becoming enlightened and thus
a Buddha, is accomplished through the ritual and meditative enactments
of the esoteric principle of correspondence, which also lies at the root of
the occult and magical modus operandi. In Stephen Beyer’s (1973: 92)
formulation, “if we should ever be forced to attempt a definition of ‘Tan-

of Buddhahood.”

for the rituals performed utilizing Vedic hymns were considered to
have inevitable results. This relates to a fundamental notion of karman,
which eventually came to mean an outcome of any action, but which
originally meant a definite outcome of the performance of a Vedic rite.

the God(s) may or may not respond favorably. Quite the contrary, the
favorable outcome was the necessary effect caused by the performance of
the ritual. This is a typical presupposition behind the theory of magical
activity. In addition, as Asko Parpola (1979: 141) argues, “the principle
underlying the Vedic ritual is the basic law of magic: similia similibus.”
Be that as it may, there can be no doubt, however, that the youngest of
the Vedic compositions, the Atharva Veda, represents a straightforward
collection of magical formulae, consisting as it does of numerous charms
that function either to dispel negative conditions or to attract positive

particularly Indian cultural manner according to which these disciplines
were intellectually conceived and historically developed.

My central argument is that Indian Yoga and Tantra are the closest
South Asian analogues to the Western esoteric traditions (and vice versa).
There are two aspects to this argument. On the one hand, I propose that
there is an actual formal similarity between important features of Indian
and Western esotericism: the common belief in the importance of cor-
respondences, in the reality of the subtle body (sūksma ˙sarīra) and what
Henry Corbin designated as the mundus imaginalis; the common belief
in the possibility of developing magical powers or siddhis, in the pos-

in human perfectibility, in the possibility of the acquisition of absolute
knowledge, and so forth. In other words, I suggest that there is common
ground, shared space, between these two (what I consider regional) forms
of esotericism. As I have already suggested in an earlier work (Djurdjevic,
2008), I propose that (at least some forms of) Yoga and Tantra stand in
analogical relation to Western esotericism, and more specifically, that at
least some forms of yogic and tantric meditation are analogous to West-

between yogic meditation and “active” or “true” imagination (vis imagina-
tiva, imaginatio vera), as understood in its technical meaning in Western
esotericism.23 This is particularly the case in those forms of South Asian
meditation techniques that utilize images in their modus operandi.24
One of the least explored and potentially most fruitful areas of pos-

concerns the presumed magical and occult properties of language,
in particular when employed, ritually or meditatively, as mantras or

potentials or cosmogonic properties, are similarly attested in both tradi-

comparative inquiry. As already discussed, Patton Burchett has recently
critically interrogated the widespread tendency to interpret mantras as
“magical” language and argued that the adjective is inadequate due to
the long history (in the West) of its derogatory use and its associations
with irrationality and superstition, which is in itself a legacy of Protes-
tant theologians’ questionable separation between religion and magic.27

The Role of Yoga and Tantra in

In the summer of 1900, Aleister Crowley (1875–1947) found himself in
Mexico pursuing his two great passions, mountaineering and magick.1
Although only 24, Crowley was already one of the highest initiates of
the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, the most important fin de
siècle occult fraternity in the West. He considered himself an adept in
magick, but the futility of his endeavors and a sense of dissatisfaction
were now starting to assert themselves with increasing force. When
a fellow mountaineer, Oscar Eckenstein (1859–1921), joined him in
Mexico toward the end of the year, Crowley confided his concerns to
his friend. Eckenstein, a railway engineer and an analytical chemist by
profession who apparently had no interest in the occult, gave a response
that addressed the heart of the matter: Crowley’s problems stemmed
from his inability to concentrate. “Give up your Magick, with all its
romantic fascinations and deceitful delights,” Eckenstein advised him.
“Promise to do this for a time and I will teach you how to master your
mind” (Crowley, 1969: 213–4).
Crowley agreed to the proposal and before long immersed himself in
a set of exercises devised by Eckenstein, the purpose of which was to
enable his thoughts to remain focused on a chosen mental image or a
sensory input. These exercises were in their essence the initial steps in
the path of Yoga, undertaken by a person who was later to become one of
the principal advocates of yogic and, to a lesser degree, tantric teachings
in the field of occultism. Crowley’s influence on the twentieth century

India, Western Esotericism, and Crowley
The tendency toward syncretism is one of the defining characteris-
tics of Western esotericism. By the end of the nineteenth century, this
tendency was strongly manifest as openness toward Eastern, most
notably Indian, religious traditions. These were otherwise becom-
ing increasingly familiar through numerous translations of original
texts and through popular accounts written by colonial travelers. A
landmark event in this regard, as far as Western esotericism is con-
cerned, was the formation of the Theosophical Society in 1875.4 This
same year also gave birth to arguably the most important and influ-
ential occultist of the twentieth century, Edward Alexander—better
known as Aleister—Crowley. In his voluminous writings and in his
own spiritual practice and teaching, Crowley engaged and incorpo-
rated elements of Indian Yoga and Tantra to a significant degree. It
may be safely claimed that Yoga on the one hand and the Western
esoteric tradition in general (including magic, alchemy, astrology, and
Kabbalah) on the other hand form the twin aspects of what Crowley
called magick.5 In addition to this, he often asserted that Eastern and
Western esoteric traditions share a fundamental resemblance, which
he attempted to elucidate and which he regularly emphasized.
A clarification is appropriate at the outset. While Crowley’s engage-
ment with Indian Yoga is a straightforward affair that may be easily doc-
umented on the basis of his theoretical writings, practical instructions,
and personal records of practice,6 his involvement with Tantra is much
more complex and controversial. To a significant extent, this involvement
shares primarily functional parallels with the tantric path. It is feasible
to recognize in the whole project of Crowley’s magick an analogy with
the approach of tantra, even if his formal knowledge of the latter was
limited. I will, below, anchor my arguments by focusing on three areas

In an early short text entitled “Postcards for Probationers” (1909b),
Crowley set out to establish a parallelism between the methods of Indian
Yoga and Western ceremonial magic. He defined each discipline as “the
art of uniting the mind to a single idea.”30 Thus, Jñāna Yoga and the Holy
Kabbalah represent “Union by Knowledge.” Rāja Yoga and the Sacred
Magic stand for “Union by Will.” Bhakti Yoga and the Acts of Worship
exemplify “Union by Love.” Finally, Hatha Yoga and the Ordeals stand
for the methods of “Union by Courage.”31 Here again we have a clear
evidence of the practice of concordance, which Faivre asserted as a major
component of Western esotericism.32 Throughout his career, Crow-
ley consistently argued a deep similarity between the assumptions and
methods of Eastern and Western esoteric traditions.33 In doing so, he
postulated the human mind as the fons et origo of mystical and magical
phenomena34 and saw its cultivation as the unifying element behind the
multiplicity of various local traditions:
All phenomena of which we are aware take place in our own minds, and
therefore the only thing we have to look at is the mind; which is a more
constant quantity over all the species of humanity than is generally sup-
posed. (Crowley, 1991a: 13–4)
Crowley thus interprets even the traditional magical paraphernalia from
a mentalist perspective: the temple is coterminous with the extent of one’s
consciousness, the magical circle protects one from hostile thoughts,
the wand symbolizes the will, the cup is understanding, and the sword
refers to the analytical faculty.35 Similarly (Crowley, 1972: 104), “To call
forth the Spirits means to analyze the mind; to govern them means to
recombine the elements of that mind according to one’s will.” This is a
significant reinterpretation of magical technique, which is otherwise
habitually associated with ritual action. Crowley did not neglect ritual,

but by placing emphasis on mental concentration as the key to success he
was elucidating an aspect of magick that has a common denominator in
the practice of Yoga.
Eight Lectures on Yoga are a series of talks that Crowley delivered to
small audiences in the upper rooms of London restaurants, starting the
series in January 1937.36 The third lecture dealt with one of the prelimi-
nary stages of classical Yoga, the concept of discipline, or niyama. Pata-
ñjali, the author of the foundational Yoga Sūtras, defines this practice as
consisting of “Purity, contentment, asceticism, self-study, and surrender
to the Lord.”37 In his elucidation of the concept, Crowley (1991a: 36) takes
advantage of “a sort of Abacus,” which he alleges to be “very useful in all
kinds of thinking.” This Abacus is the kabbalistic Tree of Life. Simply put,
the Tree of Life as used in Western occultism is a symbolic representa-
tion of the totality of existence, consisting of ten circles, or sephiroth,
connected by 22 paths, arranged in a particular design. Each of these
circles and paths is a focal point for a cluster of correspondences.38 In
accordance with a standard practice, the planets of the solar system in
their astrological signification are also assigned their appropriate posi-
tions on the Tree.
Crowley explains the qualities of the planets as understood by West-
ern astrology and in the process stresses the “virtue” or “the positive
power” of each of the planets as an aspect of its niyama. Thus, Saturn—
traditionally correlated with the human skeleton—represents the firm
foundation of one’s spiritual practice. In addition, melancholy associated
with Saturn carries a virtue of “the Trance of Sorrow that has determined
one to undertake the task of emancipation” (1991a: 36). Jupiter is “the
vital, creative, genial element of the cosmos” (1991a: 38). Mars refers to
energy and strength associated with the muscular system. Its niyama is
“the virtue which enables one to contend with, and to conquer, the physi-
cal difficulties of the Work” (1991a: 38). The Sun is harmony and beauty,
the heart of the system as of the human being. The niyama of Venus con-
sists in ecstasy and graciousness. Mercury relates to intellectual powers.39
Finally, the niyama of the Moon is “that quality of aspiration, the positive
purity which refuses union with anything less than the All” (1991a: 42).

yogic discipline and Western astrology in its connection to the Tree of
Life lies in several factors. In addition to its manifest value as an exercise
in comparative esotericism, the correlation serves the purpose of illus-
trating the point that “similar methods producing similar results are to
be found in every country. The details vary, but the general structure is

“Liber XV, Ecclesiæ Gnosticæ Catholicæ Canon Missæ,” or simply
“The Gnostic Mass,”42 is a major OTO ritual that Crowley composed in
1913 while in Russia. This hieratic ceremony is one of the quintessential
expressions of Crowley’s religious philosophy, and it combines influences
and ideas from Western Gnosticism, Crowley’s Thelema, and, arguably,
Hindu Tantra. The principal officers of the Mass are the Priest and the
Priestess, who are assisted by the Deacon and the two “Children.” This
is a synopsis of the ritual: the Priestess enters the Temple and “wakes
up” the “dead” Priest from his tomb (an event symbolized by the lift-
ing of the Priest’s lance, with an obvious sexual referent). Together they
approach the high altar at the opposite end of the Temple, upon which
the Priest installs the Priestess, who then hides behind the veil. The Priest
invokes the goddess Nuit, a major deity in the Thelemic pantheon, iden-
tified as the “Infinite Space, and the Infinite Stars thereof.”43 The Priest-
ess answers, becoming at this point one with the goddess, whose speech
from The Book of the Law she now quotes. Now the veil is rendered apart
and the Priestess is seen holding the cup with wine, or the “Holy Grail,”
in her hand. The so-called “Cake of Light,” an equivalent of Eucharist, is
consecrated and placed on the tip of the Priest’s lance, whereupon the tip
of the lance is pressed into the cup, allowing a part of the Cake of Light to
dip into the wine within. The Priest eats the rest of the Cake of Light and
drinks the wine, after which he declares, “There is no part of me that is
not of the Gods.” This in effect represents the consummation of the ritual.

ceremony of the Mass as practiced within both the Catholic and Eastern
Orthodox Churches. However, although the form is Western and eccle-
siastical, the underlying process that the Mass portrays in its ritual mode

Sex Magick and Tantra
The Gnostic Mass is a public ritual, and as such it refers to the actual per-
formance of sex magick in a veiled form. It is clear from Crowley’s writ-
ings that he associated some aspects of sex magick with certain elements
of Tantra. Hugh Urban argues (2003c: 218–9), too strongly and not quite
correctly in my view, that “Crowley’s practice is the clearest example of
Western sexual magic combined (and perhaps hopelessly confused) with
Indian Tantra.”46 The methodological aspects of the practice of sexual
magick are elaborated for the most part in Crowley’s official instructions
for the highest degrees of the OTO, while the references to his actual
performance of this form of magick are scattered throughout his dia-
ries.47 Succinctly stated, in Crowley’s view the sex act is a sacrament,
and the consummation of sexual fluids a Eucharist. The key to success
in sex-magick ritual lies in the ability to concentrate one’s mind so that it
remains focused on the goal of operation, especially during the orgasm.
“For in the preparation of the Sacrament, and in its consummation also,”
writes Crowley (1974b: 216), “the mind of the Initiate must be concerned
absolutely in one rushing flame of will upon the determined object of
his operation.” He describes the essence of the practice by indicating the
similarity between sexual and meditative ecstasy, which is otherwise a
standard argument of a tantric orientation: “[T]he spiritual flower of this
process is that at the moment of [sexual] discharge a physical ecstasy
occurs, a spasm analogous to the mental spasm which meditation gives.
And further, in the sacramental and ceremonial use of the sexual act, the
divine consciousness may be attained.”48
Although it is not completely clear through which channels Crowley
arrived at the technique of sex magick—through intuition and books,
through actual contacts with Hindu and Muslim practitioners of simi-
lar rites, and/or through the OTO teachings49—there is no doubt that
similar methods have a long history of use in some forms of Hindu and
Buddhist Tantra. Belief in the potentially divine nature of the semen, so
strongly present in Crowley’s theories, is evidenced in Hinduism since
earliest times. In his study of asceticism in Vedic India, Walter O. Kaelber
(1989: 40; emphasis added) explores at length the notions of the fertil-
ity of the male seed—esoterically often associated with rain—and states:
“Male seed, even without benefit of female contribution, is fertile and

semen or seed retained increases in potency. It is capable of produc-
ing rain and fertilizing fields. Yet it is also capable of generating spiritual
rebirth and immortality.” (It needs to be said that both Indian Tantra and
Crowley display a tendency to overvalue the importance of the male
seed.) Since the retained semen brings about power, celibacy is in India
often encouraged: not necessarily because there is something inher-

perceived to be conducive to disease, aging, and ultimately death. The
connection between Eros and spirituality was eventually to receive the
highest emphasis in some theories and practices associated with Tantra.50
In the system of the Nāth Siddhas, a North Indian tantric tradition
credited with the development of Hatha Yoga,51 the semen, or bindu, was
perceived as the carrier of immortality. It, however, continuously drips
from its origin at the top of the head and either gets burned by the diges-
tive fire in the stomach or is ejaculated through the sexual act. Crowley
was familiar with important yogic treatises composed from the Nāthist
perspective, such as the Gheranda Samhitā, Śiva Samhitā, and Hatha
Yoga Pradīpikā,52 so it is quite possible that he adopted ideas about the
divine potential of (male) sexual fluid(s) from these sources. However,
there are differences: the Nāths are habitually celibate, and their main
objective is to achieve the return of the semen to the top of the head
through the manipulation of bodily postures, muscular contractions,
breathing exercises, and meditation.53 Alternatively, some forms of tan-
tric practice allow for the actual sexual congress to occur, but the male
adept is not supposed to release his semen. As Wendy D. O’Flaherty
(1973: 262; emphasis added) explains, “The upward motion of the
seed . . . represents the channeling of the life forces, and in order for the
ritual to be effective it was essential for the yogi to restrain his seed.” If on
the other hand the seed had been ejaculated, it was necessary to reabsorb
it, sometimes through the urethral suction (vajroli mudrā). In Crowley’s
practice, however, the semen is emitted, commingled with female sexual
fluids, and orally consumed.54

The Occult Aspects and Powers of the Human Body:
Cakras and the Kundalinī
The theory and practice of Yoga and Tantra postulate the existence and
spiritual importance of hidden aspects of the human body. Within the
gross material body there is another, subtle body (sūksma śarīra), consist-
ing of the centers of energy positioned along the axis that stretches from
the genital organs, through the spinal column, to the top of the head.
These subtle centers are customarily called “wheels” (cakras) or “lotuses”
(padmas), and their numbers are habitually given as four in Buddhist,
and six or seven in Hindu Tantra. It is assumed that the cakras are latent
or “asleep” in the case of an ordinary person. When awakened, however,
they bring about occult powers (siddhis) and gnostic insights. The tantric
teachings conceptualize that the primary spiritual energy lies at the base
of the spine in the form of the “coiled snake” kundalinī.62 This “serpent
power,” kundalinī śakti, is a microcosmic equivalent of the Great God-
dess, whose divine spouse, Śiva, has his own esoteric dwelling place on
the top of the human head. When the two appear as separate, the result

[Page 56]
48 INDIA AND THE OCCULT
is the illusory existence suffused with pain, in which the ordinary people
live. If a yogi manages to bring these two inner divinities together—by
making the kundalinī rise along the spinal column until she reaches the
top of the head, “waking up” the cakras along the way—the result will be
spiritual awakening. In this manner, the yogi gains immense powers and
effectively becomes a “second Śiva.”
There are numerous references to the cakras and kundalinī in Crow-
ley’s writings. The earliest mention and description of the cakras is given
in the fourth installment of the serial “The Temple of Solomon the King”
in The Equinox I, 4 (1910), written by J. F. C. Fuller, but incorporating a
good number of quotations from Crowley’s diaries. There is in this issue
an illustration showing a yogi with the seven cakras along the central axis
of his body. At a later date, Crowley added notes to his own copy of the
book, providing the correlation between the cakras and the introductory
degrees of the OTO.63 It is not entirely clear whether the intention was
to suggest that the OTO rituals actually “activate” the cakras during the
initiation of the candidate. It is however important that the two esoteric
traditions—represented by references to the Western magical fraternity
and Indian Yoga—were brought into correlation on the basis of the per-
ceived analogical convergence of their respective properties, of their
mutual conceptual and symbolic correspondence.
As far as the practical work with the cakras is concerned, of partic-

called “Liber Yod.”64 The text is introduced as providing “three meth-

One.”65 The first method is anchored in the Western magical tradition
and consists of a series of banishing rituals that refer to the planets, zodi-
acal signs, and finally the sephiroth on the Tree of Life. The rituals cul-
minate in the banishment, we might say deconstruction, of the symbolic
order represented by the highest sephira, Kether or the “crown.” In this
final phase, the magician tramples his foot upon the light of the candle
and falls outside the circle that is symbolic of his individual conscious-
ness. This represents the return to the primordial condition prior to the
manifestation of the phenomenal universe, before the emergence of the
separate sense of identity and the discriminative mind.

Cefalù: Decadence and Transgression as a Spiritual Technique
In order to further assess those of Crowley’s practices that contain tantric
characteristics, I suggest that we focus on certain controversial76 episodes
that took place at the Abbey of Thelema. The Abbey was established in
a small Sicilian town of Cefalù in the period between 1920 and 1923. It
consisted of one large house occupied by a small number of Crowley’s
disciples and mistress(es), and an outbuilding called the “Umbilicus.”
Life at the Abbey was for the most part Crowley’s attempt to translate his
magical and Thelemic ideas into social reality. For the participants, the
regime of life involved a great deal of occult and sex-magic activity as well
as experiments with various mind- and mood-altering substances, such
as hashish, cocaine, heroin, and opium.77 Crowley wrote extensively and
claimed to have attained the highest grade of the A∴A∴78 during this
period, but life at the Abbey was far from the ideal. In addition to internal
turmoil and frictions, he and his community became the frequent target
of the yellow press, with Crowley being labeled as “the wickedest man in
the world” and “the man we’d like to hang.”79 He was eventually expelled
from Italy by the order of Benito Mussolini.80
In a certain sense, life at the Abbey of Thelema may be taken to para-
digmatically represent Crowley’s lifestyle and philosophy in their most
intense aspects. He considered himself the prophet of the New Aeon,
which was to replace the old patriarchal religions that are most typi-
cally exemplified by Christianity. He was thus in a very important sense
asserting his self-identity and pursuing his orientation against the grain
of what was accepted as normative by his contemporary society, religion,
and culture. I would like to single out this element of intentional opposi-
tion to the normative societal values and emphasize its consanguinity
with the tantric worldview in general. The ethics of Tantra are often char-
acterized by opposition to Brahminic priestly orthodoxy, and its method
of practice is often defined as the process of regression.81 This regres-
sion, or going against the current—ultā or ujāna sādhana—addresses
an array of lifestyle choices as well as the actual methodology of tantric
Yoga. Crowley (1972: 248; emphasis in the original) expresses the same
orientation when he writes, “I recognize Magick as concerned to reverse
any existing order.” This implies the transformation and transcendence
of everyday profane reality by the method of going against its flow. From
this perspective, the profane world is topsy-turvy and is set aright only by
being turned upside down. This is a difficult task, and for that reason the
tantric practitioner is often described as the “hero” (vīra).

A newcomer to the Abbey of Thelema was expected to spend a night
in what Crowley designated as the “Chambre des Cauchemars.” Crowley
himself had painted, in vivid colors and sinister imagery, its murals rep-
resenting hell, heaven, and earth. The intention was
to pass students of the Sacred Wisdom through the ordeal of contemplat-
ing every possible phantom which can assail the soul. Candidates for this
initiation are prepared by a certain secret process before spending the
night in this room; the effect is that the figures on the wall seem actually to
become alive, to bewilder and obsess the spirit that has dared to confront
their malignity.82
The “secret process” most probably referred to the use of a psychedelic,
possibly mescaline.83 Crowley describes the outcome of the ordeal as
follows:
Those who have come successfully through the trial say that they have
become immunized from all possible infection by those ideas of evil
which interfere between the soul and its divine Self. Having been forced
to fathom the Abysses of Horror, to confront the most ghastly possi-
bilities of Hell, they have attained permanent mastery over their minds.
The process is similar to that of “Psycho-analysis”; it releases the subject
from fear of Reality and the phantasms and neuroses thereby caused, by
externalizing and thus disarming the spectres that lie in ambush for the
Soul of Man.84
Although Crowley compared the process of facing and conquering one’s
fears with the method of psychoanalysis, there is here also a functional
parallel to a standard procedure in the practice of Tantra, which consists
of spending the night and performing rituals or meditating in the crema-
tion ground or in a similar fear-inspiring place. Mircea Eliade (1969:269;
emphasis added) suggests that by meditating at the cemetery, the tantric
yogi “more directly achieves the combustion of egotistic experiences; at
the same time, he frees himself from fear, he evokes the terrible demons and
obtains mastery over them.” This is the philosophy of the method that car-
ries associations to the Nietzschean precept that what does not kill you
makes you stronger.

argued against overly philosophical and whitewashed representations
of Tantra. “Too often scholars have equated Tantra with a philosophi-
cal school enshrined in ancient Sanskrit texts,” claims Kripal (1998: 28),

inner limitations and psychological barriers. Crowley was possessed
of an intuitive understanding of the transformational power of sexu-

(Crowley, 1969: 80), he asserted his sense of independence and rebel-

of his first sexual acts with a servant maid on his mother’s bed. Kri-

fear—these three must not remain,” as one of the definitions of Tantra.
This precept is equally applicable to Crowley and his own experiments in
conquering shame, disgust, and fear. He argued:
The Magician should devise for himself a definite technique for destroying
“evil.” The essence of such a practice will consist in training the mind and
the body to confront things which cause fear, pain, disgust, shame and the
like. He must learn to endure them, then to become indifferent to them,
then to analyze them until they give pleasure and instruction, and finally
to appreciate them for their own sake, as aspects of Truth. When this has
been done, he should abandon them, if they are really harmful in relation
to health and comfort. (1997: 579–80)

In July 1920, a Hollywood silent film actress. Jane Wolfe (1875–1958),

attraction between the two. When she finally met Crowley face to face,
she was appalled by his unkempt looks and by the general state of affairs
at the Abbey. Only later was she to learn from another of Crowley’s disci-

the “mystery of filth.”85 It appears that Crowley was guided in this prac-

Since the pioneering work of Mary Douglas (1970), scholars have been
alerted to the complexity of human behavior and emotions surrounding
the issue of purity and dirt. Alexis Sanderson has thus contrasted the
Brahminic fixation on the rules of purity with the tantric deliberate dis-
regard of the same:
The conscientiousness essential to the preservation of purity and social
system was to be expelled from his identity by the Tantric Brahman as
impurity itself, the only impurity he was to recognize, a state of ignorant
self-bondage through the illusion that purity and impurity, prohibitedness
and enjoinedness were objective qualities residing in things, persons and
actions. (1985: 198)
The functional parallel to this spiritual orientation is provided in Crow-
ley’s intentional exploration of the “mystery of filth.” He writes in his
magical diary (1972: 257; emphasis added) about “a protest against . . .
the thought that anything is common or unclean.” Even more intense in
this respect were some forms of his practice of sexual magick, conducted
with the same objective of transcending the sense of shame and disgust.
Crowley’s principal partner during the Cefalù years was an American
citizen of Swiss origin, Leah Hirsig (1883–1975). She was in this period his
“Scarlet Woman,” a role designated by The Book of the Law, a female coun-
terpart to Crowley as the Beast. (It is significant, in the light of present con-
siderations, that Crowley (1974c: 103) defines these two officers as follows:
“The Beast and the Scarlet Woman are avatars of . . . Shiva and Shakti.”)
In addition to being each other’s principal partner, both Crowley and
Hirsig had sexual relations with other persons. Crowley’s ideas on sexual-
ity were in agreement with the liberal injunctions expressed in The Book
of the Law, which contains statements such as “take your fill and will of
love as ye will, when, where, and with whom ye will!”87 and “The word of
Sin is Restriction. O man! refuse not thy wife, if she will! O lover, if thou
wilt, depart! There is no bond that can unite the divided but love: all else is
a curse.”88 Commenting on the last verse, Crowley (1996: 42) writes: “The
sexual act is a sacrament of Will. To profane it is the great offence. All true
expression of it is lawful; all suppression or distortion is contrary to the
Law of Liberty.” Crowley’s sexual life was consequently uninhibited and
abundant, and included both heterosexual and homosexual liaisons.89

their affair. It might be safely said that Eros and Thanatos were intensely

intentional pursuit of those experiences, which one regards with “shame,
fear, and disgust,” is also a distinctive orientation in the method of Tantra,
whether conceptualized as the “conscious use of decadence as a spiritual
technique” (Kripal, 1998: 29) or as the path that seeks “power through
impurity” (Sanderson, 1985: 200). As Kripal (1998: 290) suggests, it
is a tantric notion that “pollution and impurity can be used to induce
mystical states.” In addition, Crowley’s method of sexual magick bears
a “family resemblance” to Indian Tantra through the paramount impor-
tance attached to the human body. In Tantra as in Crowley’s magick, the
human body is both the instrument and the locus of gnosis. One can gen-

to sublimate the goal of the religious quest. Crowley is similar to those
tantric practitioners who maintain the necessity or even supremacy of
embodied experience and bodily gnosis. This attitude is also congenial to
alchemy as the science of transformations (of “base metals” into “gold”),
and it is interesting to note that in India there was a close connection
between tantric Yoga and the methods of alchemy. In Crowley’s case, this
orientation was yet another application of his fundamental precept that
