## Section: Conclusions

Conclusions
Western occultists’ approach to India and her spirituality is rooted
in the long tradition of analogical thinking that, together with
the practice of concordance, forms the basis on which they build their
interpretations, and in particular their claim of the mutual correspondence
and similarity between Yoga, Tantra, and the occult. And while this claim,
like any other, is open to criticism, what needs emphasizing is that its
nature is primarily religious and that methodological conventions and
epistemological convictions differ between scientific and religious truth-
claims. The contingent nature of human knowledge about reality makes
any absolute claim of veracity and objectivity to at least some degree
suspicious. There is thus, by that very fact, a place for a Western occultist
imagining of India besides other imagined constructs of the same place
and its culture. To the degree that there is selection among the data,
that there exist personal preferences and agendas, social and cultural
influences, sexual and gender conditionings, political motivations, and a
host of other factors involved at each and every form of human cognition
and expression, what has been conceived of and expressed will be to that
degree colored by those factors and in that sense subjectively constructed.
There is no India, there are many Indias: the India of a male Brahmin
differs and is differently understood, experienced, and imagined than that
of an untouchable woman; a Hindu India is not the same as the Muslim
India; scholarly India is different from the occult India. Whether or not
the Western occultists studied here knew a lot about the “real” India, the
fact remains that they wrote about it and felt compelled to compare it
positively to what they have been engaged in throughout their career,
which was the pursuit of the occult.
But is it imperialistic? Is such appropriation of Indian tradition a ves-
tige of postcolonial mentality? Is this an example of an uncritical lumping
together of disparate ideas and practices, a syncretic mishmash of incom-
petently conceived notions about unrelated phenomena? Do Tantra and
Yoga belong only to India and Hinduism? These are challenging questions.

However, it is a common fact, as we already saw, that tantric traditions
exist (or have existed) in Indian Buddhism, Jainism, and even some
forms of Islam, and we know that there are (or used to be) more or less
developed tantric traditions in Tibet, Bhutan, Mongolia, China, Japan, and
Southeast Asia. If Tantra is obviously not exclusively an Indian (or Hindu,
or Buddhist) phenomenon, it follows that there is no inherent reason to
regard it as a solely Asian tradition, especially since it is apparent that
Tantra was always able to adapt to diverse ideological positions and cultural
environments.
Mutual influences, borrowings, appropriations, and cross- pollinations
between ideas and practices are regular occurrences in cultural and
religious history, and there are many precedents to the Western occult-
ist embrace of Indian Yoga and Tantra and their mapping of the same
on the model of the kabbalistic Tree of Life. In order to contextualize
this remark, let us take a brief look at the confluence of yogic, tantric,
and Islamic currents of thought and types of practice in South Asian
Sufism. Carl Ernst studied this subject in a number of elegant and eru-
dite essays, and he in particular interrogated the peculiar position of the
yogic text called “The Pool of Nectar” (Amrtakunda) and its translations
into the Arabic, Persian, and even Turkish languages as well as its popu-
larity among some Sufi groups in and outside of India. Shaman Hatley
similarly explored the “Islamization”1 of Yoga in Bengal and provided
examples of a correlation, among other things, between the concept of
cakras and the Sufi notion of maqāms, the “stations” or “stages” that a
Muslim mystic passes through along the spiritual path (2007: 355). This
particular correlation is comparable to the one that Crowley, Fortune,
and Grant establish between yogic cakras and kabbalistic sephiroth. Hat-
ley makes clear that in addition to influences and borrowings, there are
also noticeable differences: sometimes hardly more than taxonomic, as
when, for example, Hindu deities presiding over the cakras are replaced
by the archangels governing respective cosmological spheres (2007: 356),
at other times more significant, as when the primary locus of spiritual
attention is moved from the yogic cakra on the top of the head to the
one located at the heart, preferred by the Sufis (2007: 357). At an even
more important ideological level, Bengali Sufis transform the ultimate
goal and purpose of the tantric sādhana so that it becomes a means for
the tawhīd, “the egoless absorption into pure awareness of the presence
of God” (Hatley, 2007: 367).
