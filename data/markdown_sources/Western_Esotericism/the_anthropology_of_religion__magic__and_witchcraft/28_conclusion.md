# The Anthropology of Religion, Magic, and Witchcraft

## Section: Conclusion

Conclusion
The interface of magic, religion, and medicine is of interest to anthropologists who spe-
cialize in medical anthropology, the cross-cultural study of medical systems. Of particular
interest to medical anthropologists are people’s beliefs and values as they confront illness
and death. Every culture has explanatory models that explain why or how people get sick
and whether or not therapy should be sought.
Medical anthropology came into being after World War II during the Marshall Plan,
which was developed in 1947 when Secretary of State George C. Marshall allocated $17
billion to rebuild war-torn Europe. Though separate from the Marshall Plan, monies were
also allocated to help Asian countries ravished by the war, including Indonesia, China,
and the Philippines. The rise of applied anthropology—using anthropologically gained
knowledge about a culture to help people within that culture—came about around the
same time. (Medical anthropology is a specialized area within applied anthropology.)
It is noteworthy that faith healing, characteristic of the charismatic Christian sects and
other religious systems, is very similar to shamanic practices. Jim Jones, founder of the
People’s Temple, led over 700 followers to commit suicide from drinking cyanide-laced
Kool-Aid in Guiana in November 1978. He had used magical tricks to “cure” members
of the audience who came to see him when the Temple was in California. Some of these
religious sects perform exorcisms and various types of faith healing. Years ago, a former
cameraperson for a TV evangelist said that people faked being healed about 50 percent of
the time, but she indicated that about 50 percent of the healings seemed genuine.
Summary
Magic, medicine, and religion coalesce in the therapeutic setting as individuals and fami-
lies are compelled to make therapeutic decisions when confronting illness and death. In

any culture, medical beliefs are subsumed into the larger category of religious beliefs. Even
though most of us are ignorant of the pharmacological effects of modern medicine, we still
seek their magical efficacy.
Arthur Kleinman presents explanatory models through the disease/illness dichotomy.
Disease is the etic model of Western medicine, whereas illness pertains to culturally and
individually constructed emic models. Whereas Western practitioners focus on how symp-
toms occurred, cultural models stress why they happened. In other words, who or what
was offended, or what tabus were broken? Despite their differences, diagnosis and prog-
nosis are important. Non-Western societies are socio-centric because families and com-
munities care for patients, whereas Western societies that are ego-centric isolate patients
in hospitals. Anthropologists who work in clinical settings understand the importance of
negotiating medical models to provide culturally sensitive healthcare.
George Foster and Barbara Anderson contend that medical beliefs and practices in non-
Western or traditional societies are either personalistic or naturalistic. In personalistic
systems, illness is purposely caused by sensate agents like sorcerers, ancestors, and ghosts.
Personalistic medical systems are found in Indigenous societies. Shamans and other tradi-
tional healers are summoned for diagnosis and treatment.
Illness in naturalistic systems has nothing to do with sorcerers, supernatural agents, or
malice. Naturalistic medical models are impersonal, systemic systems characterized by
equilibrium, or balance of insensate agents, e.g., hot/cold, yin/yang, or humors. Imbalance
causing illness is associated with eating the wrong foods, changes in temperature, strong
winds, or other factors.
Although Western medicine came of age in the 1940s, vestiges of ancient medical beliefs
are apparent in symbols associated with pharmacies, ambulances, and barbershops. Today,
associated with Western biomedical facilities, we increasingly find access to religious and
spiritual resources, including things such as chapels, contemplative spaces, labyrinths, and
spiritual staff, such as chaplains.

Culture change is inevitable, as witnessed by world history. The last 500-plus years, how-
ever, have been distinguished by the European colonization of the Americas, Africa, Aus-
tralia, and the Pacific Islands. As a result of colonialization, imperialism, global trade,
warfare, migration, and generational changes, among many other factors, societies change
and adapt in response to external and internal pressures. Through an examination of world
history, there are numerous examples of revitalization movements, conscious attempts
“to reinstitute the traditional cultural values and beliefs of a group faced with dramatic
changes.”1 They are movements where people try to preserve lifeways that are rapidly
vanishing and are an attempt to “reconstitute a destroyed but not forgotten way of life.”2
Participants in revitalization movements are also attempting to bring about change in their
society.
Smithsonian Institution ethnologist James Mooney was the first to investigate a North
American revitalization movement—the Great Plains Ghost Dance (see further on)—which
anthropologist Ralph Linton later identified as a nativistic movement: “Any conscious,
organized attempt on the part of a society’s members to revive or perpetuate selected
aspects of its culture.” Linton suggested “that certain current or remembered elements of
culture are selected for emphasis and given symbolic value,”3 which is compatible with
current thoughts about the “ideological manipulation of heritage.”4 After the publica-
tion of the seminal Anthony F.C. Wallace article, “Revitalization Movements” in 1956,
anthropologists began focusing on revitalization movements among Indigenous popula-
tions in Africa, the Americas, the Caribbean, and Pacific Islands, where, in the context
of Western settler colonialism, Natives have been subjected to and oppressed by colonial
rule. Following Wallace’s lead article, many scholarly works have emphasized stress and
cultural deprivation in the analyses of classic revitalization movements.
Recent reinterpretations of the revitalization paradigm suggest that deprivation mod-
els best describe historical or classic revitalization movements, though anthropologist
Michael Harkin believes that deprivation “is only one possible state of relations between
aboriginal groups and colonial societies. Other relations include parity and ascendancy.”
Furthermore, it is the movement between states “rather than the absolute state, that will
stimulate revitalization. Thus, a movement from deprivation to parity or ascendancy may
provoke revitalization, just as the reverse trajectory did.”5 Instead of attributing revitaliza-
tion movements as responses only to deprivation and stress, Harkin’s all-inclusive model

Classic revitalization movements
British social anthropologist Victor Turner began ethnographic fieldwork among the Ndem-
bus (Congo culture area) in the early 1950s, where he observed how interest in “Western
individualism” had caused many “tribal religions [to] wilt and perish in a surprisingly short
time, and with them vanished the ritual symbols.” Colonizers, the “new social order,” had
introduced new symbols, along with social cohesion and instability; however, once rapid
social change slowed down and stabilized, traditional rituals were revived and infused with
some of the new symbols. Importantly, Turner noted that restored Ndembu rituals were
hybridized with new and old ritual symbols, making it difficult for him to separate “tradi-
tional from new social tendencies in . . . Ndembu behavior.”6 Like others that occur during
revitalization movements, these rituals represent “a collection of world views in flux.”7
The phenomenon Turner observed is now referred to as religious syncretism. By defini-
tion, syncretism is the blending of beliefs and values from two or more cultures. (Note
that in syncretism, cultures do not blend together, but ideas do as they are borrowed and
integrated among cultures.) Turner suggested that rapid culture change creates periods of
social adjustment where people are compelled to maintain cultural identity through adap-
tation, accommodation, and innovation. Syncretism occurs when diverse symbols come
together in new religions. Thus, syncretic integration occurs when “traditional and innova-
tive” patterns or symbols fuse together. The syncretic blending of vastly different cultural
beliefs and values is often characterized by social conflict and ideological competition.
Deprivation models have dominated studies of Indigenous revitalization movements. For
instance, historian Wilbur Jacobs used this model to examine revitalization movements
in Melanesia and Native North America based on acculturation, the process whereby a
culture is modified by traits from a dominant society. To Jacobs, acculturation has two
meanings: first, it is a two-way cultural exchange after first contact, where each group bor-
rows ideas or items of material culture from one another (e.g., American Indians appre-
ciated firearms and steel, whereas cotton, corn, and tobacco were rapidly incorporated
into European cultures); second, during the process of settler colonialism, acculturation
becomes a one-sided affair in which the colonizers expect the Indigenous peoples to aban-
don polygamy, communalism, and Native rites in favor of monogamy, individualism, and
Christianity. Missionaries and government officials typically are the agents of accultura-
tive pressures. Jacobs’ model recognizes three acculturative stages:
1. Initial contact with congenial relationships strengthened by gift giving and trade, gives
way to overt hostilities and warfare when Europeans begin encroaching on Indigenous

Revitalization movements are sometimes called nationalistic movements when subordi-
nated groups are expected to abandon their customs in favor of those of the colonizers.
Depopulation and discrimination by the dominant group produce cultural disruption and
breakdown of ethnic or tribal social organization. Members of the subordinate group are
thus reverted to a “marginal” status in the new social order. According to Jacobs’ model,
prolonged discrimination leads to “group unity and solidarity” among the “marginal per-
sons” who resent their subordinate status.
Anthropologist James Slotkin also interpreted revitalization movements based on stress
and deprivation models. Accordingly, the oppressed attempt to change their relative status
to the dominant group through nationalism, a social movement designed to “overthrow
the domination-subordination” relation by two different means:
1. Socially, through militant nationalism, where social means are used to achieve a new
social status. This implies that societal institutions, e.g., military, political, economic,
and religious, are utilized. Violence usually accompanies this type of social movement.
This model applies to many militant groups, such as the Taliban.
2. Culturally, by manipulating cultural symbols created to oppose the dominant group.
Nativistic nationalism is a type of revitalization movement featuring syncretic symbols,
in which acculturation brings about irreversible cultural change that makes it “impos-

Revitalization movements are also called transformative movements because the con-
stituents usually attempt to transform their low (or oppressed) position in the social order.
The newly formed religions are often comprised of distressed individuals who feel helpless
in the current social order; the movement awakens once apathetic and hopeless people
and unites them with a common prophetic destiny. Prophesies feature syncretic symbols
to project a “glorious future state” based on the “legends of a glorious past.” Associated
doctrines promise that everything affiliated with the dominant population will disappear
by the “rebirth” of the oppressed people.11
Revitalization movement rituals can be extremely elaborate and founded in Indigenous
ceremonialism or amorphous mass hysteria. Recall that rituals reflect the general attitudes
of the performers, as well as the prescribed course of action to resolve problems. Outside
observers, then, can learn the ethos, or religious values, through an examination of the
ritual symbols, which reflect the deprivation and destitution felt by the performers.
In “Revitalization Movements,” A.F.C. Wallace described six types of revitalization
movements and characteristic behavioral patterns in each:
1. Nativistic movements are oriented towards eliminating the dominant culture.
2. Revivalistic movements attempt to revive cultural traditions from the past or those that
are currently threatened.
3. Cargo Cults appear when Indigenous populations adopt “alien values, customs,
and material” wealth (cargo). Many cargo cults appeared in early twentieth-century
Melanesia.
4. Vitalistic movements involve the adoption of alien values and customs but without a
singular focus on cargo as the mechanism for transformation.
5. Millenarian movements promise a “supernatural transformation of the world into some
idealized state” or utopia.
6. Messianic movements involve a prophesied savior who will transform the world into an
idealized state.
The six types of revitalization movements and associated behaviors are not mutually
exclusive, so a particular movement can manifest many of these patterns.12

Haitian Vodou
Vodou originated in Haiti and spread throughout the Haitian diaspora. The Vodou reli-
gion is extremely rich in symbolism, with art and dance playing central roles in ritual.
Vodou grew out of several religions indigenous to West Africa, especially the religions of
the Fon, Kongo, and Yoruba peoples. The term vodou, used by outsiders to describe the
religion, comes from the Fon language of Dahomey (now Benin) and means “spirit” or
“deity.” Practitioners say that they “serve the spirits.”
History of Vodou
The country of Haiti occupies the western third of the Caribbean island of Hispaniola,
which was discovered by Europeans in 1492. In 1697, Haiti became a French colony. (The
rest of Hispaniola today is the Spanish-speaking country of the Dominican Republic.)
The French colony of Haiti eventually became one of the richest colonies in the Caribbean,
largely because of plantation agriculture dedicated to sugar cultivation and other extractive
activities, such as the production of molasses and rum. Sugarcane cultivation is very labor
intensive, and large numbers of slaves were brought from West Africa to work the plantations.
The slaves soon outnumbered the French colonists, who lived in constant fear of slave
rebellions. In the late seventeenth century, the French government decreed that all slaves
had to be baptized and instructed in the Catholic religion. Yet, other than the baptism
ceremony itself, slaves were given little or no religious instruction because few landowners

made to appease the lwa (Figure 12.2). Dance and music play major roles in Vodou ritual.

Cargo cults
A well-known historical example of nativistic movements is the cargo cults of New Guinea.
The term cargo cult comes from the word cargo, which in the pidgin English spoken in New
Guinea and the Melanesian islands means “trade goods.” These movements began along
the coast in the late nineteenth century but reached their peak during and after World War II
when the U.S. military brought in large quantities of manufactured goods.
When European explorers, missionaries, and colonial administrators first entered the
area, they brought with them a wealth of manufactured goods that sparked the imagina-
tions of the Native peoples and became highly desirable items. The newcomers were seen
as conduits for material goods and were perceived as being very powerful. In the context
of Native culture, power comes from knowledge of the supernatural. Thus, the activities
of the missionaries resonated with the population, and Christianity became of interest
partly to discover the ritual secrets that the missionaries used to bring material wealth
(cargo) by sea from what the Natives perceived as the Land of the Dead.
Soon, however, it became clear to the local people that the key to controlling the cargo
was not in Christian rituals. They believed the missionaries refused to share the magi-
cal secrets with them, and other Europeans were unwilling to share their goods with
the Natives. They also resented the condescending attitude and strange appearance and
behavior of the Europeans toward the Natives.
This disillusionment led to the emergence of a number of stories about the origin and
control of the cargo and the power of the outsiders. The Europeans did no obvious work
and engaged in a number of very strange activities. One Native explanation was that the
manufactured goods must have been made in the Land of the Dead by the ancestors of
the Melanesians. The Europeans, through ritual, intercepted the ships and airplanes and
stole the cargo that was meant for the local people.
The solution to the problem was to discover and learn the Europeans’ magic. Then, the
people could rid the land of the outsiders and permit the ancestors to land the planes and
bring the cargo directly to their descendants. This would also usher in a period of paradise
on earth and, in some cases, the return of the ancestors.
Several cargo cults emerged over these years. They often appeared in response to a prophet
who believed they had discovered the secret used by the Europeans in controlling the cargo.
The European activities seen as magic rituals varied from place to place, but Natives attempted
to reproduce the rituals that they witnessed first-hand. They included making marks on paper,
running flags up poles, marching with sticks over their shoulders, dressing up in European-
style clothes, and sitting around a table with a vase of flowers in the center. One group cleared
a long strip of land in imitation of a landing strip, complete with a control tower.
As puzzling as these activities were to outsiders, in some movements, the prophet
announced that the ancestors and manufactured goods would not appear until the people
destroyed their own traditional sacred objects or exposed these objects to people who
were not supposed to see them, especially women and uninitiated boys. In other move-
ments, success would not happen as long as the people had adequate food, so pigs and
crops were destroyed. The results were tragic.
One of the best-known of the early cargo cults was the Vailala Madness, which occurred
between 1919 and 1923. It centered on divination trances. Old rituals were set aside and

[Page 366]
The search for new meaning 339
many Native ritual objects were destroyed, and new rituals, containing many Christian
elements and military-style activities, appeared. For example, messages from the dead
could be received through flagpoles. In 1932 and 1933, another cargo cult emerged among
the Buka people. They believed that steamships would arrive with cargo, and a large ware-
house was built to store these manufactured goods. However, the steamship would not
arrive as long as the people had food, so they destroyed their farms to get ready for the
expected arrival of the cargo. Magical thinking, common to almost all religious traditions,
led to these dire consequences.
The Ghost Dance movements of 1869 and 1890
Cultural disruption by settler colonists also set in motion Ghost Dance movements among
the Northern Paiutes in 1869 and 1890. The visions of Wodziwob, or Fish Lake Joe—
so-called because he lived in the Fish Lake area 90 miles southeast of Walker Lake—led
to the first movement. Unique to the Fish Lake Paiutes were mourning ceremonies and
cremations honoring the dead, practices also found among non-Paiute tribes to the west
in California. Moreover, Northern Paiutes of Fallon, Mason Valley, and Walker Lake all
performed rituals to bring back the dead.
Wodziwob’s visions fit perfectly into the cultural milieu of Natives living in western Nevada
and adjacent parts of California. Wodziwob’s visions began in the mid-1860s when he dreamt
about a dance in which men, women, and children formed a circle, facing inward, holding hands
with interlocking fingers, and shuffle-stepping clockwise. Performing the dance five nights in a
row fatigued some dancers, resulting in visions and new Ghost Dance songs. Wodziwob’s vision
also specified that if the dance should be performed properly at least 20 times a year, deceased
Indians would be resurrected to live a happy life, as opposed to the destitution of the living.
Deceased Anglos, however, would not be reborn. The 1869 Ghost Dance was a transformative
movement attempting to reverse deprivation suffered by the Northern Paiutes and their neigh-
bors, who suffered depopulation from diseases, land loss, starvation, and discrimination.
Not surprisingly, the Ghost Dance quickly spread to the northwest and southwest into
California and parts of the Great Basin. Northern Paiute communities adopting the dance
included Fish Lake, Owens Valley, Walker River, Walker Lake, and Mason Valley. South-
ern Paiutes, Northern Shoshones, Bannocks, and Eastern Shoshones from the Wind River
Reservation were other Basin groups who adopted the dance.
Unfulfilled prophesies caused the Ghost Dance to fall into disuse several years after its
inception, though it continued in modified form in some areas through the 1880s. Neverthe-
less, the 1869 Ghost Dance synthesized Northern Paiute beliefs in visions and circle dances
associated with luring antelopes into traps during antelope drives with beliefs in prophets,
prophesies, and the return of the dead. These latter traits were possibly borrowed from
Sahaptian or Salishan speakers in the Plateau and Northwest Coast culture areas, where
participants attempted to restore what had been lost through depopulation and deprivation.
It is noteworthy that Mormons perceived that the Ghost Dance heralded the Millen-
nium. During the 1870s, members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints used
parallel beliefs in the resurrection of the dead to their advantage to convert Bannocks and
Northern Shoshones at Fort Hall, as well as Eastern Shoshones. Through proxy, they also
baptized deceased Indians in the belief this would accelerate the Millennium.

The story behind the 1890 Ghost Dance was recorded by ethnologist James Mooney
beginning in January 1891, shortly after the massacre of several hundred Miniconjou
Lakota Ghost Dancers in South Dakota. The tragedy at Wounded Knee Creek quelled
the movement among the Sioux, but the Ghost Dance was still going strong among other
tribes, particularly the Northern Arapahos, who were proselytizing in Oklahoma Terri-
tory when Mooney arrived and witnessed a dance at the Darlington Agency in January
1891. A year later, Mooney traveled to Mason Valley, Nevada, to interview Wovoka, the
Northern Paiute whose vision started the revitalization movement that swept eastward to
the Plains in 1890.
Wovoka, “The Woodcutter,” also known by his Christian name, Jack Wilson, lived in
Mason Valley, 40 miles northwest of Walker Lake. Mooney traveled to Mason Valley in
early January 1892 to visit Wovoka, who related the great revelation he had received on
January 1, 1889, during a solar eclipse. Wovoka “died” and was taken into heaven.
Here he saw God, with all the people who had died long ago engaged in their oldtime
sports and occupations, all happy and forever young. It was a pleasant land and full
of game. After showing him all, God told him he must go back and tell his people they
must be good and love one another, have no quarreling, and live in peace with the
whites; that they must work, and not lie or steal; that they must put away all the old
practices that savored of war; that if they faithfully obeyed his instructions they would
at last be reunited with their friends in this other world, where there would be no more
death or sickness or old age. He was then given the dance which he was commanded to
bring back to his people. By performing this dance at intervals, for five consecutive days
each time, they would secure this happiness to themselves and hasten the event.16
The syncretic elements in Wovoka’s revelation probably derive from his association with
his father, Tavibo, a follow of Wodziwob, the 1869 Ghost Dance prophet. Wovoka received
Christian instruction and was taught to read the Bible by the Wilson family, who hired
him as a farmhand, and it is possible that Presbyterian, Catholic, and Mormon traits were
incorporated into his Ghost Dance teachings.17
Mooney explained the basic doctrine of the 1890 Ghost Dance:
The great underlying principle of the Ghost dance doctrine is that the time will come
when the whole Indian race, living and dead, will be reunited upon a regenerated earth,
to live a life of indigenous happiness, forever free from death, disease, and misery. On
this foundation each tribe has built a structure from its own mythology, and each apos-
tle and believer has filled in the details according to his own mental capacity or ideas of
happiness, with such additions as come to him from the trance.18
During the winter of 1889–1890, the prophecy of Wovoka spread throughout the Great
Basin, diffusing north and east to the Great Plains. The dance and other Ghost Dance
symbols were part of the religion, and each tribe interpreted the symbols according to its
own values.19 When Mooney quizzed Wovoka that cold, snowy January, the “prophet”
emphasized that he did not invent the “ghost shirts” that were so popular among the

Plains, and that he preached reconciliation, not conquest of the Anglo-American race.

evident by Mooney’s January 1892 interview, Wovoka obviously stopped proselytizing the
Ghost Dance in the wake of the Wounded Knee tragedy. Nevertheless, Wovoka held fast
to his vision and received visitors until his death in 1932. He traveled throughout Indian
country and survived by selling magpie and eagle feathers, red ocher like he gave Mooney,
and ten-gallon Stetson hats like the one he wore. Wovoka’s Indian followers believed these
items had healing attributes.22
Besides his vision that spawned the 1890 Ghost Dance, Wovoka is famous for two other
prophesies. He prophesied that his grandson would “one-day fly in the sky” and become
“a captain of men.” In 1944, his grandson, Vaptain Harlyn Vidovich of the Flying Tigers,
was shot down by the Japanese over China. His second vision specified that an earthquake
would occur following his death to prove that he had re-entered heaven. Three months
after his death on September 29, 1932, an earthquake shook Mason Valley and neighbor-
ing areas, confirming to his descendants that his prophecy was fulfilled.23
If you wonder what a Ghost Dance looks like, pay attention to the performance of the
Round Dance next time you attend an intertribal powwow. The Round Dance is derived
directly from the round dances performed in the Great Basin for the Ghost Dance.
The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Mormonism)
Many new religious movements emerged in the United States in the early nineteenth cen-
tury. It was a time of great stress and crisis for a country heading into a Civil War. The
Industrial Revolution brought many changes in traditional lifestyles, including the move-
ment of many people to cities and the subsequent breakdown of old ideas of community.
The proliferation of different Christian sects—new branches of mainstream religion—led
to choice fatigue, as a single dominant church was replaced by numerous options. Out of
this stress grew many revitalization movements, including the Seventh Day Adventists and
the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (LDS).
The LDS Church was founded by Joseph Smith (1805–1844), who grew up in New York,
where his family were seekers—what we might call nondenominational Christians. Smith

[Page 369]
342 The search for new meaning
was troubled by the number of Christian sects that existed and wanted to find the “true
Christianity.” Smith received his first vision at the age of 14. God and Jesus came to him and
told him that all of the various sects were in error and that he should not join any of them.
A few years later, when Smith was 17, he had three visitations from the angel Moroni to
prepare Smith as a prophet to restore the “true Christian Church.” Moroni revealed to him the
location of golden tablets containing additional biblical history, and Smith was able to use
decoding stones buried with the tablets to translate them into what is now known as the Book
of Mormon: Another Testament of Jesus Christ. This book did not replace the Christian Bible
but rather supplemented it. Mormon was an ancient prophet who was believed to have com-
piled the sacred record, the source of the name Mormons for members of the LDS Church.
The early LDS Church was heavily persecuted, and in 1844, Smith was jailed for his destruc-
tion of an opposition printing press in Nauvoo, Illinois. While he was incarcerated, a mob
attacked the jail and killed Smith and his brother, provoking a crisis in the group. However, God
sent a revelation that Brigham Young would be the next leader, and Young led them to what
became Salt Lake City, Utah. There, the LDS Church encountered difficulties with both Native
Americans already living in the area and the U.S. government, which refused Utah recognition
as a state because of the LDS practice of polygyny. Utah finally received statehood after the LDS
leadership received an 1890 revelation from God that disallowed plural marriages.
In some ways, LDS beliefs are similar to those of evangelical Christianity, including the
literal truthfulness of the Bible, atonement, resurrection, and tithing. However, there are
also significant differences. For example, while most conservative Christians believe that
salvation is based on faith alone, Mormons believe that salvation also requires good works.
In Chapter 9, we discussed the concept of the Trinity for monotheistic Christianity—an
indivisible single God in three manifestations. Among Mormons, the Trinity is seen as the
Godhead, composed of three separate beings with different identities. Smith described God
as self-made, finite, and having a material body. Jesus is “God the Second, the Redeemer,”
and the Holy Spirit is a Spirit Personage.
Another obvious difference is the Book of Mormon, a history of a group of Israelites
who departed from the Middle East around 600 BCE, before the time of the Babylonian
captivity, and came to North America. This included a patriarch, Lehi, and his sons, two
of whom were named Nephi and Laman. Two tribes, the Nephites and Lamanites, are
descended from these two sons. The two groups lived in a state of continual feuding, and
eventually, the Lamanites killed off the Nephites (around 385 CE). The Book also says
that after his resurrection, Jesus came to North America, where he performed miracles,
delivered sermons, and selected twelve disciples from among the Nephite tribe.
The Mormons believe that these are the same doctrines that were held by the very early
Christian Church. They believe that they are restoring the original church of the apostles
to how it was in the first century of the Common Era.

Harkin’s newer revitalization movement model can be used to examine cultural revivals
that are not the result of deprivation and stress. This broader, more inclusive model suggests
that revitalization movements, whether they are politically or religiously motivated, occur
where indigenous populations are in recovery from oppression. Whereas the older model
adequately describes classic revitalization movements resulting from the stressors of set-
tler colonialism, the newer model helps explain contemporary cultural revivals that reflect
pride in tribal identity. In Belize, pride in Maya culture and medicinal plant lore is reflected
by numerous medicinal trails. Ecotourism is a major part of today’s Belizean economy.
New meaning in fundamentalism
The term fundamentalism originated in the nineteenth century and is associated with the
revitalization of a mainstream religion. Initially, it was used to refer to the opponents of lib-
eral Protestantism who were urging a return to the “fundamentals” of Christianity to guide
those whom they believed had lost their way. Among these fundamentals was a belief in the
inerrancy of the scriptures and a resulting millenarianism based on the Book of Revelation.

[Page 372]
The search for new meaning 345
Fundamentalism then spread to other scripture-based religions, mainly Judaism and Islam.
Judaism, in particular, has focused historically on debates, commentaries, and interpretations
of scriptures, and differences in these interpretations led to different Jewish denominations.
In Islam, however, the Qur’an is seen as the Word of God and is not treated as a historical or
literary text. Therefore, debates over meanings are not seen as challenging the foundations
or sanctity of Islamic script. Because of this, many Islamic and some Jewish groups question
the use of the term fundamentalism to refer to non-Christian traditions. Some Islamic writ-
ers prefer the terms “absolutism” or “extremism” instead of “fundamentalism.”
Over time, however, the use of the term fundamentalism has shifted from an emphasis
on religious scriptures to religious and social movements that share certain features. It is
that etic perspective that we use here.
Characteristics of fundamentalist groups
In many ways, fundamentalist groups are easier to define by what they are against than
what they represent. These groups protest against and fear modernization in general and
the secularization of society specifically. They see society as no longer focused on the big
questions of morality and salvation. Change is now prized over continuity and tradition;
an emphasis on production and commerce has replaced more traditional values; loyalty
to and identification with the state has replaced loyalty to and identification with one’s
religious group.
The fundamentalist worldview is focused on finding certainty, clarity, and simplicity in
an otherwise complex and uncertain world. They tend to see issues in terms of right and
wrong and reject the idea of relativism. Because of this mindset, they generally refuse to
engage in dialogue, compromise, or find common ground.
Richard Antoun describes fundamentalism
as an orientation to the modern world, both cognitive and emotional, that focuses on
protest and change and on certain consuming themes: the quest for purity, the search for
authenticity, totalism and activism, the necessity for certainty (scripturalism), selective
modernization, and the centering of the mythic past in the present.29
Totalism is a reaction to the increasing separation of religion from other domains of life.
Fundamentalists believe that religion is relevant to, and should be a part of, all parts of a
society. Religious texts play an important role in fundamentalist beliefs, and scripturalism
refers to the practice of justifying beliefs and actions through the religious text, generally
held to be inerrant. Another aspect of the importance of religious texts is the idea that
these texts are relevant to life today, what Antoun calls traditioning.
Other important themes of fundamentalist groups include millenarianism, anticipation
of Christ’s second coming, an ensuing 1,000-year period of peace, and a focus on the
perceived struggle between good and evil. Fundamentalist groups are characterized by
activism, and Antoun points out that “Fundamentalism is inherently oppositional and
minoritarian. It is the protest of those not in power.”30 By this, he means political and
cultural power, not necessarily economic power.

Although the themes of fundamentalist groups are very similar cross-culturally, indi-
vidual movements obviously have arisen in response to very different cultural and his-
torical circumstances. The growth of Christian fundamentalism in the United States was
a reaction to secular Protestant ideology, which was important in the early days of the
country. A belief in secular progress and ideas such as manifest destiny served to elevate
nationalism to the level of religion. In contrast, Islamic fundamentalism is largely a reac-
tion to Western colonialism and the extent of Western cultural and economic infiltration
into Islamic countries, and Jewish fundamentalism has its roots in reactions to the strong
anti-Semitism of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries in eastern and central Europe.
The Iranian revolution
Islamic fundamentalism is a movement grounded in social, religious, and economic stress-
ors that exist in many Muslim countries—since the fall of the Ottoman Empire in the
1920s, many Islamic countries have been politically marginalized globally. The colonial
context of political and economic domination is also an important backdrop. In many
areas of the Middle East, the borders established by European colonial powers in the early
twentieth century rarely coincided with the boundaries of preexisting communities. Ethnic
and regional diversities, as well as local loyalties to various tribes or religious sects, made
it difficult to integrate people into one nation.
Even after Muslim countries gained independence, colonial influence remained: Many
Muslim countries were ruled by a Westernized elite, and there was a plethora of mass-
produced Western goods and mass media. Although the 1970s increases in oil prices
brought wealth to the region, this was amassed by the elite and only served to accentuate
the relative deprivation of the majority of people. For many Muslims, this cultural and
economic domination by the outside was seen as a sign of God’s wrath and a call to return
to a strict adherence to the principles of the Qur’an. Islamic fundamentalism illustrates
many of Antoun’s themes described earlier, including scripturalism and traditioning.
Perhaps the best example of political activism in Islamic fundamentalism is the 1979 Ira-
nian Revolution. This was a religious revolution against secular nationalism, led by reli-
gious leaders and using religious idioms. An example of this was the use of the myth of the
Imam Husayn. The Shi’a Muslims in Iran focus not on the Prophet (like Sunni Muslims
do) but instead on his descendants. According to Shi’a tradition, almost all twelve of the
original imams, descending from the prophet Muhammad, died by violent means and at
the hands of the secular government in power at the time. These stories form the backdrop
for a worldview of alienation from society, and more particularly from the government,
which is seen as unjust and illegitimate.
Of particular importance is the story of the third imam, Husayn, the grandson of Moham-
med. Before the revolution, religious devotion centered on Husayn’s role as an intercessor
between humans and God. However, during the course of the revolution, a new interpre-
tation and emphasis emerged. The story is that in the seventh century, Husayn was on
a pilgrimage to Mecca when he heard that his adherents in Iraq were surrounded by an
army and needed his support. He broke off his pilgrimage, one of the five required pillars
of Islam, to go to them. In Iraq, he, his family, and his followers were martyred. The focus

shifted to Husayn as a revolutionary who believed that the struggle against oppression
was more important than even the performance of basic Muslim worship obligations.
At the time of the revolution, Iran was a constitutional monarchy ruled by Shah Moham-
med Reza Pahlavi. While it was a strict dictatorship with censorship laws and the impris-
onment of political activists, some rights and conditions in Iran had improved. The White
Revolution in the 1960s had begun a series of social, economic, and political reforms that
gave more freedom to women and emphasized secular over religious education. These
attempts to modernize Iran involved the help of the United States, who saw Iran as a
potentially stabilizing force in the region. The Shah, though, was opposed by many groups.
Some disliked the Shah’s autocratic rule and the corruption of the very wealthy royal fam-
ily. Religious leaders saw his rule as overly secular and tied to the West, particularly to the
United States.
The revolution against the Shah utilized the imagery of the story of Husayn. Demonstra-
tors yelled, “Everywhere is Kerbala, and every day is Ashura” (Kerbala and Ashura being
the place and time that Husayn was killed). The U.S. president at the time, Jimmy Carter,
was identified as Yazid, the ruler of the army at Kerbala, and the Shah was seen as Shimr,
the general sent by Yazid to kill Husayn. The United States was called the “Great Satan.”
On December 10, 1978, the Day of Ashura—the anniversary of the day that Husayn
was killed—two million demonstrators marched for hours. They carried flags of green,
red, and black, symbolizing Islam, martyrdom, and Shi’a. By the end, a resolution was
passed, and Ayatollah Khomeini, living in exile, was invited to become the new leader of
Iran. He returned in February 1979 and formed the Islamic Republic. Islamic law was re-
established, mandated religious instruction was reinstituted, and many new social norms
were instituted, including the veiling of women, a ban on alcohol and gambling, and the
censoring of all media for sexual content.

While the details and underlying theology may differ, these rules function to iden-
tify members of the group and to display one’s commitment to the group’s religious
practices. In Muslim countries, it is traditional for women to cover their heads, and
sometimes even their entire body, in the presence of non-family males. This is seen
as adherence to a level of modesty that is required by religious and social custom.
The nature of the covering varies from society to society. The most commonly
worn garment is the hijab, a headscarf that covers the head and neck. Other forms
of veiling include the niqab that covers the entire body except for an opening for
the eyes; the chador, a long shawl that covers the head and body but leaves the face
uncovered; and the burqa, a full-body veil with the eye-opening covered by mesh.

[Page 375]
348 The search for new meaning
The wearing of such coverings is mandated by law in several countries, such as Saudi
Arabia; in other countries, it is customary but optional.
In some communities undergoing assimilation, the hijab has become an important
symbol not only of a religious system but of one’s cultural origins and identity. In
France, which maintains a strict separation between religion and the state in the edu-
cation system, this became a major issue in 1989 when three teenage girls of North
African descent wore headscarves to school. When asked, they refused to remove
them and were subsequently expelled. This became known as the “headscarf affair”
and led to attempts to outlaw the wearing of headscarves in public schools.
In 2004, France passed a law banning the wearing of all conspicuous religious sym-
bols in French public schools. The law does not single out the hijab, and each school
must find its own interpretation of “conspicuous religious symbols.” A cross and the
Star of David are usually allowed, but in one case, a student was expelled simply for
wearing a long dress that came to her ankles. Yarmulkes (Jewish skullcaps) are fre-
quently allowed as cultural and social symbols rather than religious symbols.
The law is controversial, and debates focus on the rights of minority groups and
religious freedom in France. The hijab is also seen as an avoidance of assimilation by
stressing one’s ancestry and cultural heritage. France has experienced several acts
of terrorism perpetrated by religious extremists, and anti-Muslim feelings are on the
rise. For many, the hijab has become a symbol of the fear of the “other.”
Now, the debates have moved beyond the schools. On the Mediterranean coast of
France, many cities banned the burkini, a full-body swimsuit worn by Muslim women,
in response to terrorist attacks there in 2016. City officials argued that the burkinis
were associated with Islamic fundamentalism and would cause emotional distress
following the attacks. In Nice, France, the human rights group Collective Against
Islamophobia brought a court case saying it was discriminatory, Anti-Muslim, and
unconstitutional. The court suspended the ban as no public risk was shown. Some
have pointed out that full-body coverings worn by Catholic nuns are permitted on
the same beaches.
Under the new religious rule, Iran has been criticized for many human rights violations,
including the imprisonment and murder of critics of the regime. Although women had
gained many important rights under the Shah, these have all been lost. Patrols were formed
to confront women for such violations as wearing lipstick or showing their hair.
The Arab Spring
The “Arab Spring” uprisings are a recent catalyst of open resistance in Islamic coun-
tries. Beginning in Tunisia in 2011, resistance spread to Egypt, Yemen, Bahrain, Libya,
and Syria. The term “Arab Spring” was coined by the Western media in response to the

[Page 376]
The search for new meaning 349
successful uprising in Tunisia and referenced the fall of Communism in Eastern Europe in
1989, when most countries in the former Communist bloc adopted democratic political
systems and a market economy in a very short time frame. The expectation that a similar
thing would happen in the Middle East, with political systems collapsing in the face of
popular uprisings, was misguided. As opposed to what happened in Eastern Europe, in the
Middle East, there was no consensus on what should replace the existing system.
Two conflicting principles were at play in the uprisings: the desire for more implementa-
tion of Islamic fundamentalism on the one hand and the belief that secularism had to be
defended on the other. This has resulted in the rise of the Islamic State in Iraq and Syria.
Instead of political reform and social justice, there has been more war and more violence.
High demand religions
Besides fundamentalist movements, many new religious movements also have connections
to established religions. If the new group is still considered mainstream and differs on just
a few points from the mainstream religion, it is usually referred to as a denomination.
Examples of Christian denominations are Baptist and Lutheran; Islamic denominations
would include Sunni and Shi’a. A sect is even more distinct from the older religion than
a denomination is. Although still connected to the mainstream religion, sects are gener-
ally associated with a founder or leader and new revelations. Examples of Christian sects
include the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, discussed earlier. There are real
challenges, however, with the term cult.
The “cult” question
Historically, a cult is a form of religious worship that includes devotion to a particu-
lar person or thing. Thus, the Catholic Church speaks of the cult of Mary. However,
very few people use the term cult with this meaning. Although there are some neutral
definitions—such as considering a cult to be a small, recently created, and spiritually inno-
vative group—most definitions are associated with more negative imagery.
Even those who use the term cult with negative meanings do not agree on what a cult is.
For example, evangelical Christian groups might label as a cult any religious group that
accepts some, but not all, of what evangelicals accept as Christian doctrines. Thus, the
LDS Church, Jehovah’s Witnesses, and the Unification Church (discussed later) would all
be considered cults. However, a group such as the Wiccans does not get attention from this
group because they are not a Christian-derived religion. This highlights the gate-keeping
issue that often occurs when a new group splinters off. Who gets to decide who can call
themselves Christian?
On the other side is the Anti-Cult Movement. Largely composed of mental health pro-
fessionals, this group targets what they consider to be dangerous and authoritarian mind
control (“brainwashing”) and doomsday cults. They are most concerned with what they
see as deceptive recruitment techniques and psychological techniques used to control
members. The media also play a large part in how religious groups are perceived. When
the term cult is employed by the media, it is most often used to refer to a small religious

[Page 377]
350 The search for new meaning
group with a charismatic leader who is brainwashing his followers and is in total control
of them. The group is seen as evil and usually apocalyptic.
Because of this confusion and the often negative connotations of the term, many
researchers avoid the term cult altogether and instead view religion as a continuum from
mainstream religions to denominations and sects and eventually to high demand religions.
These are at the far end of this continuum but otherwise are no different from other reli-
gious groups and should be regarded as such. In the next section, we will focus on high
demand religious groups as legitimate forms of religious expression while acknowledging
the challenges and dilemmas these groups present for the larger society.
Characteristics of high demand religions
All religious groups require their members to have certain kinds of beliefs and to behave
in certain ways, and all groups require some degree of conformity from their members.
However, groups vary in the level of this demand and the degree of control they attempt
to exert over members.
An example of a higher-end demand situation that very few would label a cult is a
Roman Catholic monastery. Monks must follow a strict schedule of sleep, work, and
prayer. Their diet is limited, and they often take oaths of celibacy and even silence. They
must accept without question the decisions of those in authority. These are traits that are
commonly associated with high demand groups.
In high demand groups, the beliefs and behaviors of group members are strictly con-
trolled, commonly through long hours of work with little or no free time, a restricted sleep
schedule, strict control of access to outside information, and the creation of a view of the
outside world as unsafe and threatening. Behavior also may be controlled by public sham-
ing, humiliation, and isolation from outside contacts. Communal living is common, and
members may be given new names and identities to signify their break with their past lives
and their affiliation with, and devotion to, the new group.
Some researchers claim that the endless repetition of prayers and other techniques are, in actu-
ality, auto-hypnotic techniques. For example, a person might be taught that when faced with
criticism of the group, he or she should repeat a certain phrase over and over. Another area of
concern for some observers is the deceptive recruitment techniques that some groups use.
Mind control?
One of the major issues surrounding high demand religious groups is perceived mind control
or brainwashing. A proposition of the Anti-Cult Movement is that the pressures exerted by
these groups move beyond normal social pressure and constitute a unique form of influence
that can be all-controlling. However, many social scientists question this assertion. How is
“brainwashing” different from other forms of social influence and normal socialization? Why
do we not say that people converting to mainstream groups are being brainwashed? Were
we all brainwashed by our parents to accept the beliefs of our cultures and our religions?
An interesting study by Jeffrey Pfeifer looks at how we label these different areas of
social influence differently. In this study, people were presented with a fictional paragraph

describing a student named Bill. They were told that Bill left college to join a Catholic semi-
nary, to join the Marines, or to join the Moonies. Several high-demand techniques were
described:
While at the facility, Bill is not allowed very much contact with his friends or family and
he notices that he is seldom left alone. He also notices that he never seems to be able to

used the term “brainwashing.”
Many studies have failed to support the idea of brainwashing. Several of these have
focused on the issue of recruitment, with the idea that if these groups did have some way
to override free will and control a person’s mind, then everyone, or at least almost every-
one, who attends a recruitment meeting for one of these groups should, in fact, convert.
This turns out not to be the case.
However, there are characteristics that serve as early warning signs of a dangerous group.
One of these is the authority claimed by the leader of the group and what that person does
with this authority. An example is when leaders set up ethical rules that everyone must
follow—except for the leaders themselves. This can also extend to the leadership dictating
important personal details in the lives of followers, such as whom a person can marry.
Another feature of a dangerous group is when the group sees itself as being above the
law or as not having a social contract with the secular state. Although some people place
apocalyptic ideology on the list, this is in and of itself not necessarily a danger sign. How-
ever, when the group believes they will be soldiers in God’s army during this apocalypse
and begins to stockpile weapons for this battle, the issues change.
Examples of high demand religions
Recent decades have seen the development of many new religious movements, including
several that could be labeled as high demand. Many of these movements are little known
in the wider society, but some have come to our attention in dramatic and often tragic
ways. This includes the Branch Davidians and Heaven’s Gate groups in the United States.
New religions are derived from older religious traditions. In the United States, these are
often Christian-based. Many of the new religions base elements of their ideology on the
Book of Revelation, which describes a future apocalyptic world transformation.
Branch Davidians (Students of the Seven Seals)
The Students of the Seven Seals can be traced back to a group that broke off from the Sev-
enth Day Adventists in the 1940s. Led by Victor Houteff, the new sect shared a number of
the same beliefs as the Seventh Day Adventists, such as a belief in the imminent return of

[Page 379]
352 The search for new meaning
Jesus Christ. However, Houteff taught that Christ would return only when at least a small
number of Christians had sufficiently purified themselves and that he himself was God’s
messenger sent to conduct this necessary cleansing. The key to this was esoteric informa-
tion contained in a scroll referenced in the Book of Revelation in the Christian New Tes-
tament. This biblical book describes events that will occur when Christ returns and the
world as we know it ends. The referenced scroll is protected by seven seals, hence the name
of the group. They are also known colloquially as Branch Davidians.
After Houteff’s death, control of the group passed to his wife, who prophesied that the
world would end in April 1959. When this did not occur, some people left the group, but
it survived, and there was a series of new leaders. In 1981, a man named Vernon Howell
joined the group as a handyman, and he married the daughter of a prominent member of
the community. After a power struggle, Howell took control of the group in 1987 and
changed his name to David Koresh, after the biblical King David and the Babylonian King
Cyrus. By the early 1990s, the group had over 100 members.
Under David Koresh, the group came to believe that the death of Christ had provided
salvation only for those who died before Christ did, that is, before 32 CE. People who
had died since that time could be saved only by the actions of the current prophet, Koresh
himself. The Book of Revelation says that the Lamb of God will open the seven seals and
trigger the sequence that ends the world as we know it. Traditionally, Christians have
made the interpretation that the Lamb of God is Jesus Christ. The Branch Davidians
believed that the Lamb of God was David Koresh. After the breaking of the seals, a battle
would occur in which the Branch Davidians believed they would play a major role, hence
the need for weapons. After the battle, they alone would ascend to heaven to be with God.
The group’s practices included many typical in high demand religious groups. They lived
communally and led a highly regulated, disciplined life. Koresh exerted control over sex and
marriage, leading couples to end their marriages so that the women in the group could join
Koresh as his “spiritual wives,” which included sexual access. Everyone else was expected
to remain celibate. Members were not allowed to go to the movies or engage in competitive
activities, and the lengths of women’s dresses and their hairstyles were regulated. Koresh
himself had veto power over all decisions. The practice that brought them to the attention
of the U.S. government, however, was the gathering of a large supply of weapons.
In 1993, in Waco, Texas, the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, and Firearms (ATF) decided
to arrest Koresh for firearms violations. When ATF agents entered the compound, a fire-
fight erupted in which six Branch Davidians and four agents died. A 51-day siege fol-
lowed. Finally, federal agents fired tear gas grenades and used tanks to try to penetrate
the building. Several fires had started in the compound, and Koresh and at least 75 of his
followers, including 21 children, died (Figure 12.5).
Much has been written about the events of Waco, and much remains unclear. Certainly,
the federal officials failed to take the Branch Davidians’ religious beliefs seriously and to
consider this as a factor in their strategy. Koresh apparently believed that the raid was the
start of the war of Armageddon. This case points out the problems associated with the
freedom of religion, especially when the group is armed and awaiting a millenarian battle.
How can a society balance religious freedom against the need for order and security?
The Branch Davidian movement has survived the death of Koresh and continues today.

UFO religions
Some new religious movements have imported elements of modern technology, such as space
travel and cloning, as a basis for a philosophy that, while not always seen as a religion to
outsiders, clearly serves as such. Most scholars consider UFOs to be within the realm of the
paranormal or supernatural, thus fitting in with our definition of religion (Chapter 1). The
UFO groups describe extraterrestrial beings, or “ufonauts,” in the same way that supernatu-
ral beings are described in more traditional religions. These wise, supernatural beings are
often seen as spiritual beings who have come to earth to help humans in some way.
Another common religious theme is the idea of an imminent apocalypse. The world is
seen as being on the verge of destruction. The “ufonauts” will somehow rescue the human
race, usually preventing a nuclear war or selectively removing people from the planet to
preserve the species. “Ufonauts” are often seen as having been involved in the original
creation of humans or the planet.
Many of the new religious movements in the United States are based in part on Christian
beliefs. Even many of the UFO religions exhibit syncretism and contain significant Judeo-
Christian elements.

“Spiritual but not religious”
“Spiritual but not religious” (SBNR) is a rising form of self-
identification in the United States and worldwide. Classified in
general as the religiously unaffiliated, in a 2023 Gallup poll,
some 33 percent of Americans said they are “spiritual but not
religious.”33 Up from an estimated 19 percent in 2012, this is a
growing designation today.34 Some SBNRs would identify or be
labeled as “Nones,” those with religious beliefs but no affiliation
(Chapter 9, an etic view), but in her research, theology professor
Linda Mercadante found that those identifying as “spiritual but

[Page 384]
The search for new meaning 357
not religious” had deep, complex relationships with religion. Coming from diverse social
and religious backgrounds, these individuals seek something sacred in their lives but do
not want affiliation with organized religious institutions.
There are diverse explanations for the emergence of SBNRs—among them, a genera-
tional shift, increasing focus on individual spirituality and mind-body-spirit balance, the
rising politicization of religious institutions in society, the uncovering of corruption and
abuse within mainstream faiths, and rapidly-changing demographics and technological
advances leading individuals into new forms of community. And there is a great deal of
diversity among the SBNRs. Mercadante found five general types of SBNRs:
• “Dissenters” are individuals who intentionally move away from organized religion;
they may or may not have had bad experiences in a church, but they do not see church
as a viable option.
• “Casuals” tend to be associated with spirituality as a form of mental and physical
therapy and may be involved with things such as meditation, tai chi, reiki, yoga, etc.;
seeking a sense of balance in their lives, they see spirituality as a function of a healthy
life.
• “Explorers” are like tourists and are looking for new spiritual adventures; they see no
need to establish spiritual roots and are not looking for any type of commitment, but,
instead, have a kind of “wanderlust” in spiritual matters, easily syncretizing religious
elements they find interesting and useful.
• “Seekers” are individuals in transition—perhaps between churches—but they seek
to eventually re-establish themselves in a religious community; many have a palpable
“spiritual longing” and are not rejecting affiliation as much as asking more of it.
• “Immigrants” tend to be those who are beginning to adapt to a new religious identity—
perhaps a new congregation—and they are still unsure; they may even have been trying
out a new affiliation and are not yet fully committed.35
Is SNBR just a catchall term for those no longer being religious? Does SBNR meet our
criteria for religion, or is it something else? Is the emergence of SNBR proof that religion
is in decline, or is it something more complex? As Mercadante notes in her study,
The “spiritual but not religious” movement may not be simply a protest against religion
but also a protest against a secular world that brackets the spirit or forces an overreli-
ance on science to answer all problems.36
The development and rapid growth of designations such as SNBR, which meet all of the
criteria in the operational definition we are using, instead of being a sign that religion is
in decline, are actually signs of its health and endurance. Individuals continue to search
for new meaning, some of which may lead to religious revitalization in traditional faith
systems.
Across cultures and time, all faith systems either adapt or die. Because religion is one of
the most critically important human experiences, it continues to drive our species to seek
deeper and more satisfying understandings and explanations of what it means to be human.
