# The Anthropology of Religion, Magic, and Witchcraft

## Section: Preface

Preface
Revising and updating the fifth edition of The Anthropology of Religion, Magic, and
Witchcraft has been an important endeavor, given the popularity of undergraduate classes
on the anthropology of religion. Anthropology classes often fulfill general education
requirements or serve as upper-division electives in the humanities and social sciences
at many colleges and universities. Given choices, students often have a deep interest in
classes about religion: Magical Worldview; Myth, Magic, and Religion; Modern Shaman-
ism; American Indian Spirituality; and Religion in the Americas are some of the classes
taught by this text’s authors. As instructors, we find that students have a special interest
in topics such as symbolism, magic, and witchcraft. Because many are not anthropology
majors, it is necessary to frame such a textbook with some basic principles of anthropol-
ogy so that students understand the value of cultural diversity. Chapter 1, therefore, has
been revised to highlight the anthropological perspective and the importance of cultural
relativism and ethics.
From its beginnings, the discipline of anthropology has focused on the importance of
religion. Early scholars, such as Sir Edward Tylor (1832–1917) and Sir James G. Frazer
(1854–1941), did armchair research on religions, collecting and classifying data on other
cultures and belief systems from missionaries, colonials, and government officials. As
anthropology matured, becoming more scientific and beginning to address its colonial
origins—an ongoing, unfinished process—religion continued to be a major interest within
the discipline. Yet, half a century ago, courses in the anthropology of religion were prac-
tically non-existent, and there were few affiliated textbooks. The counterculture move-
ment of the 1960s, the rising popularity of the New Age and Wiccan movements, and a
renewed interest in Indigenous cultures and spirituality undoubtedly influenced the crea-
tion of anthropology classes about religion. Most of the early texts were anthologies com-
posed of previously published articles arranged topically, e.g., myth, ritual, witchcraft,
etc. Subsequent editions omitted and added new articles, but the content was most often
oriented to upper-division anthropology students. Today, instructors teaching anthropol-
ogy of religion classes have more options, as there are a handful of available textbooks.
So, what features of this textbook make it distinct?
The fifth edition of The Anthropology of Religion, Magic, and Witchcraft includes two
new authors who collectively have taught anthropology of religion classes for decades.
Moreover, their fieldwork experiences in the Americas add deeper ethnographic saliency

Chapter 1
The anthropological study of religion
Human beings pose questions about nearly everything in the world, including themselves.
The most fundamental of these questions are answered by a people’s religious beliefs and
practices, which are the subject of this book. We will examine the religious lives of a broad
range of human communities from an anthropological perspective.
The term anthropological perspective means many things. It is a theoretical orienta-
tion that compares human societies throughout the world—contemporary and historical,
industrial and tribal. Many college courses and textbooks focus on the best-known reli-
gions practiced by millions of people and referred to as the “world’s great religions”—
Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, and Buddhism, among others. This book will
expand the subject to include a focus on lesser-known religious systems, especially those
in small-scale, traditional communities. As we do this, we will look for commonalities and
learn to appreciate diversity and the adaptive nature of cultures.
This book will not simply describe a series of religious systems. We approach the study
of religion by looking at particular topics usually included in the anthropological defini-
tion of religion with examples from the anthropological literature and our own and other
anthropologists’ ethnographic fieldwork.
The anthropological perspective
The subject of this book is religion from an anthropological perspective. What does this
mean? The term anthropology refers to the study of humanity. However, anthropology
shares this subject matter with many other disciplines—sociology, psychology, history,
and political science, to name a few. So, how is anthropology different from these other
disciplines?
One way in which anthropology differs from other disciplines is that anthropology is an
integrated study of humanity. Anthropologists study human societies as systematic sums
of their parts, as integrated wholes. We call this approach holism. For example, many dis-
ciplines study marriage, but the anthropologist believes that a full understanding of mar-
riage requires understanding all aspects of the society. Marriage is profoundly influenced
by politics and law, economics, ethics, and theology; in turn, marriage influences history,
literature, art, and music. The same is true of religious practices and beliefs.

through the mechanisms of biological evolution, learned behavioral patterns can change
very rapidly in response to changing conditions. In addition, the human species, which is
quite homogenous biologically, exhibits enormous cultural diversity.
Another important feature of culture is that it is based on the use of symbols. Symbols are
shared understandings about the meaning of certain words, attributes, or objects, such as the
color red symbolizing stop in traffic signals. The connection between the two is arbitrary;

Culture is learned primarily through symbols. Language is a string of symbols, and we
learn, communicate, and even think by using these symbols. Symbols are obviously an

Culture is the blueprint for behavior. As humans, we are bearers of culture because we are
born into social groups and acquire our beliefs and values from birth. We take our cultural
beliefs and values with us wherever we go. When we journey away from our homes and
communities of origin, we begin to notice that not everyone is like us. On a larger scale,
society is the configuration of people in a geographic area; however, not all members of soci-
ety share the same cultural beliefs. More specifically, culture can be identified by four basic
concepts or characteristics. Culture is (1) relative, (2) learned, (3) shared, and (4) integrated.
(1) Culture is relative—In the study of different societies, anthropologists often observe
behaviors that seem strange and sometimes disturbing. We have grown up in a particular
society, and the behaviors and ideas of our own society seem to us to be natural and cor-
rect. It is also natural to use our own society as the basis for interpreting and judging other
societies. This tendency is called ethnocentrism. In its mild form, it causes people to stay
together in groups with individuals who are “like-minded,” leading to cultural diversity;
in an extreme form, it can lead to racism and genocide.

Anthropologists realize, however, that a true understanding of other cultures cannot
develop through ethnocentric interpretations. Thinking of other people as primitive, super-
stitious, and immoral only colors our observations and prevents us from reaching any kind
of true understanding of human behavior and thought. Anthropologists attempt to remain
neutral and to accept the ways of life of other communities as appropriate for those who
live in these communities. Anthropologists also attempt to describe and understand peo-
ple’s customs and ideas from an emic perspective, the study of a society through the eyes of
the people being studied. The goal is to study what people believe, not whether or not what
they believe is true. This anthropological approach of cultural relativism is one in which we
suspend judgment of other people and customs until we have an emic point of view.

Anthropology is a holistic, integrated discipline utilizing a multi-disciplinary approach
to understanding human behavior. Anthropology borrows from other social and behav-
ioral sciences, such as history, sociology, geography, political science, criminal justice,
psychology, economics, and hard sciences, such as geology, genetics, osteology, and biol-
ogy. As mentioned previously, anthropologists study human societies as systematic sums
of their parts, as integrated wholes.
Why can’t everyone be like me? Why are we different? Peoples throughout history have
pondered these questions. Biology teaches that variation within a species is essential to
its evolutionary survival. The same must apply to our species, Homo sapiens, for if all
cultures were the same, we surely would be extinct. Cultural differences reflect the vari-
ations within human groups, as cultures around the world are vastly different from one
another and adapted to different social and physical environments. In anthropology, the
cross-cultural approach allows us to examine beliefs and values in different cultures so we
can better understand the similarities and differences that are part of the human condition.
Understanding other cultures on their own terms, not ours, is crucial for gaining knowl-
edge about other peoples and religions.
Four fields of anthropology
The holistic nature of anthropology emerges from four subdivisions (specialties) within
the discipline. Traditional anthropologists speak of four-field anthropology. These fields
are biological anthropology, archaeology, linguistic anthropology, and cultural anthro-
pology. Today, with the rapid increase and complexity of anthropological studies, anthro-
pologists are becoming more specialized. The often-simplistic concept of anthropology as
being composed of the integrated study of these four fields is rapidly breaking down, but
a review of these four fields will acquaint those studying anthropology for the first time
with the essential nature of the discipline.
Biological anthropology (also called physical anthropology) is the study of human biol-
ogy and evolution. Biological anthropologists are interested in genetics and genomics;
evolutionary theory; the biology and behavior of primates, the group of animals that
includes monkeys, apes, and humans; and paleoanthropology, the study of the hominin
fossil record.2 Anthropologists with a biological orientation often discuss the evolutionary
origins and the neurobiology of religious experience.
Archaeology is the study of material culture (artifacts)—the things humans make
and use—with a focus on reconstructing human behavior. Humans have the capacity
to take raw materials and convert them into life-sustaining products—implements,
tools, inventions, weapons, etc. Like detectives, archaeologists rely on reconstructing
the past and present, focusing on human behavior through the study of these material
remains. Evidence of religious expression exists in the ruins of ancient temples, the art
and writings of people who lived in societies that have faded into history, and in the
modern world, such as in the ceremonial use of sacred space by devotees of the Vodou
religion.
The field of linguistic anthropology is devoted to the study of language, which, accord-
ing to many anthropologists, is a unique feature of humans. Many religious practices
are linguistic in nature, involving the recitation of words, evidenced often in the myths,

The holistic approach
Studying a society holistically is a very daunting task. It requires a great deal of time—time
to observe human behavior and time to interview members of a society. Because of the neces-
sity of having to limit the scope of a research project, cultural anthropologists have often
been involved in long-term studies of small, remote communities. However, as isolated small
communities become increasingly incorporated into larger political units, anthropologists are
turning more and more to the study of larger, more complex societies. For example, within
an urban setting, anthropologists may study specific companies, hospitals, neighborhoods,
gangs, clubs, and churches. Anthropological studies take place over long periods of time and
usually require the anthropologist to live within the community and to participate to a degree
in the lives of the people they study, while at the same time making objective observations.
Most commonly, anthropology students are introduced to small communities such as
foraging bands, small horticultural villages, and groups of pastoral nomads. They become
familiar with the lives of the Trobriand Islanders off the coast of New Guinea, for exam-
ple, the Navajos of the American Southwest, the Yanomamös of northern South America,
the Murngins of northern Australia, and the Sans of southern Africa. Some people refer
to these societies as being “primitive,” but primitive is a pejorative term, one laden with
negative connotations such as inferior and “less than.” A better term is small-scale. When
we say small-scale, we refer to relatively small communities, villages, and bands that
practice foraging, herding, or technologically simple horticulture.
We will also examine aspects of what are often referred to as the “world’s great religions.”
Like the term primitive, the term great involves a value judgment and is not used frequently
in anthropology. These familiar religions include Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and Buddhism
(Figure 1.5). They are similar in that the origins of these religions are based on the lives of
a particular individual or founder, such as Moses, Christ, Mohammad, and the Buddha.
These religions have spread into thousands of different societies, and their adherents number
in the millions. The small-scale societies and religions that are more traditionally studied
by anthropologists, by contrast, are usually not based on the lives of particular prophets or
founders, and their adherents might number only a few hundred or a few thousand.
If they involve only a very small number of people, then why study these small-scale
religions? Among the many questions anthropologists ask about humanity are the fol-

The study of human societies
Ethnography, usually associated with cultural anthropology, is the descriptive study of con-
temporary human societies. Cultural anthropologists who study human societies and write
ethnographies are also called ethnographers. However, not all descriptions of human socie-
ties are written by ethnographers. For example, an archaeologist who studies the physical
and cultural remains of societies that existed in the past and are known today only from their
ruins, burials, and material remains can, to a limited degree, reconstruct the lives of people
who lived in ancient societies. Sometimes, the only descriptions we have of people’s lives are
those written in diaries and reports by explorers and colonial administrators. Although these
descriptions are far from complete and objective, they provide information.
In our text, we will visit some societies known solely from archaeological remains, but most
of the examples in this book are from societies that exist today or have existed in the recent
past. Many were first visited and described by anthropologists in the early to mid-1900s.
Although these societies have changed over time, as all groups do, and although some have
passed out of existence, anthropologists speak of them in the ethnographic present; that is,

The Fores of New Guinea: an ethnographic example
In the preceding sections of this chapter, we learned some basic concepts of anthropology.
Now, let us turn our attention to a particular example to illustrate these ideas.
The holistic approach sees human behavior as a complex set of interacting behaviors
and ideas. In examining a society, we might begin with a particular problem that interests
us, but we soon realize that to understand this problem fully, we have to look at many
other aspects of the society.
An example of this is the study of the Fores, a group of about 20,000 horticulturalists
living in the eastern highlands of Papua New Guinea. The problem that brought the Fores
to the attention of the Western world was a medical one. The solution to the problem
brought the Nobel Prize in Medicine to one of the investigators.
When the Australian government first contacted the Fores in the 1950s, a significant
number of individuals were suffering from a strange illness, and about 200 were dying
of this annually, primarily women and children. This was having a major impact on the
population.
The illness is characterized by a variety of symptoms, but the most obvious ones are jerk-
ing movements and shaking, which make voluntary motor activity difficult. The course
of the illness lasts about nine months. In the end, the victim can no longer stand or sit up
and can no longer eat or drink water and soon dies. The Fores call this illness kuru, which
means “to tremble with fear” in the Fore language.
The medical team sent to help with the disease sought the cause. Because it appeared
to be largely confined to the Fores, the team thought it might be genetic or due to a
toxin in the environment. However, kuru was ultimately determined to be the result of an
infectious agent called a prion. But how was the kuru prion passed from one person to
another? Was it passed on through contaminated water, air, or sexual activity? The answer
anthropologists found was in a traditional religious ritual: cannibalism.
It was the Fores custom to eat some of the deceased’s body as part of the funeral ritu-
als. The body of the deceased was carried to an abandoned field, where kin dismembered
and cooked it. Close relatives then consumed some of the flesh. Because cooking does not
destroy the prions, they entered the bloodstream through cuts and open sores and even-
tually settled in the brain, where, many years later, the person who consumed this flesh
began to show symptoms of the disease. Because women and children traditionally ate the
deceased’s brain, where prions are primarily found, they were the most likely to develop
the disease.
The modern medical community now had an explanation for what caused the disease
and knew how it was transmitted from one individual to another. The government had
a “cure” to the epidemic: eliminate the practice of cannibalism. As a result, cannibalism
stopped, and kuru eventually disappeared, although this took some time because of the
long incubation period of the disease. However, the Fores themselves did not understand
this explanation and stopped eating the bodies of their dead only because not doing so
would mean spending time in jail. The Fores did not accept the scientific explanation of
the disease. Think about how difficult it would be for the doctors to convince the Fores
that kuru was caused by tiny prions that no one could see.

Fores had an emic explanation for kuru. It was the result of sorcery. Sorcery is a form
of magic, which we will discuss in Chapter 10. The sorcerer, the person who practices
sorcery, would steal something that was once a part of or in contact with the victim, such
as a piece of clothing or a lock of hair. The material was made into a bundle with some
leaves, bark, and stones bound up into a package. After reciting a spell, the sorcerer would
place the bundle into muddy ground, and as the bundle rotted, the victim would develop
the symptoms of the disease. This belief influenced everyday behavior, as individuals were
careful to hide things that could be retrieved and used by a sorcerer.
In spite of this caution, people still developed kuru. In this case, a divination ritual
was used to reveal the identity of the sorcerer causing the illness. As we will see in
Chapter 7, many people use such techniques to reveal things that are difficult or impos-
sible to discover by other means. Once the sorcerer was identified, the Fores had many
options to counter the activity of the evildoer. A person with kuru might also have
consulted a healer.
The fact that kuru primarily struck women had significant social consequences. Many
men lost wives through kuru, and the shortage of women meant that many men were una-
ble to find wives. In addition, men with children who had lost their wives had to perform
many domestic chores normally reserved for women, including farming.
The ethnography of the Fores and the description of kuru illustrate the concept of holism.
From the Western point of view, we begin with a medical problem: a disease. Then, we see
how this fatal disease affects various aspects of the society because of the death of women
of childbearing age. This includes marriage, the family, the raising of children, farming,
and so forth. Also, we see how the society attempts to explain and deal with the disease
through religion. A description of kuru among the Fores as only a medical problem fails
to provide us with a complete understanding of that disease.
Two ways of viewing culture
What causes kuru among the Fores? From our viewpoint, a complete answer to that ques-
tion includes both biological factors (the disease-causing organism) and cultural factors
(the practice of cannibalism). However, the Fores themselves would give another answer
to this question: Kuru is caused by sorcery. A holistic approach must consider both emic
and etic perspectives.
An anthropologist—or any scholar, for that matter—cannot be completely neutral and
objective when describing a culture. Observation, recording, and analysis involve process-
ing data in one’s mind. One’s own cultural background, education, training, and other
factors will act as a filter or lens that colors what are thought of as objective observations.
Using a biomedical model, physicians searched for the cause of kuru through techniques
learned in medical school based on the scientific method. Although the physicians were
able to discover the biological cause of kuru, the disease-causing prion protein, they were
unable to discover the mode of transmission. Medical science identified a series of trans-
mission pathways, and none of them offered a valid explanation. Anthropologists viewed
the situation from a holistic, anthropological viewpoint to make the connection between
kuru and cannibalism.

The physician and the anthropologist are outsiders looking in. They see Fore culture in
terms of Western philosophy and theory. There are advantages to an etic perspective. Just
as a friend or therapist might see patterns in a person’s life that the person might overlook,
an anthropologist might see patterns of behaviors or beliefs in a culture that the members
of that group may not recognize. Another advantage is that anthropologists can apply a
consistent form of analysis to many different societies being studied, permitting them to
make comparisons between societies and discover some universal principles about human
behavior.
Yet the Fores see their world from an altogether different perspective, using linguistic
categories and basic assumptions that differ profoundly from ours. To the Fores, sor-
cery is the ultimate cause of kuru, which makes sense in their culture. Theirs is an emic
perspective.
Understanding the differences between these two perspectives is a key component of
ethnographic research. People in other cultures use linguistic/cognitive categories and
basic assumptions about their world that differ profoundly from ours; key informants and
collaborators express worldviews in terms that are meaningful to them. Anthropologists
documenting the emic perspective study a society through the eyes of the people being
studied. Significantly, members of a society are not necessarily like-minded, so there is
emic variation within a group, as we saw in the previous Kiowa example. The ethnogra-
pher, therefore, should work with more than one or two people, seeking the “multiplicity
of voices” that describe a cultural system.
Emic and etic data should not be seen as mutually exclusive or right or wrong. There
should be a balance of emic/etic viewpoints in ethnographic reporting. Importantly, each
voice speaks for itself; it is important to know who said what and when (Box 1.2).

Viewing the world
The idea of culture involves much more than simply describing human activity. People
also have different belief systems, perceptions, and understandings of their world and their
lives. Culture gives meaning to reality. We live in a real, physical world translated through
the human mind. We look out of a window and see a mountain rising above us—to the
geologist, the mountain is a structure made of rock formed through natural processes; to
the hydrologist concerned with bringing water to a desert town, the mountain is the place
where there are snowfields; to the biologist, it is the home of many plants and animals,
some of them perhaps endangered.
To many people, however, a mountain is much more. The mountain might be the home
of their gods (Figure 1.7) or the place where the souls of the dead congregate after death.
Mountains figure prominently in many Biblical stories. For example, Mount Sinai was
where Moses received the Ten Commandments, and Mount Ararat was where Noah’s ark
came to rest. Other sacred mountains include Mount Olympus, where the gods of ancient
Greece lived, and the four sacred mountains of the Navajo world. We may label this as
part of the imagination of a people, yet to the people who hold these beliefs, the sacredness
of a mountaintop may be as real as the presence of rocks, snow, or plants.

The Marxist approach
Another influential theorist of the 1800s was Karl Marx (1818–1883) (Figure 1.8). Like
many of this era, Marx was critical of religion. However, Marx did not criticize the logic
of religion as others had done. He felt that religion reflected society, so any criticism of
religion must, therefore, also be a criticism of society. Indeed, the Marxist approach sees
religion as a human construction, more specifically, a construction by those in power.
Marx felt that religion did not reflect the true consciousness of people but a false con-
sciousness designed to divert people’s attention from the miseries of their daily lives. This
misery was seen as the result of exploitation of the masses by those in power under the
capitalist system. Of course, religion existed before capitalism. Marx’s basic view is that
religion is a natural consequence of the human experience of distress. Critics of Marx
point out that there usually is no single, dominant ideology in a society; instead, there are
different ideologies that correspond to different subcultures and different classes.
The functional approach
In contrast to the evolutionary and Marxist schools, the functional approach asks: What
does religion do? What role do religions play in a society? For example, a religion might
enforce social cohesion by bringing members together for rituals to provide a foundation
for shared beliefs and might also function on the individual level to relieve individual fears
and anxiety by providing explanations and meaning.
French sociologist Émile Durkheim (1858–1917) saw society as problematic.10 Although
sanctions exist to keep people in line, Durkheim thought that these were not enough and
that the key lies in the collective conscious, a system of beliefs that act to contain the natu-
ral selfishness of individuals and promote social cooperation. Collective representations, or
symbols, reflect the collective conscious, and during rituals, these collective representations
are displayed and reinforced, resulting in a reattachment to the value system of the group.
Both Durkheim and British anthropologist Alfred R. Radcliffe-Brown (1881–1951) saw soci-
ety as an organism in which the parts act to maintain the whole.11 Specifically, Radcliffe-Brown
focused on the study of social institutions as parts of society, noting that those institutions of
greatest social value were believed to possess supernatural power. He believed that rituals func-
tion to express the basic sentiments of a society and these ideas are passed down from generation
to generation. Radcliffe-Brown saw religion, in general, as an integrative force in society.
Another important theorist in the functional school, Polish anthropologist Bronislaw
Malinowski (1884–1942), had a different approach. Malinowski looked at religion and
other features of a society in terms of their purpose in meeting basic human needs. For exam-
ple, in his analysis of magic, Malinowski stressed that magic is a logical system that people
turn to in times of uncertainty or emotional stress. Magic functions to provide control and
certainty in an otherwise uncertain world and meets a basic human need (Figure 1.9).

The interpretive approach
Clifford Geertz (1926–2006) was an American anthropologist who popularized the meta-
phor that culture is a meaningful text to be read.12 Part of his inspiration for this interpretive
approach was the work of the sociologist Max Weber (1864–1920) and his concept of ver-
stehen (i.e., understanding the other’s point of view).13 This Weberian approach was opposed
to the more popular functionalist approach of the time. Geertz emphasized that the task of
anthropologists was not to discover laws or study origins and causes but instead to make sense
of cultural systems by studying meaning. Anthropologists need to seek to interpret the cultur-
ally specific “webs of significance” that people both create and are caught up in. Interpretive
anthropology can reveal these webs of meaning through detailed ethnographic descriptions.
Religion, specifically, comprises a cluster of symbols that together communicate a cul-
ture’s ideas, values, and way of life. The set of symbols provides ways to interpret the
world. Geertz described symbols as playing a double role. They are both “models of” and
“models for” in that they both represent the way things are while also directing human

activity. Geertz argued that religious symbols establish very powerful moods and feelings
and help explain human existence by giving it an ultimate meaning. These symbols claim
to connect humans to a reality that, in some ways, is “more real” than everyday life, thus
giving religion a special status above and beyond daily life.
Geertz felt that the study of religion needed to take place in two stages. The first stage is
an analysis of the systems of meaning embodied within the religious symbols. The second
stage involves relating these systems to social structures and psychological processes. Critics
point out that, in reality, he devoted much more time to the first stage than to the second.
The psychosocial approach
The psychosocial approach to the study of religion is concerned with the relationship
between culture and personality and the connection between society and the individual.
One example is the work of Sigmund Freud (1856–1939).14 Freud’s model of the mind and
his concept of defense mechanisms have been used both by Freud himself and by his follow-
ers to explain religious phenomena. For example, defense mechanisms are psychological
maneuvers by which we distort reality in ways that help us avoid conflict and reduce
anxiety. The most important of these for our discussion is projection, in which the subject
is transposed, and the emotion is projected. So, “I don’t like X” becomes “You don’t like
X.” Psychosocial anthropologists believe that individual emotions also get projected at the
cultural level. However, correlation does not equal causation, and this and several other
issues challenge the correlational approach.
The bio-cognitive approach to religious behavior
What we perceive and think of as our reality is actually a creation of our brain. Studying
religion from this perspective is a bio-cognitive approach. Our awareness of what is “out
there” is based on input from a series of receptors such as our eyes, nose, and tongue. The
stimuli picked up by these receptors enter the brain, where they undergo processing before
they enter our consciousness. Not all of the information that enters our brains from the
outside enters our consciousness.
Brain scans comparing brains at rest with brains of individuals in deep meditation show
distinct regions that are active during particular mental activities. In the meditating brain, we
see increased activity in the frontal lobe, indicative of increased concentration, and decreased
activity in the parietal lobe. This latter area is in the region associated with people’s orientation
of their bodies in time and space. Changes in activity in this area may be related to the develop-
ment of out-of-body experiences or a sense of blurring of the boundaries between self and other.
Some theorists have focused on the nature of human cognition as an explanation for
the origin of religious beliefs and experiences, a general term for processes of the human
brain that include perception, learning, memory, concept formation, and problem-solving.
Religion is seen not as existing to serve a purpose but rather as an accidental by-product
of the way the human brain works.
Despite what psychologists know about how the brain works, for example, we intui-
tively feel that we merely occupy our bodies, not that we actually are our bodies. This

Another way cognition feeds into the evolution of religion is the human tendency to
overextend our system of social understanding and infer purpose, goals, intention, and

experience paranormal phenomena—what looks like a face in a stucco ceiling or a spec-

extensions of everyday cognitive categories and the way that the human brain processes
information. As he writes, “People do not invent gods and spirits; they receive information
that leads them to build such concepts.”15
Postmodernism
Beginning with the Renaissance, scholars based their knowledge on the ideals of rational-
ity, objectivity, and reason. Science was seen as the means for the discovery of knowledge,
truth, and progress. This way of approaching an understanding of the world is termed
modernity. At the time, scholars believed that it was possible to gain a true understanding
of all peoples and all societies using standard formal principles of modernity.
In the 1980s, however, the postmodern movement, originating earlier in art and archi-
tecture, began to affect anthropology and other social sciences. In stark contrast to the
ideas of modernity, postmodernism denies the existence of a single formal truth in any
society. All knowledge is seen as a human construct that we must try to “deconstruct” in
order to understand it. The postmodern movement emphasizes the limitations of science,
values reflexivity (the awareness that the anthropologist’s presence affects the nature of
the data collected in the field), and argues that there are multiple viewpoints and truths
(called multivocality). In contrast to modernity’s emphasis on order, postmodernism sees
contradictions and instabilities as being inherent in any social group or practice.
The value of postmodernism for anthropology has been to reinforce the idea of multiple
ways of seeing the world—that there is no one right way to think or to do things. This is
an extension of the concept of cultural relativism. Postmodernism serves as a reminder of
how the ethnographer can influence the fieldwork situation. As a result, ethnographers are
more self-conscious and more aware of their own positions and biases. Every person sees
the world through the lens of their own culture. We cannot remove the lens, but we can
become more aware of it.
However, taken to an extreme, postmodernism argues that it is impossible for a person
from one culture to ever understand fully someone from another culture. As an extreme
theoretical position, it asks if anthropology as a discipline is even possible. As anthropo-
logical theories now move into a post-postmodern period, most anthropologists have taken
a middle-ground approach, appreciating the lessons of postmodernism while attempting
to avoid this extreme point of view.

Attempts at defining religion
The beginning point of any study is to define the object of study—in this instance, religion.
Yet the task of defining this term is a challenging one indeed. We must avoid using a defini-
tion that is too narrow or too vague. Many definitions proposed apply only to some cul-
tures and only to some of the phenomena that anthropologists traditionally place within
the domain of religion. Such definitions are often ethnocentric, including only those ideas
considered “religious” in one particular culture. In such definitions, topics such as magic
and witchcraft may be excluded. On the other hand, a definition that is too inclusive and
vague loses much of its meaning and usefulness.
In spite of the difficulties of defining religion, anthropology is a social science, and
the methodology of science requires that we define our terms. We need to use an opera-
tional definition, one in which we define our terms so that they are observable and
measurable and, therefore, can be studied. So, what would a good operational defini-
tion of religion be? We can start by looking at the various ways scholars have attempted
to define the term.
Many definitions of religion share elements that we included in our definition of culture:
Analytic definitions focus on the way religion manifests itself or is expressed in a cul-
ture. An example would be defining religions by stating that religious practices generally
include rituals. British religious studies scholar Ninian Smart, for example, argued that
there were the six dimensions of religion.16 These include an institutional dimension
(organization and leadership), a narrative dimension (myths, creation stories, world-
view), a ritual dimension (rites of passage and other important ritual activities), a social
dimension (religion being a group activity that binds people together), an ethical dimension
(customs, moral rules), and an experiential dimension (religion involving experiences of a
sacred reality that is beyond ordinary experience).
Analytic definitions are valuable for identifying components within a religious system,
but they can also keep us from noticing unique aspects of a religion, certain rituals, or
“dimensions” that may not be included in the original list of parts. It is important as
ethnographers that we do not use only pre-determined definitions or checklists when we
study other cultures.
Functional definitions focus on what religion does, either socially or psychologically. For
example, rituals would be seen as a means to bring a group together and bring individuals
comfort. One of the problems with functional definitions is that they could apply equally
well to beliefs and behaviors that are not religious in nature. Some scholars feel that func-
tional approaches are reductionist, reducing religion to a few feelings and behaviors that
are not, in and of themselves, religious. For these reasons, it can be difficult to separate
religious and nonreligious systems using a functional definition. This does not mean that
the social and psychological functions are not important. They are, and functionalism as
a theoretical approach to studying religion has much to offer. As a definition, however, it
alone is not sufficient.
An essentialist definition of religion looks at what the essential nature of religion is. It
emphasizes that religion is the domain of the extra-ordinary—things beyond the com-
monplace and the natural. On the basis of this idea, we would say that religion is a system

of beliefs and behaviors that deals with the relationship between humans and the sacred
supernatural.
The term supernatural refers to things “above and outside of the natural.” Supernatu-
ral entities and actions transcend the natural world of cause-and-effect as we empirically
know it. In the supernatural world, wondrous things can occur, and supernatural beings
often defy the basic laws of nature. In the supernatural world, objects can move faster than
light, heavy objects fly, and creatures become invisible.
However, not all supernatural phenomena are thought to be religious. Consider the
folktale in which the handsome prince is turned into a frog. This is surely a supernatu-
ral occurrence—handsome princes do not turn into frogs in the natural world—but this
occurrence is hardly religious. To address this problem, we add the term sacred to the defi-
nition of religion. Sacred denotes an attitude wherein the subject is entitled to reverence
and respect by a specific group or community.
The problem with an essentialist definition is that such definitions often become too spe-
cific, focusing narrowly on spirit beings, for example, or they risk being too vague if they
only reference the supernatural. As with other definitions we have looked at, essentialist
definitions by themselves may not be enough, but they do point to areas of great impor-
tance in religion.
The practices that are included under the rubric of religion vary from scholar to scholar,
and definitions that focus upon religious systems found in large, urban societies differ
considerably from those found in small-scale societies. Each previously explored definition
offers clues to important elements of religion, but each by itself is incomplete.
A true understanding of the breadth of religious practices among the world’s societies
will become clearer as you progress through this text. We encourage you to keep an open
mind and settle on a definition as you gain more knowledge and understanding. However,
as an endeavor in the social sciences, this text will use an operational definition in order
to proceed.
Perhaps it is best to think of religion as a set of cultural beliefs and practices that usually
include some or all of a set of basic characteristics. While not an exhaustive list, it will
provide us with an operational definition as we move ahead with our studies of religious
systems. These characteristics are as follows:
• a belief in anthropomorphic supernatural beings, such as spirits and gods
• a focus on the sacred supernatural, where sacred refers to a feeling of reverence and awe
• the presence of supernatural power or energy that may be found in supernatural beings
as well as physical beings and objects
• the performance of ritual activities that involve the manipulation of sacred objects to
communicate with supernatural beings and/or to influence or control events
• an articulation of a worldview and moral code through narratives and other means
• a provision for the creation and maintenance of social bonds and mechanisms of social
control within a community
• explanations for the unknown and a sense of control for the individual

The domain of religion
The discussion of definitions highlights cultural differences. The very concept of religion
as a separate cultural category is a Western one. Western cultures are divided into distinct
cultural domains, such as economics, politics, technology, and, of course, religion. As
we move through our day, our domains do not always overlap, or they overlap only to a
small degree. For example, when we go to work, we might punch a clock or sign in, for
“work” is a distinct segment of our life, which we can define in terms of location, activ-
ity, relationships with co-workers, and so forth. Religion as a domain may be restricted
to very specific activities held in special places during specific times—a Sunday morning
church service, for example. When we use the term religion, we might immediately picture
such things as special buildings dedicated to religious activities (churches, temples, and
mosques) and full-time specialists who perform religious rituals (priests and rabbis).
Our analysis of religion becomes more difficult when we turn our attention to more
traditional societies. If we analyze small-scale religious systems by applying the definitions
and concepts that have been developed in Western cultures, we find that certain elements
that we consider to be vital parts of our religious systems may simply not exist in other
societies.
For instance, many Western studies of Native American belief systems confuse the con-
cepts “religious” and “spiritual.” According to religion scholar Lee Irwin, religion is a
“post enlightenment concept often rooted in polarity between ideas of the ‘sacred and
profane.’” He continues,
My perception of the interactive spheres of Native communal life is that they have a
relatedness through personal relationships that finds common expression in mutual,
everyday concerns. Ceremonial activity, prayer, or simply carrying out daily activities
like driving a friend to work or struggling for political rights may engage individuals in
aspects of “religious” concern. It is that connectedness to core values and deep beliefs
that I mean by “spirituality,” a pervasive quality of life that develops out of an authentic
participation in values and real-life practices meant to connect members of a community
with the deepest foundations of personal affirmation and identity. In this sense, spiritu-
ality is inseparable from any sphere of activity as long as it really connects with deeply
held affirmative values and sources of authentic commitment, empowerment, and genu-
ineness of shared concerns.17
American Indian studies scholars Suzanne Crawford and Dennis Kelley remind us that
Native cultures do not always perceive religion as a “unique and separate category of
experience” and that “traditional spirituality encompassed nearly all areas of life and
operated in an integrated mode with the rest of life experience.”18 Joe Sando, a member
of Jemez Pueblo, concurs, pointing out that there is no word for “religion” in Pueblo
languages. Instead, “knowledge of a spiritual life is part of the person twenty-four hours
a day, every day of the year.”19 Thus, to Native Americans (and many other Indigenous
peoples), spirituality is about relationships and the kinship bonds between humans and

Spirituality exists on a daily basis and is woven into daily activities, such as prayer or shar-

When we study traditional societies, there might be no equivalent term to our concept

As Islamic scholar Wilfred Cantwell Smith wrote, “To the believer, they are parts of the

The dependent and independent variables of religion
Although it may be difficult to generate an accepted definition of religion, anthropologist
Felicitas Goodman21 suggests that all religions, to some degree, share six characteristics or
variables:
1. Ritual—As creatures of habit, human behavior is ritualized. Religious rituals have spe-
cific purposes and “recreate the drama of human life” from birth to procreation and
death.
2. Altered states of consciousness—These are religious experiences that “can take place
only if there are radical changes in the way the body functions, initiating an alteration
in consciousness, in the perceptual state.” Two altered states have been institutional-
ized: lucid dreams and the religious trance. Both states lead to an alternate reality.
3. Alternate reality—This is “another part or dimension of reality as a whole” that is cul-
turally conditioned and internally consistent. Those who have experienced an alternate
reality can recall the details of the vision for the rest of their lives.
4. Good fortune, misfortune, and the rituals of divination—Many people believe that
non-human agents are responsible for the good and bad things that happen in their
lives. Divination rituals attempt to discern and predict outcomes.
5. Ethics—In order for people to live in harmony with others and to maintain a balance
between quotidian and alternate realities, they must obey principles of conduct incor-
porated into a system of ethics.
6. Semantics—Every society has words or named categories for “religion.” However, such
terms are often unknown, mistranslated, or misinterpreted by outsiders.

munities are found throughout the world, including some very remote areas. To better
understand their nature and distribution, we organize these societies into culture areas,
a geographical area within which societies tend to share many cultural traits.22 These
groups face similar challenges from the environment, often come up with similar solu-

(called diffusion).

Foraging—Foraging is the oldest known subsistence pattern (Figure 1.10). Although
anthropologists believe the division of labor by gender in human societies originated in
foraging societies, where men and boys tended to hunt wild animals while women and
girls gathered food and cared for the young, old, and infirm, these gender roles were not
strict and were based, instead, on individual availability and opportunity. When gathering
vegetals, or wild plant foods, both women and men noted animal tracks and pursued meat
as it was available. While men tended to utilize tools such as spears, bows-and-arrows,
or boomerangs (among Aboriginals) and usually hunted in groups, women used dibbles
(or digging sticks) to gather not only vegetals but also any small game that was available.
Plants provided a majority of the total calories consumed by most foraging groups, and
plants with the highest protein content were preferred, but meat was an important food
source. Although foragers have always faced problems of illness and food shortage, stud-
ies of extant foraging societies have shown that they live in harmony in their environments
and take only what they need.
Myths of foraging peoples speak of a time when they were immortal, but the environ-
ment became crowded; death came about to maintain harmony and balance. Death brings
sadness to foraging peoples, not only because of the loss of a member of a culture but
because the habitat has suffered as well and must be consoled.

Ant’s son died, and he begged Saynday to bring his son back to life. However, Saynday

Religious practices in foraging societies focus on:
1. Ensuring future supply of game. Because foragers/hunters do not control animals
through breeding, they must rely on supernatural intervention to ensure a future food
supply. For instance, Inuit hunters throughout the Arctic throw the unused bones of
seals and fish back into the ocean so that they can reanimate, Australian Aborigine
hunters conduct rites to increase animal fertility, and nineteenth-century Plains Indian
Sun Dances were performed to renew the people and the buffalo.
2. Ensuring success in the hunt. Many hunting peoples believe in a “spirit owner” of game
animals who must be appeased after the animal is killed. The spirit owner, or guardian of
the species, endowed with mystical power, is perceived as a large and idealized member
of that species. Offerings are given to the spirit owner to express gratitude for providing
meat for the people. For instance, facing a dying animal to the east and pouring water
into its mouth is a “bloodless” sacrifice. American Indian hunters might offer tobacco to
the spirit of the slain animal. Hunters also follow certain taboos in order not to offend
the spirit owner; angering it could cause the animals to go away. Many Baffin Island

whose arrow killed the animal; the owner of the arrow, not necessarily the one who
shot the animal, distributes the meat. The killer, therefore, is not the owner of the meat:
one cannot own what was given as a sacrifice by outside forces.
4. Ensuring gain in mystical power. Many cultures believe guardian spirits exist in animal
form and humans can obtain these powers. For instance, the Pentecost Islanders of Mel-
anesia believe that a man who obtains power from pigs and snakes will be successful in
society. In Native North America, animals appear as spirit helpers to bestow recipients
with power; among many groups, the bear is noted for its power to heal the sick.24
Horticulture—Horticulture, or simple farming of small plots with hand tools—hoes or dib-
bles (digging/planting sticks)—produces more calories per unit of labor than foraging. After
plants were domesticated in different parts of the world—Asia, Africa, and the Americas,
beginning ca. 10,000 years ago—various farming technologies appeared, including horticul-
ture (Figure 1.11). Although simple farming augmented food brought in through foraging
and usually created a more controlled food supply, it is also associated with an increase in
famine and food shortage, something that increases dramatically in agricultural societies.25
Unlike wild plants, domesticates are almost totally dependent on human intervention. Most
Native Americans employed horticultural farming technologies using hoes or dibbles made
of sharpened sticks and antlers.

Religious practices that horticultural societies focus on
1. Distance from the habitat. Burial customs in horticultural societies suggest that people
no longer needed to console the habitat after the advent of farming systems. “Instead,

Religious practices that pastoralist societies focus on
1. Animals as mediators. Herders do not alter the habitat but interpose their animals to
exploit it. Animals “represent the bridge between humans and the all-encompassing
habitat, both in subsistence and also psychologically.” Because the environment is
sacred, they are mediators who serve as “the bridge or messenger between the habitat
and its alienated children.”30
2. Sacrifice of domestic animals. These are “bloody” sacrifices, in which the meat is eaten,
although the animal is not sacrificed just to feed the people. Usually, the ritual meal is
a social occasion in which deceased ancestors are invoked to share the food. The main
purpose of the sacrifice is to please the supernatural powers. Ethnologist E.E. Evans-
Pritchard noted that among the Nuers of South Sudan, “It is the role and destiny of the
cattle to be slaughtered in sacrifice.”31
3. Equitable distribution of meat. Animal owners are pressured by religious considera-
tions to sacrifice some of their animals periodically. The meat is distributed throughout
the community, and there are rigid rules, with supernatural sanctions, about how to
divide the meat.32
4. Emphasis on male generative power. Along with animal domestication came an empha-
sis on male generative power, as noted by phallic art.33
Agriculture—Agriculture involves the intensification of farming methods, or farming
large tracts of land using draft animals, plows, terracing, irrigation, and crop rota-
tion to produce more calories per unit of land. Because agricultural economies create
a surplus, storage is crucial. Agricultural societies feature complex social organization,
stratification, and a well-defined system of labor. In the past, state societies could not
exist without a solid agricultural base. In the Americas, early state societies appeared in
Mesoamerica (Mexico and Upper Central America) and along the Pacific coast of South
America. The Aztecs, Mayas, and Incas utilized agricultural technologies without using
draft animals (Figure 1.13).

Through their rituals, agriculturalists express the need to control their environment, pri-
marily because they are tied to the land as a productive base and cannot easily move. Unlike
horticulturalists, agricultural peoples have no “remorse” for tilling the earth. Instead of
guilt, they express concerns about “bad” plants (weeds) and animals that invade their
fields; they are pests to be exterminated. According to Goodman,34
These attitudes are part of a hidden, more abstract complex. In the agricultural adapta-
tion, humans have drawn a magic circle around themselves, their homes and fields. They
are confined within their fences, but also protected against what lies beyond. Any intru-
sion is experienced as threatening in the extreme. The intruder brings with it danger,
sickness, pollution, the curse.
Replacing “ravished earth” prophecies of horticulturalists are prophecies predicting future
cataclysms that will replenish the earth with more desirable people. Stories of past cata-
clysms, like the Great Flood, abound across the cultures of early state societies.
Religious practices that agricultural societies focus on
1. Planting and harvest rituals. These are technological rituals that deal with the capricious
nature of the environment. Farmers contend with droughts, floods, weeds, insects, plant
diseases, and other calamities that necessitate protective rites. Planting rituals ensure
the success of the crops as they grow. Maya fields are rectangular like the quincunx, the
horizontal plane of the earth’s surface. Before planting, the farmer builds an altar and

Urbanization—Permanent agricultural villages developed into larger urban centers due to
nearby food resources that were both predictable and abundant. Most ancient cities were
situated on rivers linking to trade routes for the transportation of food and other prod-
ucts. City dwellers do not produce food themselves but rely on subordinate producers,
including farmers. Goodman contends that,36
Urbanites are divorced from the habitat. The earth, the sky, the rain, the plants, and
the animals are not their partners in the struggle for subsistence. This alienation became
even more pronounced with the advent of industrialization, eventually causing the
destruction of traditional peasantries and the takeover by agribusiness. The latter has
no direct tie with the habitat at all. Rather, it exploits it by the interposition of machin-
ery. The earth, the land, is nothing but the site, the location of manufacture, commerce,
and services.
To urbanites, the environment is an outside force that is “distant, incomprehensible, and
blind.” Urbanites express no guilt feelings towards the habitat yet maintain “a morbid
curiosity about cataclysmic prophecies” where exceptional individuals will be saved by
extra-terrestrial beings.
Religious practices that urban societies focus on
1. Introduction of new religions. City dwellers bring their belief systems and religious
practices to the new environment of the city and pass them along to their children.
2. Missionizing from traditional faiths. New religions are brought into cities by mis-
sionaries who proselytize their faith, though intergenerational conflict results
when the youth find that the beliefs and practices are not relevant to their environ-
ment. Oftentimes, people join congregations for social gain, e.g., to fit in with a
community.
3. Rituals become secularized. Due to the multiplicity of religions present in urban areas,
secular rituals, especially sporting events, become characteristic of cities. Sporting events
involve displays of physical and mental skills of competition, resulting in winners and
losers.37
