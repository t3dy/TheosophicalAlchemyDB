# The Anthropology of Religion, Magic, and Witchcraft

## Section: Conclusion

Conclusion
Religions exist to answer questions, show us a culturally defined correct path, and make
us feel safe and secure in the world we live in. Religions are specific to the cultures and
circumstances in which they are found. But what happens when those conditions change,
and the religion no longer meets these needs? When a new religion, more suited to the
new situation, is needed, a revitalization movement is born. Revitalization movements are
likely to be popular in periods of rapid social change when the current ways of doing and
thinking about things are no longer satisfying. They spell out a clear path, a new path (or
the return to a former one) that they say will lead people out of despair and into a better
future, which will answer questions and provide meaning to life. Deprivation models best
describe historical or classic revitalization movements, though other possible relations
between Indigenous groups and colonial societies improve when the former is in recovery
from oppression. This, in turn, can trigger modern day cultural revivals, including gaming,
the invention of tradition, language revival, healing, ethnomedicine, massage therapy, and
Indigenized Christianity.
Summary
Culture change through forced acculturation is inevitable, as witnessed by the last 500-
plus years of European colonialism that has spawned revitalization movements in Indig-
enous cultures around the world. Participants in the newly formed religions attempt to
bring back and preserve cultural lifeways and values that they feel are being threatened by
outside forces. Notably, the new religions feature traits fused together from two or more
cultures, a process called syncretism. Deprivation and stress explain classic revitalization
movements, but movement from deprivation to recovery from oppression may spark a
cultural revival.
Revitalization movements form as people deliberately attempt to bring about change in
a society. They are initiated frequently by an individual or a small group that promises
better times. A.F.C. Wallace identified six types of revitalization movements, including
nativistic movements, revivalistic movements, Cargo Cults, vitalistic movements, mille-
narian movements, and messianic movements. The six types of revitalization movements
and associated behaviors are not mutually exclusive, so a particular movement can mani-
fest many of these patterns. For instance, Christianity features millenarian and messianic
characteristics. Classic revitalization movements are exemplified by the Great Basin/Plains
Ghost Dance movements, Vodou in Haiti, Santeria in Cuba, Cargo Cults in Melanesia,
and Latter-day Saints in the United States. An example of modern-day cultural revival is
ecotourism and medicinal trails inspired by Maya traditional medicine and H-men.
Fundamentalism is also a revitalization force in religion. The fundamentalist world-
view is focused on finding certainty and simplicity in an otherwise complex and uncertain
world. While the term was originally used with Christian groups, it has been expanded
now to include all of the Abrahamic faiths. Recent fundamentalist movements in Islam
include the Iranian Revolution and the Arab Spring.
All religious groups require some degree of conformity from their members. However,
groups vary in the level of this demand and the degree of control the group attempts to
