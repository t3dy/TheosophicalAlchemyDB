# The Anthropology of Religion, Magic, and Witchcraft

## Section: Conclusion

Conclusion
As we have seen in our discussion of the definition of religion and different approaches
to the study of religion, ethnocentrism can be a major impediment to developing a true
understanding of religious beliefs and practices in other societies. The goal of anthropol-
ogy is to move past ethnocentrism toward an approach of cultural relativism. This is espe-
cially true in the study of religion. The anthropological approach—and the central way
of looking at the religious world in this book—is to study what people believe and do in
regard to a sacred supernatural, not to judge whether these beliefs and actions are based
on objective truth or not. The anthropological study of religion calls for methodological
agnosticism.
Although agnosticism has taken on the connotation of not having made up one’s mind,
the original meaning of the word is different. Agnostics say that the nature of the super-
natural is unknowable and that it is as impossible to prove the nonexistence of the super-
natural as it is to prove its existence. In this book, we will seek neither to prove nor to
disprove but merely to observe. In the words of the philosopher Baruch Spinoza, writing
in the seventeenth century, “I have made a ceaseless effort not to ridicule, not to bewail,
not to scorn human actions, but to understand them.”
Summary
Anthropology is the study of humanity. Anthropologists study human societies as inte-
grated wholes, an approach termed holism. This approach is seen in the broad scope of
anthropology, which is often divided into the fields of biological anthropology, archae-
ology, linguistics, and cultural anthropology. In cultural anthropology, societies may be
studied over long periods of time during which the investigator lives within the community
and participates in the lives of the people under study, a technique known as participant
observation. The final product is an ethnography, a descriptive study of a human society.
Anthropologists attempt to see the world through the eyes of the people being studied
(emic perspective) and understand others through a more scientific lens (etic perspective).
In their description and analysis of people’s customs and ideas, anthropologists practice
cultural relativism and do not judge other cultures unless there are human rights violations
under consideration. The goal is to study what people believe, not whether or not what
they believe is true or right.
A central concept in anthropology is culture. Culture includes all aspects of the human
experience passed down from generation to generation. Culture gives meaning to reality;
we live in a real, physical world, but our minds interpret this world through a cultural lens
and even create new realities.
There have been many theoretical approaches to the study of religion. The evolutionary
approach, developed in the late 1800s, focused on when and how religions began and how
they evolved from the simple to the complex. The Marxist approach, based on the writings
of Karl Marx, sees religion as a construction of those in power, designed to divert people’s
attention from the exploitation in their lives. The functional approach asks the question:
What does religion do? For example, Malinowski concluded that magic functions to pro-
vide control and certainty in an otherwise uncertain situation. The interpretive approach,

associated with Clifford Geertz, believes that the task of anthropologists is to make sense
of cultural systems by studying meaning. The psychosocial approach is concerned with the
relationship between culture and personality and the connection between the society and
the individual, and the bio-cognitive approach studies religion as a product of the stimuli
in the brain. The postmodern approach is a theoretical movement that emphasizes subjec-
tivity over objectivity, reflexivity, and the belief that all knowledge is a human construc-
tion that scholars must seek to deconstruct.
Religion is a difficult concept to define when we try to include all human societies. An
analytic definition focuses on how religion manifests itself or is expressed in a culture. A
functional definition is concerned with the role that religion plays in a society. Finally, an
essentialist definition looks at what the essential nature of religion is and emphasizes the
fact that religion is the domain of the extraordinary. Our operational definition in this
textbook looks at religion as a set of cultural beliefs and practices that usually include a
basic set of characteristics and draw on elements of all three of these approaches.
When studying religion, it is important to understand the broader aspect of a group’s
culture: how they make a living, how they relate and interact socially, and how decision-
making is distributed within the group. As societies change and adapt to diverse physical
environments and subsistence systems, including foraging, horticulture, agriculture, pas-
toralism, and urbanization, religious beliefs and practices shift as well. While foragers
frequently have an interactive social relationship with the flora and fauna within their
environments, more intensive extractive subsistence systems, such as agriculture, increas-
ingly see the physical environment purely in terms of its material resource value. These
changing relationships and value systems are reflected in religious beliefs and practices.

Religious symbols
All animals communicate with one another. Most often, this communication is simple and
specific to the situation. A stimulus—such as the sight of a stranger, predator, or food—
may bring about a response of some kind: threat, flight, or social interaction. The reaction
is an immediate response to the specific circumstances.
Humans also encounter things like strangers, predators, and food. However, the human
response is more complex than other living creatures. Humans may react to the presence
of a stranger entering their midst by seeing the stranger as an enemy warrior, a merchant,
or a monk, for example. The reaction to a predator might be to prepare a spear for defense
or to perform magical rites to ward off the danger. While humans might feel hunger and
respond to the presence of food, many edible and nutritious foods are shunned because
of cultural or religious prohibitions. All of these behaviors involve communication, be it
storytelling, ritual, or the articulation of food prohibitions.
The complexity of human communication is made possible through the ability of humans
to create and use symbols. Symbols permit people to discuss abstract topics and talk about
things in the past, in an envisioned future, or even in a supernatural world. The world of
religion is a symbolic world. Interpretive anthropologist Clifford Geertz famously defined
religion as
a system of symbols which acts to establish powerful, pervasive, and long-lasting moods
and motivations in men by formulating conceptions of a general order of existence and
clothing these conceptions with such an aura of factuality that the moods and motiva-
tions seem uniquely realistic.1
Geertz saw the defining feature of all religious systems as symbols because they move
people and aid them in understanding the world as they envision and create it. In essence,
through symbols, humans become co-creators of the cosmos they inhabit.
What is a symbol?
In its simplest form, a symbol is something that stands for something else with no neces-
sary natural connection. It is arbitrary, meaning it is not directly related or connected to
what it symbolizes. As long as there is agreement within a community as to the meaning

of the symbol, individuals can communicate with one another using symbols standing in

Being able to create and use symbols is critical to being human. We use symbols to refer
to things not directly in front of us—a faraway place, for example, or something we would
like to do in the future, including talking about and sharing this with others. This ability to
use symbols to refer to things and activities remote from the user is termed displacement.
Symbolic anthropologist Victor Turner described symbols as being multivocal, meaning
they have many possible meanings.2 A symbol’s meaning can shift depending on circum-
stances (operative meaning), the group’s particular interpretation of the symbol (exegeti-
cal meaning), and even other artifacts found alongside the symbol (positional meaning).
These properties of symbols make them open or changeable. Consider, for example, the
cross as a Christian symbol. What it means at a church altar is quite different from what
it means at a cross burning by the Ku Klux Klan. Symbols are dense, multi-layered, and
have meanings fully understood only by the groups (and sometimes sub-groups) using the
symbol.
We also use symbols to refer to states of being or to ideas. Symbols can stand for emo-
tions and complex philosophical concepts that exist only in our minds. Symbols can create
a supernatural world or create myths about the past. Joseph Church, discussing language,
writes:
[W]e can manipulate symbols in ways impossible with the things they stand for, and so
arrive at novel and even creative versions of reality . . . We can verbally rearrange situa-
tions which in themselves would resist rearrangement . . . we can isolate features which
in fact cannot be isolated . . . we can juxtapose objects and events far separated in time
and space . . . we can, if we will, turn the universe symbolically inside out.3
Many symbols are physical objects or artistic representations. Symbols do not have to be
physical, however. Language is a system of symbols, but here, the symbols are sounds and
characters (e.g., letters or logograms). The only reason that the word “apple” means a par-
ticular type of fruit is because when we learned English, we learned that the combination
of sounds and/or letters that make up the word “apple” stands for that particular fruit.
There is nothing inherently connecting the sound of the word apple to the fruit it is desig-
nating. In fact, this fruit is known by many other names in other languages—manzana in
Spanish and elma in Turkish, for example.

Of course, not all symbols are physical artifacts or artistic representations. Words, written and
spoken, are also critical elements in religious practice. In Jewish ritual, the Torah is removed
from the ark with great ceremony to be read in a service. In Tantric Buddhism, found in Tibet,

The swastika
A symbol such as the swastika can stand for very complex ideas and can carry great emo-
tional resonance. Most Americans and Europeans looking at the swastika experience anger
or dread. In 1919, the German Nazi Party adopted the swastika as its symbol, and it is asso-
ciated with the terrible events perpetrated by the Nazis in World War II. A swastika spray-
painted on a wall can be legally defined as a hate crime in Germany and the United States.
But the swastika also serves as a religious symbol in some faith traditions. As a pattern
of lines set at right angles to one another, clockwise or counterclockwise, it carries no
inherent meaning. The word itself, swastika, is derived from the Sanskrit su (“good”) and
avasti (“to exist”), and it is found in the religious art associated with Hinduism, Buddhism,
and Jainism. It is also found in the ancient art of Scandinavia and the Middle East and
even in early Christian art. In most of these contexts, the swastika has a positive meaning,
such as prosperity and good luck. However, in some cultures, the reverse swastika is called
the sauvastika and stands for darkness, misfortune, and suffering.

by a Zuni priest (see Chapter 6 for a discussion of Zuni priests) and are taken into

to collectors and those who believe in their protective and healing powers.
A less common, albeit controversial, craft involves painting katsina dolls carved
from cottonwood roots. In Zuni cosmology, the kokko, “katsinas,” are “intangible
spirits . . . including deities, past heroes, lost children, the ancestral dead, and numer-
ous supernatural assistants or allies.”6 Masked dancers personify the katsina spirits
in two six-month ceremonial cycles marked by the summer and winter solstices. For
instance, at the beginning of the winter solstice ceremonies, six gigantic Shalakos—
Couriers of the Rain Gods—plant prayer sticks in shrines outside old Halona village.
During kiva and medicine society rites, katsina dolls are given to initiates to help them
learn about that katsina’s spirit. Katsina dolls also have personal uses: dolls connected
to fertility are put in the bedrooms of couples trying to conceive, or parents some-
times place punisher, or disciplinary katsinas, in the bedrooms of naughty children.
Several Zuni artists began illustrating katsinas in the 1910s, and by the mid-1960s,
Duane Dishta used opaque watercolors to paint sets of 220 and 155 katsinas. Shortly
afterward, Dishta and a few others began carving katsina dolls for the commercial
marketplace.7 In recent years, Zuni tribal elders and priests have complained about
carvers who manufacture and sell katsina dolls traditionally restricted to personal
or ceremonial purposes. Similar to Islamic beliefs that forbid rendering images of
Muhammed, they also oppose paintings and other representations of Zuni deities.
One priest even suggested that the plethora of commercially sold katsinas has led to
an increase in unplanned children. Some believe the few daring souls who surrepti-
tiously carve or paint katsinas face supernatural sanctions. Publicly displayed images
of katsinas—on Facebook and in newspaper and magazine articles—in recent years
have caused the Zuni religious hierarchy to rebuke those responsible.

Sacred art and architecture
Although the swastika, pentagram, cross, and other symbols are important representations in
their respective religious systems, they are also combined with other symbolic elements found
in more complex settings or works of art. Imagine, for example, walking into a great Euro-

Maya architecture and hierophany
Today, 31 distinct Maya cultures representing eight million people live where their ancestors
built elaborate ceremonial centers in Mexico’s Yucatán Peninsula and Upper Central America
(Guatemala, Belize, and Honduras).8 Maya city-states emerged during the Middle Preclassic
Period (1800–300 BCE) and flourished throughout the Late Preclassic (300 BCE–300 CE) and
Classic periods (300–925 CE), though individual city-states never unified into a polity. Maya
architecture designated sacred space through monuments built to praise rulers and legitimize
their descent from gods by justifying their royal bloodline through symbolism using celestial
metaphors everyone understood. According to archaeoastronomer Anthony Aveni, religions
that “associate the creation of the universe with the origins of humanity tend to dramatize the
cosmogony by attempting to reproduce on earth a miniaturized version of the cosmos.”9

symbolized the place of creation on Day 0–3114 BCE—when the World Tree, along with
landforms and mountains, rose from the primordial sea. Classic Period Maya p yramids—
referred to as “tree-stones”—and palaces are arranged around open plazas to symbolize
creation. Pyramids represent the mountains that rose from the sea and signify the human-
constructed axis mundi connecting the three vertical levels of the universe recognized
throughout the Americas: Underworld, Middleworld, and Upperworld. Because of their
shamanic abilities, only the royalty could traverse all three levels. During important cere-
monies, shaman-kings ascended the staircases of the pyramids and walked into the temples
situated at the summit (Figure 2.7). Entering through the dark recesses of the temple into
the juncture of the three levels of the universe at that location, the shaman-kings manipu-

Yoruba color terminology
The language of the Yorubas of Nigeria in the Guinea Coast culture area has only three
basic color terms. Each term covers a much larger part of the color spectrum than English
color terms. Funfun includes what English speakers call white, silver, and pale gray. Pupa
covers red, pink, orange, and deep yellow. Dúdú includes black, blue, purple, green, dark
brown, red-brown, and dark gray.
In the English language, some colors are paired with emotions. We say that a sad
person is feeling blue; when angry, we see red; a jealous person is green with envy; a
coward is yellow. Yorubas also associate colors with particular temperatures and tem-
peraments. For example, funfun is associated with coolness, age, and wisdom; pupa
evokes hotness; dúdú is dark and warm.
The supernatural world of the Yorubas is populated by many spiritual beings called
orisha. (The orisha will be discussed in greater detail in Chapter 8.) Obatalá, the king
of the orisha, an ethical and merciful deity, is linked to the color funfun, the color of
wisdom and respect. Objects associated with Obatalá are frequently colored white, and
he is sometimes called the “King of the White Cloth.” In contrast, Sango is associated
with the color pupa. He rules thunder and lightning and is proud and quick-tempered,
and his images are often red. Ossosi, who is associated with hunting, is introverted and
unstable. He is linked to the color dúdú, green and blue. The messenger of the gods is
an orisha named Esu-Elegba. He is the intermediary between the people, the gods, and
ancestors and is the first deity addressed in any ritual. Representations of Esu-Elegba
are usually done in black and white or, in Yoruba color terminology, funfun and dúdú.
These are contrasting colors and represent the god’s unpredictability. Artistic represen-
tations of Esu-Elegba in carvings, paintings, and embroideries primarily use these two
colors. A Yoruba seeing such a representation can identify the orisha in part by the
colors being used. In this way, color serves as a language.

Great Cycle of the calendar ended on December 21, 2012, 5,125 years from Day 0, but

Different religious systems have different views of time. For some, time is cyclic,
and for others, it is a progression from one phase to another. This was true of the
ancient Mayas. The Mayas lived in the Fourth World that began on August 11, 3114

the baktun, which consists of 144,000 days (approximately 395 years). The endpoint

winter solstice, marked the end of the thirteenth baktun since creation.16 Although
many predicted the end of the world on this date, there is no evidence that Mayas
see the end of a baktun as an apocalyptic event.
Other religious systems also see time as being cyclical. One characteristic of
Christian fundamentalism (Chapter 12) is dispensationalism. This term refers to
the belief that God has divided the history of the Earth into distinct ages or “dis-
pensations.” In each age, God has a particular relationship with the world. This idea
was developed by the Englishman John Nelson Darby (1800–1882), who read the
Bible as if it were a history of predictions. He believed there were seven phases,
each ending with a catastrophe—such as the Fall, the Flood of Noah, and the Cru-
cifixion. People today are living in the Sixth Dispensation. Like previous stages, this
stage, too, is predicted to come to an end in a catastrophe. However, just before,
there would be a “Rapture” in which born-again Christians would be taken up to
heaven and escape the sufferings of the Last Days. This is called the pre-tribulational
rapture theory, another of Darby’s predictions based on a literal reading of the Bible.
Later, the born-again Christians would rule with Christ in the Seventh Dispensation,
the last historical age.
William Miller (1782–1849) was a Baptist preacher who, through careful study of
the Bible as a historical document, believed that the world as it was known would

much of American Christianity. His movement began to spread and was referred
to as Millerism. As the date approached, Miller predicted that the Second Com-

March 21, 1844. When the predicted event failed to take place, he announced a
second date based upon “corrected” calculations—October 22, 1844. The failure
of the Advent occurring on that date, too, is called “The Great Disappointment.”
Nevertheless, the idea had taken hold in American Christianity, although usually
without a specified date. Out of the tradition of Millerism grew several contem-
porary religious traditions, such as the Seventh Day Adventists and Jehovah’s
Witnesses.

Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar, the month during which the
Qur’an was sent from Allah/God to Mohammad. Ramadan is a time for worship and
contemplation. During this month, Muslims follow many constraints in their daily
lives, such as not eating or drinking during daylight hours. At the end of the day, the
fast is broken with prayer and a meal. The Laylat-al-Qadr (the Night of Power) is cel-
ebrated on the evening of the 27th day of Ramadan, the night when Mohammad first
received the revelation of the Qur’an. According to the Qur’an, this is also the night
when God determines the course of the world for the following year. The end of the
fasting and the month of Ramadan is celebrated for three days in Id-al-Fitr (the Feast
of Fast Breaking).
The Islamic calendar is a lunar calendar. Each month begins with the sighting of the
first crescent of the new moon. The length of the month thus defined does not divide
evenly into the solar year—it is about 11 to 12 days short. As a result, the start of
Ramadan shifts from year to year. In 2024, the month of Ramadan began on March
10, while in 2027 it begins February 7. One of the consequences of this is that when
Ramadan falls in the winter season, the period of fasting is relatively short, and the
weather is relatively cool. It is quite different when Ramadan falls during the summer
season.
Another example of periodic rituals being set to older calendars is the Yamim Nora’im
(Days of Awe), or Jewish high holy days, which are observed for a ten-day period between
the first and tenth days of the month of Tishri, the seventh month of the Jewish calendar.
Rosh Hashanah (Jewish New Year) marks the beginning of this time period, and Yom
Kippur (Day of Atonement) marks the end. These are considered the most important of
all the Jewish holidays and are the only ones considered purely religious and not based on
any historical or natural event.
The high holy days are a time for penitence and prayer, during which Jews are given time
to repent of their sins against God and ask God for forgiveness. On Rosh Hashanah, God
judges the people and records His judgment in the Book of Life. However, there is a ten-
day reprieve until Yom Kippur, when the Book is closed and sealed. Yom Kippur itself is
a day for fasting and prayer.
The Hebrew calendar is also a lunar calendar, but in order to keep the holidays from
shifting too far, a thirteenth month is added every two or three years. In some years, extra
days are added to prevent Yom Kippur from occurring on the Sabbath. Thus, the major
ceremonies are maintained within specific, predictable seasons of the year.
In the early Christian church, the celebration of Easter was correlated with the Jewish
celebration of Passover since the Last Supper was a Passover Seder and celebrated in the
spring. However, because different Jewish communities set the date somewhat differently,
the early church attempted to set the celebration of Easter according to their calendar (the
Julian calendar, originally based upon the calendar reforms of Julius Caesar). Yet the date
kept drifting later in the year. Finally, in 1582, Pope Gregory XIII decreed a new, reformed
calendar that we use today (the Gregorian calendar).

the totemic species. Members of a totemic group have responsibilities to perform religious
rituals, including initiation ceremonies. There are many other rules that are part of the
special relationship between an individual or group and its totem.
Totemic affiliations also organize Aboriginal societies. Specific clans are associated with
particular totems. There are rules regulating who can marry whom, defining the affiliation
of one’s children as well as the appropriate social behaviors between different classes of kin.
The determination of the group into which a child is born can be complex. In some
groups, it is determined by rules of kinship, and in many groups, pregnancy is thought to
be the result of a totemic spirit, perhaps residing in a sacred water hole, entering the womb
of the woman. The husband and other elders will question the woman to determine which
spirit from what totemic group entered her womb to determine that child’s affiliation. In
some groups, the identity of the spirit is revealed in the father’s dream. On death, the indi-
vidual’s soul returns to the totemic well.
At some point during adolescence, young men are initiated into manhood. These initiation
rituals involve the learning ritual knowledge, including the ability to look at and handle
totemic objects. These rituals also involve some type of alteration of the body, such as
circumcision or the knocking out of a tooth. When visiting sacred places described in the
Dreamtime stories, wounds are opened, or new wounds are made on the body, and a
person’s blood is allowed to fall on sacred objects.

The symbolism of music
Music is a key element in ritual, and it can involve a wide array of sounds, including
natural sounds, the human voice, or material instruments. It may simply set the mood
for a ceremony or may actually be the primary vehicle by which religious stories are
told and by which people communicate with the gods. Music fills many roles during
religious rituals and is used to teach, express or engender emotional states, to produce
altered states of consciousness, to please the supernatural powers, or to make contact
with them.
An important function of music is the facilitation of memorization. In nonliterate socie-
ties, large amounts of narrative must be committed to memory. Anyone who has had to
memorize a piece of prose in school knows how difficult this can be. Poetry is easier to
memorize than prose because poetry has rhythm and rhyme. The easiest of all to memo-
rize are the lyrics to a song, primarily because of the relationship between rhythm and
memory. For this reason, narratives and prayers are frequently chanted or sung.

Examples would be the sound of running water or the echo of a human voice from a cliff.
The way for humans to make contact with supernatural powers is to imitate their sounds.

and can be used to contact the spirits of the earth. The type of vocal sounds Tuvas use is also

Besides the human voice, instruments are also important in the expression of music, and
an unbelievable array of musical instruments can be found throughout the world. There
are four basic types:
• idiophones are instruments that are struck, shaken, or rubbed. Common examples are
rattles, bells, wooden drums, rasps, bullroarers, marimbas, and xylophones.
• membranophones are instruments that incorporate a taut membrane or skin. These
include drums.
• cordophones are instruments with taut strings that can be plucked, strummed, hit, or
sawed. These include harps, zithers, and violins.
• aerophones, in which air is blown across or into some type of passageway, such as a
pipe, include whistles, pipes, didjeridus, flutes, and trumpets.
For some cultures, sounds produced by specific musical instruments have religious signifi-
cance. The best-known examples in the anthropological literature are the didjeridu of the
Australian Aborigines and the molimo of the Pygmies of the Ituri Forest of Central Africa.
These are very simple aerophones that are essentially nothing more than long pipes, usu-
ally wood. The sounds of these instruments are said to be the sounds of spirits. Initiated
men play the instruments out of sight of the women and children, who are told that the
sound is the actual voice of the spirits. The true nature of the sound is revealed to young
men as part of initiation rites.
Music plays an important role worldwide, and it is associated with religious rituals and
storytelling. Membranophones, especially drums, are important instruments in Africa, for
example. Rhythm is highly developed and, in contrast with Euro-American traditions,
melody and harmony are secondary. Many types of drums are played, often combined
into large drum ensembles.

The symbolism of dance
While the use of dance is not as common in Western religious rituals, across cultures, dance
is an important means of symbolically representing the supernatural world and telling reli-
gious stories. Of course, dance does not exist in isolation. Dance is usually performed to
music, frequently involves chanting or singing words, and involves the manipulation of
physical symbols such as costumes and masks, sets, and props.
Culture consists of patterned, traditional behaviors. We define dance as a system of pat-
terned, traditional movements. These movements, involving the whole body or sometimes
just a part of the body, such as the hands, are symbolic in that they have culturally deter-
mined meanings. Characters in well-known stories are identified by their traditional move-
ments, such as their way of walking and moving their arms, as well as by their costumes,
makeup, and masks. In societies that lack systems of writing, dance becomes an important
vehicle for telling sacred stories to the community. For example, among native Hawaiians,
hula dances told the stories of the gods and goddesses.
Indigenous Kwakwaka’wakws of the North American Northwest Coast culture area
say that a human family line was created when the ancestor of the group came down to
Earth, took off his mask, and became human. Carved animal masks are an important
religious art form for Kwakwaka’wakws and are frequently worn by dancers who retell
important mythic stories. Dance, however, goes far beyond the telling of stories. Dances
can act as offerings and many deities like to be entertained. Dancers also can become
conduits of supernatural power. The Kwakwaka’wakw masked dancers, for example,
become the beings whose mask is worn for the duration of the ritual (this transforma-
tion is called transmogrification) as dance moves them from the natural to the super-
natural world.
In many cultures, gods and spirits enter the human body and take over its functioning.
The particular movements during possession identify the god within the body and may
actually provide communication between the deity and the human participants. For exam-
ple, possession is an important feature of Vodou. During a Vodou ritual, a song summons
a particular god. When the god arrives, he or she possesses one of the dancers. A common
metaphor is that the dancer is a horse on which the deity rides. Which god has possessed a
dancer can be deduced by the dancer’s movements and actions. (We will discuss the Vodou
religion in more detail in Chapter 12.)
Early in the history of Islam, a group that was most interested in the mystical aspects
of the religion broke off to form the Sufi. One of the most important of the Sufi mystics
was a man named Jalāl al-Dīn Muḥammad Rūmī (also called Mevlana Rumi), who lived
in the thirteenth-century in the Ottoman Empire. He was a poet, mystic, and theologian
who studied with and was a devotee of the Persian poet and spiritual instructor Shams-i
Tabrizi. When Tabrizi mysteriously disappeared (rumored to have been murdered), Rumi
revived the practice of whirling as a way to alleviate his extreme grief. After Rumi’s
death, his followers established the Mevlevi Order, or Mawlawiyya, in honor of him.
Members of the Mevlevi Order are often called “whirling dervishes” in Turkey, where
many of them settled.

The oldest evidence of tattooing was discovered on the body of Ötzi, also known as the
Iceman. Discovered in the Ötztal Alps between Germany and Italy in 1991, his mummified
remains had been buried under glacial ice for 5,300 years. The tattoos appear to have been

possible to know for certain the purpose of this tattooing; however, many are located on

The history of tattooing in more recent American and European cultures can be traced
to the voyages of James Cook in the eighteenth century. Many of Cook’s sailors were tat-
tooed during their stay in Tahiti, and the word tattoo is derived from the Tahitian word
ta-tu, meaning “to mark or strike.” Tattooing involves piercing or cutting the skin and
then introducing a pigment into the wound. In contemporary Western societies, this is
done with an electronic device, but the principle is the same.
The social implications of tattooing in Western society have changed over time. At times,
tattooing has been a fad among the aristocracy or members of particular social groups, such
as sailors, gangs, and prisoners. In this context, a tattoo served as a mark of social identity.
At other times, tattooing has been a way to express dissatisfaction with the social order and
a way to distance oneself from mainstream society. Tattoos also serve a religious purpose
when one places a religious symbol or a memorial to a loved one permanently on their skin.
Closely related to tattooing is cicatrization or scarification. This is frequently seen in
peoples with dark skin on whom tattoos would not show well. In scarification, a piece of
skin is raised and cut, and some material, such as ash, is rubbed in to encourage the pro-
duction of scars. Closely related to cicatrization is branding, in which the scars are created
by burns.
