# The Anthropology of Religion, Magic, and Witchcraft

## Section: Conclusion

Conclusion
In Chapter 1, we listed the six dimensions of religion as set forth by Ninian Smart. One of
these was the experiential dimension, an encounter with a sacred reality beyond ordinary
experience. Religious experiences can be highly emotional. They can range from a general-
ized feeling of well-being to dramatic visions. These mental states—states that differ from
one’s normal state of awareness—are altered states of consciousness. They define religious
experience. It is through such altered states that supernatural phenomena and powers
manifest themselves, and abstract beliefs move into the realm of lived experience.
Interpreting the changes in mental state when one is in an altered state of consciousness
is largely influenced by culture. In other words, culture provides a framework of meaning
and an interpretive lens for our experiences.
The religious interpretations of altered states of consciousness generally fall into two
categories. First, supernatural power, usually in the form of spirits or gods, enters the
person’s body, a phenomenon that we call spirit possession. An individual can control
the spirit within his or her body to accomplish certain goals, or the spirit that possesses a
human body can use that body to heal or to divine the unknown, often without the knowl-
edge or the memory of the possessed person. However, possession by an unwanted spirit
can also bring about illness that requires exorcism rituals.
The second common religious interpretation of an altered state of consciousness is that
a person has entered a trance state because the soul has left the person’s body. The expe-
rience of the individual in the altered state is then associated with the experiences of the
soul, which is operating in a supernatural realm.
Altered states frequently play an important role in treatment for the healer and the
patient. Healing is facilitated, and in some cases even accomplished, through suggestibil-
ity, emotional catharsis, and feelings of rejuvenation. For example, when possessed by a
spirit, the healer may use the supernatural power of the spirit to remove the cause of the
illness, often by sucking the offending spirit out of the patient’s body. When an illness is

The most common idea of religious altered states, though, is the idea of achieving
a unitary state, a state in which the individual experiences a feeling of becoming one
with the supernatural, however this is conceived of by the community. For some, this is
becoming one with God or a spirit; for others, it may be expressed as becoming one with
a generalized supernatural force. We saw an example of this in our discussion of the
Sabarimala pilgrimage, the goal of which is a unitary state with the god Lord Ayyappan.
The idea of the unitary state is often one of the major components of a religious ritual
or even an entire religious system. A common religious theme is that humans were once
at one with the supernatural but somehow became separated. The goal of many religious
practices is to regain that unity. This theme can be seen in many familiar religions: for
Christians, Jesus provides the pathway back to God; for Buddhists, following the teach-
ings of Buddha allows an individual to attain oneness with the universe; for Muslims,
reconciliation is possible through submission of the will to Allah.
In previous chapters, we discussed symbols, narratives, and the importance of religious
rituals. However, it is only with a discussion of altered states of consciousness that a true
appreciation of the power of rituals can be reached. Narratives provide a basis for belief,
but it is only with ritual that these ideas are turned into experiences.
A religious altered state, for the believer, offers visceral proof of the existence of the
supernatural. These experiences move the supernatural from the realm of abstract belief
into that of a lived reality.
Summary
An altered state of consciousness is any mental state that differs from a normal mental
state. Such states are characterized by a number of psychological experiences, such as
alterations in patterns of thinking, disturbed sense of time, changes in emotional expres-
sion, distortion in body image, and others. A person can enter an altered state through
a number of situations, including reduction of external stimulation, increase of external
stimulation, increased alertness or decreased alertness, pain, or alterations in body chem-
istry, such as those that accompany fasting and sleep deprivation. These factors create
observable changes in the activity of the brain.
Altered states of consciousness can also be brought about by the use of hallucinogens
within a religious context. The experience is strongly influenced by cultural expectations.
Examples of altered states of consciousness are found throughout human history and
across cultures. Today, there are increasing opportunities for individuals to become “spir-
itual tourists,” resulting in the potential disruption of Indigenous communities where their
substances have an ancient history.

Religious specialists
In small-scale societies with relatively simple technologies, rituals are performed by most
or all of the adult members of the community. However, some individuals may develop a
special interest in religious practices and develop an ability to contact the supernatural.
An example is the healers found among !Kung Sans, in which around half of the men
and a number of women become healers while remaining full participants in the secular
life of the group. Full-time, specialized religious statuses usually do not exist in such
societies because these societies do not produce the food surplus necessary to support
full-time specialists.
As discussed earlier, religious activities are not clearly delineated from non-religious activi-
ties in small-scale societies. Religious activities are interwoven with secular activities, and,
sometimes, the separation between religious and secular is not even recognized. While these
societies lack full-time religious specialists, some societies have developed part-time special-
ists. These are people who earn their living at an economic task, such as hunting or farming,
but who are called on to perform rituals when necessary because of their special knowledge
or abilities. Such a person might be paid for their services, but many are not.
In larger and more technologically complex societies, we see the development of many
occupational specializations, including religious specialists. These practitioners may be
full-time specialists who derive their income primarily from the performance of religious
rituals either through voluntary community support or through payment for services by
individuals whom they have helped. For example, religious practitioners may be found in
a marketplace, ready to be approached by clients who need their services in order to secure
economic success for some endeavor or perhaps a cure for an illness. In some societies,
religious practitioners attain important political and economic positions.
There are many terms used to describe religious specialists. Unfortunately, the terms are
not used in a consistent manner—sometimes, it is a problem of translation because the
nature of religious practitioners and their activities in many societies (emic perspectives)
might not neatly fit a defined category in our society or as defined by anthropologists (etic
perspectives).
Two terms frequently used to categorize religious specialists are shaman and priest.
These are not mutually exclusive categories but rather ends of a continuum. Very often, a
religious practitioner that we might classify as a priest will perform some functions that
are more characteristic of a shaman; the reverse is also true.

Shamans
The distinction between priests and shamans is not always clear-cut, and there are many reli-
gious specialists who fall somewhere in between. Generally speaking, in contrast to a priest,
a shaman receives his or her power directly from the spirit world. He or she acquires status
and abilities, such as healing, through personal communication with the supernatural dur-
ing shamanic trances or altered states of consciousness. The route to becoming a religious
specialist and how each functions within a society will also differ for shamans and priests.
Defining shamanism
Like many terms used in the study of religion, the term shaman has been used in different
ways. Some use the term very broadly to encompass a wide variety of phenomena; oth-
ers use it in a very narrow sense. Most agree that shamanism refers to techniques used by
specific kinds of religious specialists and that shamans can be found in a wide variety of
cultures and religions (Figure 6.1).
The term shaman actually comes from the Tungus language of Central Siberia. It refers to
religious specialists who use handheld drums and spirit helpers to aid the members of their

In other cultures, they may be known as healers, curers, preachers, or even “witchdoctors.”
For those who apply the term more broadly, the crucial elements of shamanism include direct
contact and communication with the supernatural through trance, the use of spirit helpers, the
act of journeying or spiritual travel, the use of a specific culturally recognized and transmitted
method and paraphernalia, and a socially recognized special position for the shaman.
Unlike priests, who are full-time community-based specialists, shamans are usually part-
time independent contractors. The authority of a shaman lies in his or her charisma and
ability to heal using secular and sacred techniques. The relationship between a shaman
and the community is a personal one. Shamans focus on specific problems, such as those
that affect a particular individual or family. Because clients often select a shaman in a
particular situation for the shaman’s reputation and track record in curing, successful sha-
mans can amass a significant degree of social authority.
Becoming a shaman
Shamans often see themselves as being called by spirits to serve as a shaman, and they
receive their power and authority directly from supernatural beings. In some societies, a
person may deliberately seek a call by inducing an altered state of consciousness. This is
most frequent in societies where shamans achieve some degree of social authority. In other
societies, the task of being a shaman is so difficult and demanding that individuals do not
seek a call. When a call comes—through a dream, a trance, or recovery from an illness—
the individual may be reluctant to act on it.
Oftentimes, shamans run in family lines, and members are called, or elected, by the
spirit world. Aurora Saqui, a Maya woman from Belize interviewed by anthropologist
Ben Kracht in December 2006, related how her uncle, Don Elijio Panti, a H’men (shaman,
the “h” is not pronounced), was over 100 years old when he died. Although Aurora did
not want to become an H’men and ignored some of the signs from the spirit world, such
as finding sastun, or crystals, lying on the ground while walking along paths, she had a
dream early one morning. In her dream, Don Elijio told her it was her turn to take his
place. When she awoke a couple of hours later, she learned that her uncle had passed away
about the time she had dreamt about him. Afterward, she became an H’men (Figure 6.2).
The spirits will commonly call to the future shaman during a particularly difficult time
in their lives, including periods of stress, illness, accident, possession, or near-death experi-
ences. Shamans are sometimes referred to as “wounded healers.” At times, a shaman may
be called through their own illnesses, including through incidents of psychosis or possession.
The shamanic initiation often includes the idea that the spirits eat, dismember, or kill the per-
son before that person can be reborn as a shaman. The spirits are testing the initiate, and the
symbolism of death, transformation, and rebirth are very common in this transformation.
Shamans often become seriously ill as a part of their divine call, oftentimes falling into
comas. Importantly, they learn how to heal other people because they recover from their

The shamanic role and rituals
The shaman may contact the supernatural by traditional, standardized methods that fit
our definition of ritual. The ritual is only a means for contacting and establishing a rela-
tionship with a supernatural entity; it is not an end in itself.
The identification and control of spirit helpers and the ability to enter altered states of
consciousness (referred to as shamanic journeying) are central to the role of the shaman.
These spirits help the shaman fight other hostile spirits and help the shaman diagnose and
treat illnesses. In some cultures, illness results from the loss of the patient’s soul. In such
occurrences, the shaman enters an altered state of consciousness and sends his or her soul
to recapture the patient’s soul and bring it back to the patient’s body.
The shamanic ritual may be a simple affair, analogous to a private consultation between
the patient and the shaman, or a major public ritual. In the latter, it is common for the ritual

shaman. Drumming, singing, dancing, and elaborate costumes contribute to this effect, as

Siberian shamanism
Shamans of Central Siberia are religious specialists who use handheld drums and spirit
helpers to help the members of their community. Siberian shamans perform rituals to
heal the sick, divine the future, and ensure success in the hunt. Like most other shamans,
Siberian shamans see the world as divided into three realms with various strata within
the upper and lower realms. The Upper Realm is one of light and good spirits; the Mid-
dle Realm is the home of people and spirits of the earth; and the Lower Realm is one of
darkness and evil spirits.3 While in an altered state of consciousness, it is the shaman’s
role to communicate with various spirits by journeying within the other Realms.
One of the main functions of the shaman is healing. This can be accomplished in many
ways. A shaman can communicate with spirits to learn what they want. They can also dis-
pel a disease-causing spirit or retrieve a lost soul. A shaman has spirit familiars or animal
soul guides, also called power animals, that assist in the shaman’s work. These spirits give
the shaman his particular qualities and powers. It is by having these spirits that the sha-
man is able to heal, but they also give the shaman the potential to do harm.
Other shamans specialize in contacting spirits to help ensure a successful hunt. In this case,
the shaman contacts the spirits of an animal species and makes a deal with them. The animal
spirits will supply humans with animal food, and the humans will eventually supply the spirits
with human flesh and blood in exchange. This is one cause of human sickness and death. It is
the role of the shaman to minimize the amount of human sickness while trying to maximize
the number of animals that will be successfully hunted. Part of a shaman’s success results from
a pact with the animal spirits through a special relationship with the “daughter of the elk” or
reindeer spirit, usually symbolized as the shaman’s relationship to their drum (see Figure 6.4).

Korean shamanism
Although shamanism is usually thought of as a feature of small-scale societies, shamans are
also found in many industrial societies. For example, Korea has a long history of shaman-
ism. At one time, shamans, who were mostly men, had considerable political influence.
Over time, this influence waned, and eventually, shamans were persecuted and driven
underground. Shamanism moved from being a prominent public institution to being a
more private, secretive activity. Today, most Korean shamans are women. This provides
many women with a good source of income and also gives them some degree of influence
over the community. Shamanism is now becoming recognized as an important part of tra-
ditional Korean culture, and many aspects of shamanistic ritual, such as songs and dances,
are also being publicly performed for entertainment.
In Korea, shamans are chosen by the spirits. Women who have experienced some type
of psychological stress in their lives are especially vulnerable. In their search for someone
to possess, society believes that the spirits tend toward individuals whose maŭm, or soul,
has already been fractured and, therefore, been made more vulnerable. The sign that a
particular woman has been selected is manifested as possession illness or sinbyŏng. There
are many physical and psychological symptoms of sinbyŏng, but the most significant is an
entrance into a trance state. The individual is ill with possession sickness until she accepts
the call of the spirits. Many individuals chosen by the spirits are very reluctant to become

Korean shamans are called to perform shamanic rituals for a number of reasons, including to
guide the dead to the “otherworld.” During this ritual, the shaman takes the role of the deceased,
who is then able to communicate with the family. Shamanic rituals are also performed to cure
illnesses, for divination, and to ensure the good fortune of the family and community.
Today, Korean shamanism is rapidly growing and expanding in urban areas. Some esti-
mates are as high as 100,000 practicing shamans, approximately five times as many as
were estimated in the 1950s.7
Pentecostal healers as shamans
The distinction between shamans and priests is not always clear, and in some Western
religious traditions, practitioners usually thought of as being priests may exhibit many
shamanistic traits. One example is Pentecostal faith healers (see Box 6.1 for another exam-
ple), who make direct contact with and channel the Holy Spirit from themselves to another
person during faith healing. Altered states of consciousness are achieved through rhythmic
speech and music. Participants in the ritual are often moved to shout, dance, run up and
down the aisle, or cry. The healing ritual, known as “laying on of hands,” generally takes
place during a formal service. The person in need of healing stands in front of the preacher,
who is standing in front of the altar. Others stand behind the person receiving the prayer
in case he or she needs physical support. The preacher anoints the person’s head with oil
and recites incantations. The preacher may also rub the head, torso, back, or legs of the
person. The whole congregation may participate by clapping in unison during the healing.
The Holy Spirit is believed to possess both the healer and the patient, which can lead either
one of them to convulse suddenly or begin speaking in tongues.

Box 6.1 Clown doctors as shamans
Although Western medicine is undeniably effective in treating many diseases, it
has been criticized for focusing only on the physical disease and neglecting the
patient’s illness experience (see Chapter 11). In an attempt to address this issue,
many complementary therapies have been introduced. One example is the use
of clowns in pediatric hospitals. A study by Linda Miller Van Blerkom of the Big
Apple Circus Clown Care Unit (CCU), which entertains children in New York
City hospitals, suggests that these clowns have many similarities with traditional

The clowns dress in outlandish costumes that include a white doctor’s coat and a

Van Blerkom points out that both clowns and shamans mediate between order
and chaos, sacred and profane, natural and supernatural, and that clowns with heal-
ing functions are known from other cultures, like the sacred clowns of the Hopis
and Zunis. She notes several similarities between shamans and clown doctors. For
example, both wear unusual costumes, and both are viewed with some ambivalence.
The use of puppets by clown doctors is reminiscent of shamanic spirit helpers, and
both use music and sleight of hand. Traditional shamans and clowns use suggestion
and manipulation of cultural symbols and pay more attention to the patient’s experi-

The clowns say they parody doctors and play with hospital equipment to lighten

Neoshamanism
The last several decades have seen a growing interest in shamanism, primarily in the
United States and Europe. Much of this can be traced to the publication of historian Mir-
cea Eliade’s book Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy in 1951.8 This book was
one of the first to look at common elements of shamanism cross-culturally and to focus
on shamanism as a technique for achieving an altered state of consciousness. The interest
in shamanism, however, really took off starting in the 1970s. Many cultural themes in the
United States helped contribute to this, including the legacy of the 1960s drug culture,
an interest in non-Western religions, environmentalism, the New Age, self-help, and self-
realization movements. Popular anthropology works also contributed largely through the
work of Carlos Castañeda and Michael Harner.
While a graduate student at UCLA, Carlos Castañeda (1925–1998) claimed to be
apprenticed to a Yaqui (Arizona) shaman named Don Juan Matus. Based on his experi-
ences, he wrote a series of books beginning with The Teachings of Don Juan: A Yaqui Way
of Knowledge, published in 1968.9 The earliest books focused on the use of mind-altering
drugs to experience another reality. Later, he moved his focus to a new technique of body
movements, or magical passes, that he called tensegrity, a term borrowed from architect
R. Buckminster Fuller, who coined the term as a combination of “tensional” and “integ-
rity.” The aim of tensegrity is to increase awareness of the energy fields that, according to
Castañeda, humans are made of. This is done through body movements and breathing,
which Castañeda said were taught to him by Don Juan and go back to ancient Mexico.
Castañeda’s work has been criticized by many academics as fictional, not true ethnog-
raphy. At best, the accounts are compiled from various beliefs and ideas surrounding
shamanic cultures. To date, no one has been able to verify the existence of Don Juan, and
experts in Yaqui culture have cast doubts on the accuracy of his accounts, among other
issues. Although Castañeda passed away in the late 1990s, workshops in tensegrity con-
tinue to be offered, and his books remain popular.
Michael Harner (1929–2018) was a cultural anthropologist who spent many years stud-
ying shamanism, primarily in the Americas among the Jívaro and Shipibo Conibo cultures
of the Upper Amazon. Through extensive ethnographic study, Harner found certain recur-
ring shamanic concepts and practices across cultures. Together, these common features of
shamanism form a specific methodology called core shamanism that Harner felt could be
taught and practiced usefully outside of their original culture-specific contexts. In 1980,
Harner published The Way of the Shaman as an introduction for those in the West inter-
ested in pursuing shamanism10 and established The Foundation for Shamanic Studies (FSS)
that offers training workshops in core shamanism:
Since the West overwhelmingly lost its shamanic knowledge centuries ago due to religious
oppression, the Foundation’s programs in core shamanism are particularly intended for
Westerners to reacquire access to their rightful spiritual heritage through quality work-
shops and training courses. Training in core shamanism includes teaching students to
alter their consciousness through classic shamanic non-drug techniques such as sonic
driving, especially in the form of repetitive drumming, so that they can discover their
own hidden spiritual resources, transform their lives, and learn how to help others.11

Although the Foundation focuses on teaching core shamanism, it also has a mission to

Although Harner’s original work with the Jívaros centered on the use of the hallucinogen
ayahuasca, core shamanism focuses on drumming to achieve altered states of conscious-
ness. In a typical workshop, the participants are told to lie down and relax with their eyes
closed or covered. A leader then directs them through an exercise in guided imagery. The
participants are told to begin their journey by entering the earth at a place well known to
them in the physical world, such as a lake, a cave, or even the root system of a tree (for
the Lowerworld) or a staircase, rooftop, or cloud (for the Upperworld). They then travel
to the other realms where they meet and interact with spirit teachers and power animals,
seeking to learn something useful, seek something lost, or carry out healing.
Those who use techniques like these proposed by Castañeda and Harner are known as
neoshamans to distinguish them from traditional Indigenous shamans. There are significant
differences between the two. Neoshamanism is focused on an individual, often as a self-
help means of improving one’s life, while traditional shamanism is focused on helping the
community. Neoshamans choose to participate and focus on what they consider to be the
positive aspects of shamanism (healing and encouragement), while Indigenous shamanism
often includes a “darker” side where spirits can be harnessed for social harm against any-
one or anything threatening an individual or community. The Indigenous shamanic trance
is often described as a terrifying experience, and Indigenous shamans may be marginalized
and feared because of their ability to do great evil. A neoshamanic trance is usually accom-
plished through drumming, singing, dancing, or breathing exercises and meditation.
Neoshamanism has also been criticized for presenting shamanic beliefs and practices
out of their cultural context through such concepts as core shamanism. A single practi-
tioner may choose bits and pieces from many different cultures. Harsher criticisms accuse
neoshamanism of cultural imperialism, neocolonial attitudes, and perpetuating racist ste-
reotypes of indigenous people. Harner and others are seen as profiting from the commodi-
fication of Indigenous identity, beliefs, and practices. Although some Indigenous healers
do encourage neoshamanism and are willing to teach non-Indigenous individuals, this is a
very contentious issue, one that speaks to the larger issue of who controls cultural symbols
and rituals. Despite these challenges, however, shamanism is a growing practice world-
wide. In the 2021 census of England/Wales, on the question of religion, 8,000 individuals
identified themselves as practitioners of shamanism, a tenfold increase since 2011, when
only 650 individuals identified themselves as such.12

deal with the reinforcement of the belief system and the established ethical code. Priestly
rituals legitimize a wide range of community ventures—for example, the coronation of the
British monarch by the Archbishop of Canterbury—and, on a more personal level, estab-

Priests also are meant to personify the image of the ideal person. They are expected to be
models of ethics and morality in their communities, and they are held to higher standards
of behavior than the population at large. When a priest fails to live up to these standards,
the significance is much greater than when another person fails in the same way.
Priestly rituals usually take place in a space that is set aside for ceremonial activities, a place
considered to be sacred space. It may be a community space as well, such as an outdoor area
or a structure, and the structure may be large enough that the entire community can enter
and participate in the rituals. However, in many societies, the ceremonial structure—a shrine
or a temple—is where sacred objects are kept and into which only a priest may enter.
