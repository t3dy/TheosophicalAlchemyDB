# The Anthropology of Religion, Magic, and Witchcraft

## Section: Conclusion

Conclusion
Fear of the existence of supernaturally evil individuals appears to be universal. And who
should we fear most as potential evildoers? People who stand out, people with whom
we have existing conflicts, and the vulnerable in any society. If my neighbor is jealous of
my success, might they not want to bring me down and cause me harm? Of course, they
might well think the same of me. The witch is often the enemy within—the member of
the community who rejects, subverts, and betrays or is simply vulnerable or marginal. As
with many religious phenomena, witchcraft accusations are closely tied to other social
phenomena—in this case, reflecting existing tensions and fears. In reality, the most com-
mon way to become a witch is to be accused of being one.

298 Witchcraft, sorcery, and wizardry
Witchcraft is a fascinating subject in the realm of religious beliefs and behaviors. Study-
ing this phenomenon is made more complicated by the different ways the term has been
used in small-scale, European, and modern Neo-Pagan communities. Our continuing
interest in the subjects of magic and witchcraft can be seen in popular media representa-
tions such as the television shows Bewitched, Charmed, Sabrina the Teenage Witch, and
Buffy the Vampire Slayer and, of course, the extremely popular Harry Potter book series.
However, these fantasy representations differ significantly from the real phenomena. First,
these sources show magic and witchcraft as very similar phenomena, if not one and the
same. Second, witches are almost always portrayed in a very positive light, which does not
fit every other case. In small-scale societies, practicing witchcraft is, by definition, antiso-
cial behavior. Even Wiccans would argue with many of the representations of the powers
of witches, which are shown as being far beyond those that are actually claimed.
The rise of the Neo-Pagan religions and their redefinition of witchcraft are but one
example of how religions rise and fall and change over time. This topic is one that we will
explore in greater detail in the next chapters.
Summary
The idea of witchcraft as an evil force bringing misfortune to members of a community
is found in a great number of societies throughout the world. Unlike sorcerers, who per-
form magic rituals and manipulations primarily to achieve negative ends, witches may
intentionally or involuntarily will death and destruction—when this happens, it is believed
to be caused by a supernatural power that lies within the body of the witch. The earliest
concept of witchcraft in small-scale societies is largely based on the work of E.E. Evans-
Pritchard among Azandes of the Sudan.
Because witchcraft and sorcery are sometimes ambiguous and can be difficult to distin-
guish, some scholars use the term wizardry to refer to these practices. In their study of
wizardry, Alice and Irvin Child point out seven social attributes of wizardry, including
social control, redirecting aggression within the group, segmentation of the community,
in-group versus out-group, maintaining social structure, restoring social equilibrium, and
supporting the worldview.
Ideas of witchcraft in Europe were influenced by Christian ideas about the nature of
evil. Christianity accepts the existence of an evil spirit known as Satan or the Devil. In this
belief system, witches are individuals whose evil power originates with a pact with the
Devil. In Europe, witchcraft beliefs were merged with sorcery. Sorcery became associated
with the invocation of spirits defined as hostile to God. Anyone doing any form of magic
was seen as calling on the servants of Satan. Magic and witchcraft became not just crimes
against society but heresy—crimes against God.
The period known as the Witchcraze began at the end of the Middle Ages (around 1450)
and lasted for about 200 years. In Euro-American witchcraft beliefs, witches define all that
is wrong and immoral. People who exhibit antisocial behavior are the most likely targets
of witchcraft accusations. Witches also fulfill our unconscious need to blame someone for
the misfortunes that we experience in our daily lives. In general, patterns of witchcraft
accusations reflect deeply felt conflicts and divisions in a society.

Magic, medicine, and religion
The central theme of this chapter is that it can be difficult to separate religion, magic, and
medicine. All three come together in the therapeutic setting as individuals and families
make decisions regarding the course of action to take when confronting illness and death.
Medical beliefs are commonly subsumed under the larger category of religious beliefs in
a culture. Any ethnography with a chapter pertaining to religion includes at least sev-
eral paragraphs describing medical beliefs and magic. As humans, we are practitioners of
magic, a subset of religion. Think about this the next time you go to the doctor’s office for
any medical issue. Do you feel ripped off if you do not get some sort of treatment, or even
an injection or a prescription? Most of us do not have the medical training to understand
the pharmacological action of drugs, but we have the feeling that they are doing something
to help us. Perhaps the efficacy of belief is one of the most powerful components of any
medical system; our mindset frames our experiences.
Western biomedicine has explanatory models related to disease (a biological pathology
or condition), whereas explanatory models of illness (a person’s experience of ill health)
are found among individuals and groups who are not biomedical practitioners. Patients
seek treatment in one or more of three overlapping, interconnected sectors of healthcare—
personal, folk, and professional. Explanatory models in the personal sector vary by indi-
vidual, and treatment often intersects with the folk and professional sectors. Folk models
found in ancient civilizations, small-scale, traditional societies, contemporary folk beliefs,
and some alternative medical practices include both personalistic medical models, where
disease is considered the result of human or supernatural behaviors, and naturalistic medi-
cal models, where disease is associated with the imbalance of natural forces in the body.
The professional sector is dominated by the biomedical model.
Although biomedicine is the foundation of medical beliefs and practices in the U.S. and
other Western-influenced countries, modern medicine only came of age within the last
one hundred years. For those who might think that the medical beliefs and practices of
small-scale societies are inferior to biomedicine, it is important to remember that until the
mid-twentieth century, many Westerners sought treatments based on Medieval medicine.
Vestiges of earlier medical models are present today in the personal and folk sectors and
some of the symbols of biomedicine.

Disease/illness dichotomy
Arthur Kleinman, Professor of Medical Anthropology at Harvard University, explains
explanatory models through the disease/illness dichotomy. Disease is the model of West-
ern medicine (biomedicine) that deals with the medical malfunctioning of the body and
disease organisms. Conversely, illness pertains to culturally constructed models in which
individuals interpret sickness through the logic of their culture.
Objectively, the primary concern of Western practitioners is to analyze how the symp-
toms came about in order to treat them properly, and there is little concern for the patient
as a member of a cultural group. Hospital patients lose their personal identity, which is

replaced by labels describing their symptoms. For example, “the colostomy patient in
room 302,” or “the C-section baby in the nursery.” Western physicians are trained to
combat disease; some even specialize in the maladies that struck down family members,
for instance, a neurologist whose mother’s suicide influenced his career choice. Doctors
are dedicated to bringing about cures for diseases, but little emphasis may be placed on
patients besides the nature of their afflictions.
Most societies around the world see illness as a cultural phenomenon in which sick people
are separated from their families because of the illness episode. Healers—shamans in tradi-
tional societies—determine why the malady occurred so they can restore the patient back to
their family and community. Perhaps the best way to distinguish between disease and illness
is that disease is a biomedical phenomenon, whereas perceptions of illness encompass all
other beliefs (including folk and alternative medicines in the U.S.). Since diseases are largely
invisible, people in small-scale societies typically blame other individuals (witches, sorcerers)
or the supernatural for their plight. Perhaps they offended another person or violated a tabu.
As in biomedicine, diagnosis, determining the cause of the illness, is necessary for proper
prognosis, the expected outcomes based on the prescribed course of treatment. In tribal socie-
ties, magic involves healing, so a good part of the prognosis is to counter shooting magic (or
contagious magic, see Chapter 7) caused by sorcerers, ghosts, or malevolent beings.
Healers in the three sectors of healthcare
The term healer is often used to refer to a priest or shaman, especially when the individual
is focused on curing illness or injury. However, more specialized healers also exist. Many
activities of healers are similar to those of U.S. medical practitioners. For example, they
may set bones, treat sprains with cold, or administer drugs made from native plants and
other materials.
Following Kleinman’s model, physician Cecil G. Helman observes that, in any complex
society, there are three overlapping, interconnected health care sectors: (1) popular, (2) folk,
and (3) professional. Explanatory models exist in each sector. “Each sector has its own ways
of explaining and treating ill health, defining who is the healer and who is the patient, and
specifying how healer and patient should interact in their therapeutic encounter”:2
1. The popular sector is represented by the lay, non-professional, non-specialist part of soci-
ety. Importantly, this is the sector where ill health is first recognized and then defined
before health care is sought in the other two sectors. Therapeutic options do not include
payment, and the family is the main health arena in this sector. The primary providers are
women, especially mothers and grandmothers. In Western and non-Western societies, 70
to 90 percent of health care takes place within this sector. Advice may also come from
disease-experienced individuals, life-experienced individuals, paramedical professionals,
doctors’ spouses, organizers of self-help groups, or members of churches or cults.
2. The folk sector is found mostly in non-industrialized societies. Practitioners use variations
of sacred or secular healing techniques not typically recognized by the professional sector.
Personalistic medical models are found in the folk sector (see further on). Healers include
secular and technical “bone-setters, midwives, tooth extractors or herbalists, to spiritual
healers, clairvoyants and shamans.”3 Folk healers form a heterogenous group and all

Personalistic and naturalistic medical systems
In their seminal textbook, Medical Anthropology (1978), Barbara Anderson and co-author
George M. Foster propose a dual division of identifying disease among non-Western peo-
ples: personalistic and naturalistic medical systems. Unlike the allopathic system of profes-
sional biomedicine that separates the body from the mind and spirit (Cartesian split), the
personalistic and naturalistic medical systems tend towards seeing human health and med-
icine as holistic, involving body, mind, spirit, and emotions. These medical approaches are
commonly at the intersection of religion and healing, mingled.

Personalistic Medical Systems—in a personalistic medical system, the disease is believed
to be purposefully caused by a sensate agent (as perceived by the senses). The agent typi-
cally is a deity, a nonhuman being, such as a ghost, ancestor, malevolent spirit, or a human
witch or sorcerer. Personalistic systems are also called supernatural models. In personalistic
medical systems, sorcery, witchcraft, and spirit possession are usually considered causes
of illness. For example, in Latin American folk medicine, a supernatural class of disease
is caused by mal de ojo, “evil eye,” or susto, “fright.” In mal de ojo, a person is cursed
through a gaze or stare by someone who intends them harm. Often, the victim is unaware
of being a target until they develop symptoms of sickness that can include fatigue, nausea,
and bad luck. Susto is believed to be caused by soul loss, provoked by anything that results
in a sharp, extreme fear response—such as seeing a ghost, being in a car accident, or having
a terrible fall. In the body, it manifests as anxiety, restlessness, and fatigue. Both of these
personalistic diseases must be treated in culturally-appropriate ways (Figure 11.3).8

Personalistic medicine in small-scale societies
Personalistic medical systems are found mostly in Indigenous North and South America,
sub-Saharan Africa, Oceania, tribal Asia, complex societies in West Africa, and among the
Mayas. They were also documented among the Aztecs and Incas. Causality in personal-
istic systems is typically attributed to humans or supernatural beings, such as sorcerers,
witches, supreme gods, lesser gods, evil spirits, or ghosts. Biomedical practitioners might
ascribe scientific causes to how someone becomes ill, but in small-scale societies, explana-
tory models of why someone gets sick are more important. Questions might involve: Who
did I offend? Did I break a tabu? Did I fail to leave offerings at ancestral shrines? Is some-
one jealous of me? Finding out the cause is critical, especially if shooting magic (or conta-
gious magic, see Chapter 7) caused by sorcerers, ghosts, or malevolent beings needs to be
neutralized by counter magic and treatment. Some cross-cultural examples of causation
and treatment follow (see Table 11.1). Note that remnants of these same ancient medical
beliefs are present in both complex and small-scale societies today.

Shamanistic techniques
Since explanatory models of contagious magic predominate in personalistic medical systems
in small-scale societies, shamans are characteristically involved in healing. Commonly, the
shaman would attempt to remove foreign agents or substances that have entered the victim’s
body. Shamans employ the sucking technique, using the horn of an animal—buffalo, cow, deer,
caribou—or their bare mouths to suck out the “poison” from the abdomen of their patients.

Beyond the magical feats of shamans, it is important to recognize that part of the heal-
ing process occurs on a spiritual level. Since shamans experience out-of-body experiences
through trances to achieve an alternate reality, they have the ability to communicate with
the spirit world to bring back information to help their patients. On a spiritual level, belief
is a key factor. If people strongly believe in the powers of the shaman and that the shaman
has the ability to heal them, then that is half of the battle. Efficacy of belief is important in
any medical system, including Western biomedicine. Attitude or mindset is a major com-
ponent of wellness, so placing faith in one’s practitioner is critical. The fact that shamans
have spiritual connections makes it even better because they can invoke supernatural assis-
tance to augment the actual medicines and techniques they employ.
In Western society, we pray for those who are sick and suffering, so if the efficacy of belief
is not real, then why should we waste our time praying? If we think tribal peoples utilizing
shamans are foolish because they depend on magic, are we any different? In double-blind
drug studies, the efficacy of belief is demonstrated by the placebo effect, where members
of a control group and an experimental group receive either the real drug to be tested or
the placebo (fake medicine, like a sugar pill). It is not unusual for one-half of the group
receiving the placebo to achieve the desired effect of the real drug. Mind over matter can
be a powerful force. In his book Spirit Talkers. North American Indian Medicine Pow-
ers (2012), William S. Lyon maintains that the observer effect best explains the efficacy
of shamanic healing techniques, whereas the collective mindset of wellness among family
members attending a healing ceremony adds to the effectiveness of the shaman’s powers.

Cholera in a Chinese village
Chinese-American Francis Hsu (1909–1999) was conducting ethnographic fieldwork in a
Chinese village in 1942 when a cholera epidemic broke out. There were different opinions
among the villagers to explain the cause of the outbreak. Most people blamed the epidemic
on angry spirits that needed to be placated with prayers and charms. Some, however, allowed
themselves to be given anti-cholera shots (reflecting Western cultural values in our version
of magic—science), whereas others adhered to local prohibitions to ward off the epidemic.
Traditional beliefs maintained that they could cure the disease by not eating certain foods
typically washed in water (cholera is a bacillus that can live suspended in water for 30 days
without a host) and by drinking magical fairy water, which probably was not contaminated
with the bacillus.15 Ironically, the Western belief in the cholera vaccine is equally founded
in magic because biomedicine has now determined that cholera shots are largely ineffective.

Novocain magic
Paul Fejos, M.D. (1897–1963), a physician, filmmaker, and ethnologist who traveled
through the Philippines, Indonesia, New Guinea, and other countries making ethnographic
films in 1937–1938, began working for the Viking Fund (now the Wenner-Gren Founda-
tion for Anthropological Research) in 1941. Fejos, fascinated with the interface between
magic and medicine, believed that explaining maladies to natives in terms they could
understand was much simpler than explaining microbiology to them, especially because
non-Western peoples do not always acknowledge the world of microorganisms. He also
believed that until non-literate societies become literate and can appreciate the differences
between Western medicine and their own world of diffuse cultural symbols, it is best to
qualify medical diagnoses and prognoses to them in terms they can understand.
Fejos wrote about his friend Unchi, a shaman he met while visiting one of the Pacific
islands. During his visit, Fejos administered Novocain while performing dental procedures
on some of the islanders. Unchi always accompanied him and carefully watched Fejos’
procedures. Upon leaving the island, Fejos decided to leave Unchi with a farewell gift.
Thinking Unchi wanted his shotgun, which he admired, Fejos was somewhat taken aback
when the shaman asked for his syringe kit and Novocain. Unchi did not understand the
pharmacological action of Novocain when administered with a syringe, but he appreci-
ated the importance of the anesthetic given Fejos’ ritual care of his equipment. Although
Fejos knew his equipment would be safe in Unchi’s hands, he had a commitment to his
profession not to give the potentially lethal doses of Novocain to him. Fejos suggested
that although non-Western peoples do not necessarily adhere to the biological rational of
Western medicine, they usually appreciate the benefits (magic) of modern medicine.

medical systems by adapting traditional explanatory models to new medicines and pro-
cedures. (Antibiotics and penicillin were the earliest proselytizing agents.) For instance,
Norma Wolff, who worked with the Yorubas of Nigeria, noted how some villages might
have several different clinics, one of which might be represented by Western medicine.

Plant spirits and medicines in personalistic medical systems
William Howells (1908–2005), a biological anthropologist, notes that many remedies for
magically induced illnesses are based on the properties of certain plants or animals. Sig-
nificantly, the “rarer or more extraordinary they are the more effective they are likely
to be.” In Africa, different parts of a lion are important, and in Medieval Europe, cures
for headaches required powers made from unicorn horns, which, in reality, are Narwhal
tusks. Prescriptions for longevity included mummy dust imported from the Canary Islands,
where the Guanches had placed their mummified family members in caves. After Spain
conquered the Canaries, mummy dust became a major export. “Dragon” bones made
from fossils, by the way, are still popular in China.17
Indigenous belief systems are steeped in animism, the belief in spirit beings or the pres-
ence of an inner soul in all things. While conducting fieldwork among Ojibwes of Mani-
toba in the 1930s, Irving Hallowell observed that Ojibwes recognize personhood in certain
weather patterns, plants, rocks, and human-manufactured items. Ojibwe grammar distin-
guishes between persons and objects. Hence rocks used for sweatlodges, tobacco, trees, and
plants are other-than-human-persons, powerful persons with benefits; tobacco smoke trans-
ports prayers to the spirit world, and many plants possess medicinal properties. Linguistic
metaphors and chromatic art in Mesoamerica and the Southwest reference Flower World, a
timeless paradise or spiritual realm of the dead. Plants obviously play an important role in
people’s lives, especially since so many medicines used today are derived from them.

qualities were independently conceived but were synthesized by the time of Hippocrates

refer to these treatments as puke, purge, and bleed.23
The historical diffusion of humoral pathology from Greece began with the famous
Greek physician Galen (ca. 130–200 CE), who lived and worked in Rome. Galen tran-
scribed notes and anecdotes from Hippocrates’ students and published them as the Hippo-
cratic Corpus. Galen’s writings influenced medical practices in the Byzantine civilization,
so humoral pathology spread to Constantinople and then to Asia Minor, where it was
adopted by Eastern Christians and Muslims. Through conquest, the Moors brought the
system to Spain, Italy, and the north coast of Africa. Humoral pathology dominated Medi-
eval Christian medicine. It then traveled to the Western Hemisphere with the Spanish
conquistadors, where it blended with the medical beliefs of the Mayas and Aztecs. (Some
scholars believe the hot/cold dichotomy already existed in Native belief systems.) The
refined model lost the concepts of moist and dry yet retained the hot/cold dichotomy,
which sounds logical if humoral pathology is blended with Native explanatory models.24
Despite cultural rebirth in Renaissance Europe and advancements in the arts and sciences,
humoral pathology and its practitioners—including quacks, astrologers, and bloodletters—
flourished.25 Beyond established medicine and alchemy—the attempt to turn base metals
into gold, the precursor of chemistry—lay people depended on common magic carried over
from Medieval times. Magical words or incantations were chanted during times of sickness,
family problems, or crop failure. Power objects (crafted charms and fetishes) or amulets
(natural objects like stones, plants, or animals) were carried or worn to provide protection
from evil. Practitioners of folk, or popular, magic known as cunning folk, were sought for
their skills in herbal medicine, midwifery, and assistance in helping people with a wide range
of problems. They performed imitative magic based on the Law of Sympathy, apotropaic
practices to ward off evil, and divination rites like augury (reading the actions of nonhuman
agents) and cleromancy (casting stones or bones) in their practices (see Chapter 7). Cunning
folk, especially women, were believed to have familiars or spirit helpers, usually cats. Their
skills as herbalists and alleged ties to deadly and hallucinogenic plants like mandrake, bel-
ladonna, and henbane made them prime targets for witchcraft accusations beginning in the
fifteenth century. Despite persecution by Church and state, folk medicine persisted.26

Humoral pathology in the North American colonies
Colonial medicine in North America paralleled European medical beliefs and practices and
was dominated by religious doctrine. For instance, New England theologian Cotton Mather
(1663–1728) perceived that sickness was caused by a wrathful God despite his experiments
with hybridization and inoculation. Even elite colonial physicians perceived barbering as the
best medicine, and bleeding was the major form of surgery. During the late seventeenth-century
witchcraze in New England, doctors helped put witches to death. Large-scale quackery and
polypharmacy (patients administered multiple prescriptions) resulted in numerous deaths,
which prompted Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790) to warn the public about dispensaries.
During the Revolutionary War, the Continental Army was plagued by poor hygiene and
widespread gangrene. Opium and rum were used as painkillers for amputations, and the
stumps were cauterized and bandaged. Following the war, Benjamin Rush (1745–1813),
the leading American physician interested in psychological disorders, was known more for
his teachings than his practice. He blamed the 1793 Philadelphia yellow fever epidemic on
the harsh fumes blowing over the city from public dumps (miasma theory). Others blamed
the epidemic on foreigners (xenophobia). Over 4,000 people died, and many survivors
abandoned the nation’s capital. Advice from the College of Physicians was to avoid ill
people and to avoid the evening air. Rush suggested chewing garlic.
In 1847, the American Medical Association was established, then incorporated in 1897.

The smallpox blanket myth
In Chapter 3, we asserted that assumptions about Indigenous cultures are often based on
unproven collective beliefs, the things that “everybody knows are true” but are based on
stereotypes or lack of evidence.32 For instance, there were many unwarranted claims that
COVID-19 vaccines are untrustworthy because they were developed too quickly or that
they believe they have unwanted side effects: infertility in women, altered DNA, and even
swollen testicles. Despite the anti-vaccination movement, however, many people trust the
science (even though we do not always understand how it works) of the new vaccines and
have been immunized—some multiple times.
Scholars and other professionals are not immune to making false assumptions without
the due diligence of good research. For example, some claim that Inuits have hundreds
of words for snow because the harsh Arctic environment produces innumerable forms
of snow and ice. Weather forecasters on local TV stations are the biggest culprits for
spreading this falsehood. Few, however, know that Franz Boas identified four Inuit snow
terms in a 1911 publication: aput, “snow on the ground”; qana, “falling snow”; piqsir-
poq, “drifting snow”; qimuqsuq, “snowdrift.”33 Perhaps your local weather reporter,
too, will mention the erroneous hundreds of “Eskimo” snow terms during the next big
snowstorm!34
A more egregious stereotype, though, is the smallpox blanket myth, a time-worn meme
about soldiers and settler-colonists deliberately plying American Indian communities with
scab-laden blankets. Before its eradication in the late 1970s, smallpox was mostly trans-
mitted face-to-face through the inhalation of airborne variola droplets and sometimes
through contact with objects infected by body fluids. After exposure, a ten to 14-day
non-contagious incubation period led to the onset of symptoms—fever, malaise, vomiting,
body aches, back pain—for two to four days, followed by lesions on the tongue, mouth,
and throat. By this time, the victim was highly contagious. Oral lesions spread from the
mouth to the entire body and were densest on the face, arms, and legs. Delirium and coma
during this stage led to mortality in 30 to 40 percent of smallpox victims. For those who
survived, their pustules formed scabs that fell off within ten days; at this point, they were
no longer contagious and were immune to further outbreaks.

weapons like anthrax spread through the news media. About that time, the smallpox
blanket myth gained new life. Since then, authors, artists, theater performers, public
speakers, and even scholars have perpetuated the myth that settler-colonists purposely

Negotiating medical models in clinical settings
Medical anthropologist Thomas M. Johnson once said, “Biomedicine is the best medical
system in the world, but the problems are with the culture of medicine.”37 In other words,
many practitioners are unaware that patients’ behaviors are culturally conditioned. The
inability to recognize cultural and religious behaviors in clinical settings often leads to
misunderstandings and ineffective treatment. Hence, the importance of anthropologists
working in clinical settings.
Arthur Kleinman pioneered clinical anthropology by establishing mechanisms in which
anthropologists and physicians can collaborate. Today, some cultural anthropologists spe-
cialize in medical anthropology and consult in hospital settings. Where possible, they
teach physicians and medical students that culture is the blueprint for behavior and that
it is important to understand culturally constructed behavior in the clinical setting. As
patient advocates, anthropologists stress that patient needs are best served through cultur-
ally sensitive health care.
Explanatory models, the disease/illness dichotomy, and the importance of negotiation in
physician-patient relationships are taught in some medical schools today. Anthropologists
and physicians are both socialized into their professions, though doctors confront life-
and-death situations. They have a unique socialization process, know their way around
hospitals, and expect those who work with them, especially medical anthropologists, to
have the same knowledge. Doctors have shared backgrounds, and they have a restricted
education the more they become specialized. In terms of communication, when doctors
are in their specialized roles, it is sometimes difficult to communicate with those outside

Anthropologists collaborating with doctors must be communicators, mediators, and

An American Indian patient in Parkland Hospital was deemed problematic by the
medical staff following her detox from acute and chronic alcoholism. Her uncooper-
ativeness, unconventional non-verbal behavior and seemingly bizarre religious beliefs
confounded the Western-trained psychiatrists and psychologists. For instance, when
they introduced themselves to her, they noted that her handshake was limp and that she
did not make direct eye contact. Moreover, she cried a lot and fought with the nurses.
To make matters worse, Indian friends brought her a Bible, an eagle feather, and a bag
of dirt, which she was seen eating. The doctor in charge thought she had incurred irrepa-
rable psychomotor damage from years of alcohol abuse and prepared to send her to the
state mental hospital.
During grand rounds by the Consultation Liaison Psychiatric Unit, her case was discussed
by psychologists, an American Indian psychiatrist, a medical anthropologist, and several
urban Indians. The latter informed the doctors that her culturally prescribed behavior was
perfectly normal within the parameters of her culture. Next, everyone visited the patient
in her room and learned that she grew up in a distant reservation community where she
had little contact with non-Indians until she attended a boarding school, where she had
been abused. This explained her uncooperativeness with medical staff in an institutional-
ized setting. According to the implicit and tacit rules of her culture, maintaining direct
eye contact with an adult of the opposite sex could be construed as sexual promiscu-
ity. Moreover, shaking hands is not part of her cultural baggage. In the West, a person’s
character is judged by firm, strong handshakes and directness of eye contact; people with
limp handshakes and an aversion to direct eye contact are considered untrustworthy. In
traditional American Indian cultures, the accumulation of different medicine powers is
normal, so Bibles and eagle feathers are not conflicting symbols. She said that eating the
dirt allowed her to connect with her traditional homeland and that her incessant crying
stemmed from the guilt she felt for not living up to the expectations of her tribal elders,
who did not approve of her drinking. Once her version of reality was compared to that of
the doctors, both sides understood one another a little better.
At the conclusion of the bedside meeting, it was agreed that one of the Indians in attend-
ance, a shaman, would personally take her to a Native-run rehabilitation center following
her discharge from Parkland. Despite differences in explanatory models, the negotiation of
medical models benefited the patient and prevented her from being warehoused in a state
mental hospital.

In the previous sections, we examined the interplay of spiritual belief and healing in the
personalistic and naturalistic (humoral) medical systems. Yet the allopathic biomedical

Across cultures, in hospital and clinical settings, it is also common to find small chapels,
meditation gardens, and labyrinths. Not only are these religious places39 architecturally

these resources are sometimes critically important to the medical specialists as well.
Spiritual architecture in the biomedical environment
Increasingly today, we find designated sacred places within clinical settings. These may be
secluded, open, or even for multiple uses, but they are often also marked as public places, areas
where patients, families, and others can access a spiritual experience. Why might religious expe-
riences be included in biomedical settings where scientific approaches are used for healing?
Architect Julio Bermúdez studies the cognitive-aesthetic effects of sacred architecture
on human biology (referred to as neurophenomenology). In his research of 30 practicing
Roman Catholics who visited two very different places—Union Train Station and the Basil-

found that sacred places produce distinct biometric effects in those he refers to as “believ-

skin conductance and found “gamma activity increased across the brain. There was also
higher activation of the frontal lobe, indicating intense focus and a disruption of default
thinking.”40 Sacred places produce altered states. We have long known this emotionally
and psychologically, which is why chapels are commonly associated with hospitals, but
research such as Bermúdez’s suggests that biology may play an even more powerful role.
Chapels are commonly found in hospital settings. If the hospital is religiously affiliated,
the chapel may be specifically designed to align with a designated religious tradition, while
in hospitals not religiously affiliated, the chapel will usually be less symbolically defined.

open to anyone for prayer, meditation, and solace, no matter what their religious roots or
affiliations might or might not be. Occasionally, clinic or hospital chapels are even used

Religious specialists in biomedical environments
We think of medical staff as doctors, nurses, aides, and therapists, but religious specialists
are also often part of many medical communities. Religiously-affiliated medical facilities are
ancient, some of the earliest ones established during the Middle Ages, and affiliated with Islam
(called dar al-shifa or bimaristans) and Catholicism. The medical arts, as they were called,
were highly developed in the Islamic world, specifically in Egypt and Persia (modern-day
Iraq and Iran), primarily based on passages in the Qur’an in which the Prophet Mohammed
referred to health as a divine blessing and called for healthcare as a basic right for all people.
Today, in biomedical religiously-affiliated medical facilities, religious leaders themselves may
be part of the medical staff (e.g., pastors, priests, rabbis, etc.), but most often, we find a group
referred to as chaplains, individuals who are certified by a specific religious group to provide

spiritual guidance and support in secular institutions. We find chaplains in the armed services,
educational institutions, correctional facilities, nursing homes, mental health facilities, and
hospitals. In these settings, including hospitals, the chaplain will conduct religious services;
administer sacraments—including Last Rites; offer pastoral counseling on related issues, such
as healthcare decisions, grieving, faith, and compassion; serve as an intermediary between the
patient, family, and other medical staff; and pray on behalf of those requesting support.
For many individuals—patients, family, and friends—it is the chaplain who will get to
know them individually, hear their deepest worries and fears, and minister to them while
they are there. Because chaplains are part of the medical staff at hospitals, they tend to
be available far more readily than a personal religious leader who has responsibilities in
and outside the hospital setting. A group of chaplains is on duty at most U.S. hospitals 24
hours a day, and it is not unusual for a patient or family member to call a chaplain, who
usually conducts rounds just as do doctors and nurses, at any hour of the day or night.

of the context. Whether in a person’s home, in a church, or even especially in a hospital,
faith and belief are cornerstones to healing and health.
