Priests also may receive a call to their religious role, sometimes through dreams, visions,
or trances. In some societies, a person becomes a priest after being cured of an illness;
being cured may be taken as a sign of divine favor. In other societies, the reason for enter-
ing the priesthood might be more practical. In the Europe of centuries past, one of the only
ways a middle-class man could get an education was by joining the priesthood. Research
and teaching would be important components of his responsibilities as a priest. It was also
the custom in some agricultural societies that the oldest son inherited the land, the middle
son entered the military, and the youngest son entered the priesthood.
The training of a priest usually involves memorization of vast amounts of knowledge that is
held within the religious institution itself. In complex, food-producing societies, the very sur-
vival of the community might depend on the priest’s competence in the performance of tradi-
tional rituals. Although a priest may connect with the supernatural through visions and trances,
this ability is not as important as the priest’s ability to memorize and perform rituals in the
proper manner. For example, ancient Maya priests could predict lunar eclipses, understood the
sidereal revolution of Venus around the sun (225 days) and the synodic cycles of Venus (584
days), and could predict when it would appear as Morning Star and Evening Star, as depicted
in the Dresden Codex, one of the Maya divinatory books that survived the Spanish Inquisition.
They also knew that the Pleiades rose in the west in May/June, bringing the summer rains and
the planting season. Maya priests predicted astronomical events to control power.
Zuni priests
Zunis, a pueblo people of the American Southwest culture area, have religious practices
that involve a complex hierarchy of priests, which forms the basis of Zuni religious and
political organization.
According to anthropologist Ruth Bunzel, there are six esoteric cults in Zuni Pueblo
with limited memberships. Each is connected to a special priesthood, participating as
masked dancers in two six-month cycles marked by the summer and winter solstices.
Summer rituals pertain to abundant rainfall and harvests, whereas winter rituals relate to
war, medicine, and fertility. Rituals occur throughout the year, beginning with the Shalako
Ceremony, which is held before December 21, the Zuni New Year.13
1. The Cult of the Sun is associated with Sun Father, who is offered sacred cornmeal offer-
ings at sunrise. The Pekwin priest, the holiest man in Zuni, derives his power from Sun
Father. He installs priests and officiates over ceremonies in which the priests of other
cults participate.
2. Cult of the Rainmakers—the spirits who inhabit all waters—consists of twelve priest-
hoods, each represented by two to six members based on clan affiliation. Rain priests
are expected to be peaceful men unconcerned with affairs of the outside world. They do
not perform public ceremonies but hold secret rituals during the rainy season between
late July and September to bring the much-needed rain.

3. All Zuni males belong to the Cult of the Katsinam (Katsinas), which is divided into

4. Associated with the Katsinam Society, the Cult of the Katsina Priests inhabit the spirit
Katsina Village, Kolhu/wala:wa, though they are connected with fecundity, not rain.
Each priest has a distinctive personality and a mask inherited from the previous genera-
tion in the family line. Pauitwa, the chief of Kolhu/wala:wa, represents the three Zuni
virtues—dignity, beauty, and kindliness.
5. Two Bow Priests, a society chief and a battle chief, lead the Cult of the War Gods,
which deals with outside threats. In Indigenous times, men who killed and scalped an
enemy were compelled to join the Cult of the War Gods and perform the Scalp Dance
to protect themselves from the enemy’s ghost. Bow Priests are war leaders, protectors
of the village, and persecutors of witches.
6. The Cult of the Beast Gods consists of the prey animals—and the patrons for twelve
medicine societies whose members are general practitioners, though each society heals
as a group and has a specialty—treating bullet wounds, epilepsy, or sore throats. Once
healed by a society, one is compelled to become a member. For instance, a person
healed by the Galaxy Society is initiated into the society as a lifelong member (women
also belong to medicine societies). Over time, society members learn to perform eso-
teric feats, such as walking over hot coals or stick swallowing. Fetishes, special altars,
and ritual paraphernalia represent each medicine society, which performs secret rituals
that distinguish it from other societies. Public rituals are conducted during the fall and
winter.
Okinawan priestesses
Okinawa, located southwest of the main islands of Japan, was once the independent king-
dom of Ryukyus. Although the language, culture, and religious beliefs are very similar to
those of other areas in Japan, there are considerable differences as well. Okinawa’s Indige-
nous religion is based on animism and shamanism but has been heavily influenced by Shinto,
Buddhism, and Taoism entering from Japan and China. Like Shinto, Okinawan religion sees
the world as inhabited by a myriad of supernatural beings referred to as kami. By placating
and pleasing the kami through rituals, misfortune can be avoided, and blessings gained.
There are a variety of religious specialists, all of whom are women (Figure 6.6). Okinawa is
the only known society in which women lead a mainstream, official, publicly-funded religion
practiced by both sexes. The two main specialists are priestesses, kaminchu, and shaman-
like practitioners called yuta. The yuta mediate between the villagers and the supernatural
by communicating with the ancestors and the kami. They practice divination as well as heal-
ing rituals. Whereas each yuta has one or more kami she communicates with regularly, the
kaminchu is believed to actually embody a particular kami associated with the clan.

Kaminchu rituals are funded by the village council or clan and take place at the village
prayer house in the main square, clan houses, or sacred groves. On these occasions, the
priestesses wear elaborate five-piece white outfits and crowns of leaves. This is the con-
ventional clothing for the kami as well. The significance of the kaminchu is simply their
presence; they actually do very little in a ritual. They sit in designated places on specific
days and receive food offerings from the villagers. They do not preach, perform rites of
passage, or heal—they sit and eat. However, like the kami themselves, they are believed to
emit good spiritual energy. The villagers also like to have the kaminchu around at events
such as housewarmings and agricultural festivals.
The role of kaminchu is semi hereditary. Each clan has a certain number of kaminchu
positions that can only be filled by female members of the clan. Within some clans, certain
positions can only be filled by women of certain families. The role of chief kaminchu for
a village is traditionally passed from mother to daughter.
Susan Sered compared the stories told by kaminchu (priestesses) and yuta (shamans) of
how they attained their positions.14 Both types of religious practitioners told of illnesses,
but Sered found significant differences between the two. The kaminchu reported minor ill-
nesses that generally involved some sort of bleeding or other bodily rupture. The Okinawan

Communities such as those on Mt. Athos are called intentional communities. These are
groups of people—in the case of Mt. Athos, adult men—who voluntarily choose to share
in a more focused, committed life that is set apart or separated in some way from a wider
society. Intentional communities may be formed of individuals practicing sustainable agri-
culture, such as ecovillages or off-grid living, but many intentional communities are estab-
lished by common religious core values.
Other specialists
While shamans and priests are well-studied types of religious specialists, there are other
specialists who practice in limited ways. The labels that are used to refer to these special-
ists often overlap extensively and are not used in any consistent manner. Some of their
functions overlap with those of shamans and priests.
A diviner is someone who practices divination, a series of techniques and activities used
to obtain normally unknowable information, including future happenings, present occur-
rences in the future at distant locations, and phenomena that touch the supernatural, such
as the identification of witches. Some divination techniques involve the interpretation of
natural phenomena, like cloud formations, or some activity, such as the turning over of
cards. Other techniques involve the diviner entering an altered state of consciousness and
obtaining the requested information while in that state.
Diviners usually focus on very practical questions: What is a good time to plant my
crop? Will my investment pay off? Whom should I marry? What is an auspicious day for
a marriage? A very important type of information that diviners provide is the cause of ill-
ness. The diviner often provides the diagnosis, and the healer provides the cure. Diviners
usually, but not always, work for private clients and are paid for their services. (Diviners
and healers are discussed in Chapters 7 and 11.)
A prophet is a mouthpiece of the gods. It is the role of a prophet to communicate the
words and will of the gods to his or her community and to act as an intermediary between
the gods and the people. Although shamans may occasionally function as prophets, in
many cases, the role of prophet is a distinct one. Prophets are found in a wide variety of
cultures and include the familiar examples of Moses and Mohammad.
Early written records from southwest Asia indicate that people believed that gods con-
trolled the world and sometimes made their intentions known to select human interme-
diaries, prophets who interpreted signs or delivered messages from the gods so that the
people could better conduct their affairs. Prophets in ancient Mesopotamian and Mediter-
ranean cultures received messages from the gods through divination and oracles. Diviners
employed a variety of techniques, like the casting of lots or examining animal entrails

The late seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Enlightenment brought about empiricism,
a new criterion of knowledge based on observation and critical reasoning, which caused
a decline in the importance of prophets as “mediators of revealed truth.” Scientists and
philosophers challenged the concept of the “truth of revelation” as the debate over the
“conceptions of God, religious truth, and divine mediation” became restricted to the intel-
lectual community. Post-Enlightenment Europe was marked by the decline of the author-
ity of the church, while America underwent the separation of church and state.18
The declining power of the church and its inability to enforce dogmas contributed to
the rise of Enlightenment thinkers who could now express their religious views publicly.
New prophets emerged who founded religious sects based on new canons. For instance,
Joseph Smith (1804–1844) received revelations from Jesus Christ and archangel Moroni,
who gifted him with golden plates containing the Book of Mormon. Mary Baker Eddy
(1821–1910) inspired the Christian Science movement through her writings, and today’s
Pentecostal and charismatic movements feature ecstatics “who consider themselves capa-
ble of receiving the spirit and speaking as divine agents.”19
In tribal societies, prophets are clan leaders or others who employ a variety of divina-
tion and visionary techniques to obtain special knowledge from divine beings. Typically,
the prophet petitions the spirits for special favors. Anthropologists who study prophecy in
non-Western societies have dispelled some of the myths about prophets:
[T]he widespread opinion that ancient prophets were individualists crying to deaf ears
from the loneliness of the desert now seems to be a romantic ideal. Rather, groups tend
to support prophets who express their interests, while prophets acting entirely on their
own rarely find a significant audience.20
Handsome Lake was a prophet of the Seneca tribe (Northeast culture area) during the
time when the reservation system was first imposed. In 1799, Handsome Lake became
ill and appeared to have died. His body was prepared for burial, but he revived. He
said that he had had a vision of three messengers who had revealed to him God’s will
and told him that he was to carry this message back to his people. Later the same
year, he received a second revelation in which he was shown heaven and hell and
was given moral instructions, which were very similar to Christian ideas. Handsome
Lake received further revelations in subsequent years. On the basis of his visions, he
preached a revitalization of traditional seasonal ceremonies, a strengthening of the
family, and a prohibition against alcohol. Handsome Lake’s teachings continued to
spread after his death in 1815 and ultimately became the foundation for the Long-
house religion.
The Nuers, a cattle-herding people living in the Eastern Sudan culture area, have a his-
tory of prophets. One example is Ngundeng, a nineteenth-century Nuer prophet. He was
born in the late 1830s. While there are no historical records of his life, there are many sto-
ries, especially those told by his family. According to tradition, he was conceived after his
mother was past menopause and was born after a 12-month pregnancy. Ngundeng is said
to have spoken to his mother at birth and to possess a number of physical characteristics

attributed to divine influence. Unlike some prophets who make contact with supernatural
powers later on in life, Ngundeng was born with that power.
As a young man, he had seizures or altered states of consciousness. He also showed very
strange behaviors, such as wandering alone in the bush, fasting, and drinking nothing but
water for long periods of time. As he began to eat tobacco, mud, grass, and human feces,
he became very thin, and his hair grew long and matted. Then, he was possessed by a god,
who revealed himself to be Deng. Ngundeng, Deng’s prophet, began to make prophecies
and developed a reputation as a peacemaker.
Nuers believe that prophets are chosen by a god and are then able to predict the future,
cure the sick, ensure the fertility of women and cows, influence the growth of a good crop,
and more. Stories began to circulate about Ngundeng’s ability to take a life through words
or even thoughts. The ability to kill was associated with the ability to control life. The god
Deng was a life-giver, controlling rain and the procreation of cattle and children, but he
was also a god of death. Ngundeng gained a wide reputation for making barren women
fertile and halting epidemic disease. He died in 1906.
Over 100 years later, the prophet Ngundeng played an important role in the movement
for an independent state in southern Sudan. Ngundeng had a dang, or ceremonial rod,
which was inherited by Ngundeng’s son. When the son was killed in battle in the resistance
against British colonial rule, the dang was taken as a souvenir to England. Years later, the
dang was sold at auction to a British scholar, Douglas Johnson, who had studied Nuer
culture. Douglas returned it to the Nuers in 2009. The rod had become a symbol of the
Nuers’ drive for independence from Sudan.
Kiowa owl prophets
Plains Indian shamans performed divinatory rites designed to find lost people, animals, and
objects or to predict the future, like the Lakota Yuwipi or Algonquin Spirit Lodge rites.
Similar rites practiced by Kiowas involved shamans called owl prophets. Kiowa eschato-
logical beliefs associate owls with the spirits of the deceased, the spirits of shamans, or the
transformed souls of evil sorcerers. Owls, like ghosts, are avoided. Contrary to the Kiowa
majority, nineteenth-century owl prophets obtained their powers directly from this dreaded
bird. Every owl prophet understood owl language, communicated with the dead using owls
as mediums, and consulted his “all knowing spirit” to discern future happenings.
Analogous to the Algonquin Spirit Lodge rite, owl prophets held séances in their tipis
to locate missing persons or lost objects, or to prophesy the future; divination rituals
attempted to divulge the whereabouts of men presumed dead or separated from war parties
or to pinpoint the location of missing horses. When an owl prophet was consulted, a spe-
cial tipi was erected before sundown, and then a smaller tipi, one and a half feet in height,
was placed inside the larger tipi on the west side for the owl spirit during the séance. Only
the owl prophet and his clients were permitted inside the tipi during the divinatory rite.
Anyone else permitted in the area was required to prostrate himself on the ground outside
the tipi, fearful of seeing the owl spirit, incurring its wrath, and risk having dirt kicked in
his eyes. Each ceremony began with drumming and low singing from inside the tipi, and
then suddenly the owl spirit was heard alighting on top of the tipi poles, causing the tipi
to shake. Shortly afterward, the spirit spoke, identifying the soul of the deceased person
