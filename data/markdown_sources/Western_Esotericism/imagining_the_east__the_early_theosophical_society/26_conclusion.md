# Imagining the East: The Early Theosophical Society

## Section: Conclusion

Conclusion
There is, then, strong textual evidence to suggest that Gandhi’s notion of Hinduism,
his specific view of Christianity, and his general belief that all religions refer to the
same truth were shaped by esotericism. The crucial task, however, is putting these
findings into perspective. It needs to be acknowledged that esotericism played an
important role in the global religious history of the nineteenth and early twentieth
centuries, and the current academic paradigm of a purely “Western Esotericism”
requires revision. For Indians like Gandhi, esotericism was an entry point into
Orientalist discourse and, at the same time, it provided opportunities for a critical
resignification of its contents, which in turn impacted on Orientalist notions.
A global history approach has been shown to provide a practical methodology
for focusing on three closely interrelated points. First, the impact of esotericism,
as part of Orientalist discourse, has been established. Second, close attention was
given to Gandhi’s concrete appropriation of esotericism, and the question as to
the influence of Indian traditions was also addressed. Third, it was possible to ask
how Gandhi came to inform religious views in Europe and North America with his
appropriated notions. The concepts in play are all understood as products of global

Afterword
Tim Rudbøg and Erik Reenberg Sand
The intention of this afterword is to provide a concise summation of the perspectives
that have resulted from this book, including a few thoughts on future research. It
has long been known to scholars of religion and related areas that the Theosophical
Society was, from an early stage, involved with a number of Asian traditions, such
as Buddhism and Hinduism. For example, in his attempt to analyze the nature of
Theosophical perspectives, Merwin-M arie Snell already wrote in 1895 that
Theosophy, while not identical with any of the various so-c alled secret traditions
of Buddhism, is in fact based upon, Yogâ’câra and Tântrika (Kâla ‘Cakra) schools of
Mahâyâna; although, as I have already intimated, it is so much more Hindunized.1
Over the years this connection between Theosophy and Asian traditions has al-
most become a given fact, but it has also habitually been coupled with the view
that Theosophy represents a distortion of Hindu and Buddhist concepts and
perspectives, which for a long time hindered more thorough research on the topic.2
However, in the wake of the growing scholarly attention to Western esotericism
and the critical study of Orientalism, Theosophy and its connection with Asia has
for the past twenty years gained renewed attention; nonetheless this present volume
is among the first academic initiatives, in the form of an anthology, to offer detailed
historical research on Theosophy and South Asia. It does of course not cover the
full scope of the subject and has furthermore been set to cover a specific timeframe,
primarily during the lifetime of first- generation Theosophists such as Helena
P. Blavatsky and Henry S. Olcott— only opening up to a few areas that go beyond
this frame. But the themes covered in the three parts of this volume include most of
the central areas relating to how the Theosophical Society approached the so- called
East (part 1); how the Theosophical Society represented the East (part 2); and how
the Theosophical Society interacted with the East, culturally and politically (part 3).
Part 1
Christopher Partridge and Donald Lopez’s chapters provide important and useful
perspectives for an understanding of the complexity of Theosophical Orientalism,
that is, the Theosophical approach. On the one hand Partridge demonstrates that

[Page 351]

while the Theosophical Society represents an affirmative and enthusiastic approach
to South Asia grounded in romanticism, the Theosophical Society still largely un-
derstood Asia through its own pre- established lenses of an esoteric wisdom, which
de facto represents a form of Orientalism. On the other hand Lopez shows how
prominent Theosophists, such as Henry Steel Olcott and Alfred Percy Sinnett,
emphasized respect for the original traditions and the importance of gaining know-
ledge about them via first- hand knowledge rather than through textual study at a
distance. The Western background of the Theosophical Society coupled with a gen-
uine interest in learning about and traveling to India and Ceylon in order to revive
the traditions, which they believed to be some of the most profound in the world,
permeate the Theosophical understanding and reception of South Asian ideas and
the social and political activities of the society. In this way the Theosophical Society
represents a distinct link in the cross-c ultural interchanges of the nineteenth cen-
tury that continued through the twentieth century. Furthermore, new research and
historical details have emerged from the individual chapters in the second and third
part of the book dealing with the various facets of the Theosophical approaches.
Part 2
James Santucci’s chapter represents the first detailed study of Blavatsky’s know-
ledge, use, and conception of Sanskrit, and shows that Sanskrit was regarded as im-
portant and central to understanding the ancient esoteric wisdom. Tim Rudbøg’s
chapter on the early debates in the reception of Buddhism shows how the un-
derstanding of Theosophists such as Blavatsky and Sinnett of esoteric Buddhism
represents an early reception of Mahāyāna Buddhism that was quite distinct from
that of the major Orientalists, who largely focused on early Buddhism, yet at the
same time this Theosophical reception of Buddhism became a part of the general
debates among scholars at the time. Rudbøg and Erik Sand continue the study of
reception history with an analysis of Blavatsky’s reception of South Asian traditions
in the chapter on Blavatsky’s early reception of Hindu philosophy, which, for the
first time since Snell’s comment, systematically shows Blavatsky’s understanding
and use of the six Hindu schools of philosophy prior to The Secret Doctrine. Joscelyn
Godwin, furthermore, systematically outlines the major facets to date of the study
of the Mahatmas and discusses how the notion of mahatmas— namely, masters—
was developed. Patrick Bowen shows how Theosophy became one of the earliest
contexts for the reception of yoga in the West, and Gillian McCann demonstrates
how the early Theosophical periodicals played a major role in the distribution of
an awareness of South Asian concepts and ideas. Finally, David Weir analyses how
Asian themes developed in Theosophy directly inspired major modernist writers
such as William Butler Yeats, Gertrude Stein, T. S. Eliot, and James Joyce and were
shared by other intellectuals at the time such as Nietzsche.

Read successively, these individual chapters collecting in-d epth research on
the role that the early Theosophical Society played in absorbing and promoting
Sanskrit, Buddhism, Hindu philosophy, ideas about masters, and yoga clearly dem-

the influence this distinct reception had at the time— ranging from literature to

Part 3
But the story does not end here, since this volume also shows how the Theosophical
Society actively participated in the Bengal Renaissance and the Hindu reform
movement represented by the Arya Samaj. K. Paul Johnson and Sand, in their indi-
vidual chapters dedicated to each of these areas, show with new historical detail and
vividness how cooperation, misunderstanding, misrepresentation, opposition, and
agreement were deemed to emerge in a number of ways when people with different
expectations met for the first time. In connection with the direct Theosophical en-
gagement with South Asia, the following two chapters equally show how the ide-
ology of universal brotherhood became key to Theosophical developments in India
and Ceylon and how early Theosophists, especially Allan Octavian Hume, were
important to the founding of the Indian National Congress. These two chapters
by Rudbøg and Isaac Lubelsky thus each provide some of the background for un-
derstanding the Theosophical initiative in India, and this background is continued
by Mark Bevir, who demonstrates in the following chapter how Theosophical ide-
ology mobilized cultural nationalism and the home rule movement. In direct re-
lation to the theme of Theosophical involvement with South Asia both politically
and culturally, the third part of this volume ends with Michael Bergunder’s seminal
study of Mahatma Gandhi’s theosophically inspired conceptions of Hinduism and
Christianity in terms of a global history perspective.
Final Remarks
This volume as a whole collects research that clearly and historically places the
Theosophical Society in broader debates relating to Orientalism, cross- cultural
dynamics, and globalization. It shows how Theosophy imagined and approached
South Asia in a distinct way based on Western esoteric notions of an ancient wisdom
coupled with Enlightenment ideals and an emphasis on living, first- hand experi-
ence. This Theosophical perspective became intertwined with the early Western re-
ception of yoga, Buddhism, and Hinduism and also stretched beyond the cultural
realm into the realm of social activism and politics, through which it became di-
rectly interconnected with India’s independence.

Covering this extensive variety of topics, it is our hope that this volume will serve
as a constructive step toward an understanding of the many ways the so-c alled East
has been imagined and of the intellectual, cultural, and political implications that
this has produced. It is furthermore hoped that it stands clear that the Theosophical
Society holds a noteworthy historical role in the emergence of a globalized world, in
which otherwise more distinct Western and Asian cultures have now become aware
of each other, changed, and merged.
This volume has to the greatest extent focused on India, but it is our hope that it
will cultivate further study of Theosophical entanglement with East Asia, such as
China and Japan, in the future. We also hope sufficient historical groundwork has
been done to further the study of Theosophy and the imagined East as it unfolded in
the twentieth century. Finally, we hope and anticipate that the much-n eeded global
history perspective will be brought more strongly into the study of Theosophy and
thereby help provide a more nuanced perspective on how Theosophy has been
perceived and applied around the world from the nineteenth century and onward.
