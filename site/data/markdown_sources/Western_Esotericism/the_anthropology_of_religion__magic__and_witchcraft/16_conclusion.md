# The Anthropology of Religion, Magic, and Witchcraft

## Section: Conclusion

Conclusion
Symbols are often based on specific episodes that are recounted in myths, and they repre-
sent a specific worldview. An example we discussed was the Tewa practice of beginning
dances by emerging from a kiva to symbolize the emergence from under the earth at the
beginning of the world.
Bloodletting was an important part of Maya religious rituals. The reason for this is
found in the origin story as told in a document known as the Popul Vuh, a seventeenth-
century book that recounts the history of the Mayas. In the Popul Vuh, we learn that the
gods created people to have a kind of living creature who would worship them. As they
prepared to create human beings, the gods said, “So now let’s try to make a giver of praise,
giver of respect, provider, nurturer.”22
This reflects the worldview of the Mayas that a reciprocal relationship exists between
the Maya people and their gods. The world was brought about by a sacrifice by the
gods, but it will continue to exist only if the Mayas, in turn, sacrifice for the gods. The
smoke produced by burning cloths soaked with blood provides nourishment to the
gods.
In a similar way, the symbolism of the Christian cross is based on the story of the cru-
cifixion of Jesus found in the Christian Bible. The symbol of the cross also expresses the
Christian worldview—that the death of Jesus provides the opportunity for salvation:
For God so loved the world that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth
in him should not perish, but have everlasting life.
(John 3:16)

Summary
A symbol is something that stands for something else. Symbols enable us to talk about
things that are not immediately in front of us and to create our own realities. They are
important elements in religious practice and religious rituals center on the manipulation
of symbols.
Symbols can appear in many forms. Language is symbolic in nature because various
speech sounds are used to create combinations that have meaning. Yet nothing of the
referent is inherent in the sound or character combination—its meaning is part of a learned
cultural tradition. Recitation of religious narratives is an important feature of religious
practices. Religious ideas also can be expressed in art that may contain many symbolic ele-
ments. Much artistic representation is arbitrary in that the nature of the symbol does not
always communicate its meaning. Some examples are the swastika, the pentagram, and
the cross. Although an outsider might have difficulty understanding a piece of religious
art, a member of that culture would have no such difficulty.

Time and space are also treated symbolically. Many units of time, such as our week
and the cycles of Maya time, are arbitrary and provide time with deep meaning. Calendar
systems exist as a way of symbolically organizing time and for scheduling periodic rituals.
Space is also full of symbolic meaning, especially when mythological events are seen as
occurring on the landscape. Such a sacred landscape is a part of the totemic system found
among the Australian Aborigines.
Music and dance are important symbolic elements in religious rituals. Musical elements
suggest emotions and have symbolic meanings. What is considered religious music in one
community might not be in another. Dance, composed of music, movement, costume,
masks, props, and sets, is often used to tell religious stories in societies where such stories
are not written down. All of these elements—language, art, music, and dance—interact to
provide rich religious experiences.
The human body is also a symbolic canvas and may include such things as tattooing,
cicatrization, tooth removal or marking, and head shape modification as ways to symbol-
ize and communicate an individual’s identity.

Chapter 3
Mythology
Myths are sacred narratives or stories of creation that provide the foundations for religious
beliefs and practices. Myths tell of the origins and history of the world and the creation of
the first human beings. They also prescribe the rules of proper conduct and articulate the
ethical and moral principles of society. Some myths exist as written texts, and others are
oral narratives. Myths also can be told or conveyed in all kinds of art and performance,
including music and dance. In this chapter, we will discuss the nature of myths and provide
examples from various religious traditions.
The nature of myths
As we learned in Chapter 1, all people use a cultural lens to impose meaning on their
world. Culture provides a body of knowledge that serves to interpret and explain reality.
However, people also experience things they cannot comprehend and cannot control. They
ponder the origin of their world. They seek to understand the interconnectedness between
humanity and the world around them, including the physical landscape, the plants and
animals that dwell in this landscape, and other human beings and societies. And they ques-
tion the existence and meaning of disaster, illness, and death.
Worldview
The way in which individuals in a society perceive and interpret their reality is known as
their worldview. Their worldview provides them with an understanding of how the world
works; it forms the template for thought and behavior and provides them with a basic
understanding of the origin and nature of humankind and their relationship to the world
around them.
To better understand the concept of worldview, we can compare the worldviews of
two cultures, Navajo and non-Indigenous Judeo-Christians. Navajos today are the
second-largest Native American group in the United States (Cherokees are the largest).
They occupy a large reservation in Arizona and New Mexico, although today, many
Navajos have left the reservation and live elsewhere.
To Navajos, humans are a part of nature. They see their world in terms of the relation-
ships and connections that bind the various elements that make up the world. All of these

Stories of the supernatural
People describe their world and express their worldview in stories and other creative
expressions such as art, drama, jokes, graffiti, folk music, and festivals. The following is a
well-known story that is European in origin and was first published in 1823, written down
from oral tradition. It has been told and retold countless times.1
The story of Snow White involves an evil queen who flies into a jealous rage when she
learns that someone in the kingdom is more beautiful than she. She keeps tabs on her
status in the beauty arena by using a talking mirror. This is a type of divination instru-
ment (see Chapter 7) used to gather information about things and events in ways that
are supernatural. (Talking mirrors do not exist in our empirical, natural world.)
Snow White is the evil queen’s stepdaughter. As Snow White matures, she eventually
becomes more beautiful than the queen, who sees Snow White as a threat that must be
eliminated. The queen orders Snow White to be killed, but the huntsman who is ordered
to do the killing takes pity on Snow White and lets her escape into the forest. The hunts-
man then kills a wild boar and presents its lungs and liver to the queen as being Snow
White’s. The evil queen cooks and eats the lungs and liver, thinking them to be those

heroes, and kings but also of local stories about buried treasure, ghosts, and saints. They
may or may not include supernatural elements and may or may not be considered sacred.
Legends you may be familiar with include the cities of Atlantis and El Dorado, the heroes
Robin Hood and King Arthur, George Washington chopping down the cherry tree, and
the story of the Holy Grail.

There are also Indigenous legends. Kiowa elders tell a humorous story about some men
who walked far south into Mexico until they reached the rainforests of Chiapas, where
they encountered a race of long-tailed people living in trees. When addressed in Kiowa,
the people scurried away through the treetops, chattering unintelligibly. Discouraged by
the rude people (howler monkeys), the travelers turned back towards home! This adven-

Legends are also commonplace in our contemporary world, although people seldom see
them as such. Known as urban legends, these stories are recounted as having really hap-
pened, primarily on the Internet or in tabloids. Some commonly circulated urban legends
are crocodiles living in the sewers of New York City and strangers giving out poisonous
candy at Halloween.
Myths and religion
Myths are different from folktales and legends. Myths are sacred stories of creation that
tell of the origin of the world and humankind, the existence and activities of gods and
spirits, the creation of order in the universe, and the nature of illness and death. Myths
relate the origins of human traditions and articulate a society’s values and norms. They
give instructions on how to behave and distinguish good from evil.
Myths also describe the beginning of the world after creation, including extraordinary
events that affected people’s ancestors. Myths describe tribal migrations and allude to
ancestral homelands and connections to sacred landscapes. For example, Zunis believe
they emerged from the Underworld through Ribbon Falls at the bottom of the Grand Can-
yon. Nimiipuu (Nez Perce) peoples believe they first came out from the Underworld at a
small promontory in Kamiah, Idaho, referred to as the “heart of the monster.” These are
Indigenous origin myths. Post-creation stories of clan migrations are connected to many of
the Ancestral Puebloan ruins dotting the Four Corners area, including Chaco Canyon and
Mesa Verde.2 Compared to legends, myths also tend to be much older historically.
Myths are thought by the people who tell them to recount real events that took place
in the remote past in a world different from the one we live in now. Myths, however, are
believed to be instructive and relevant to modern life, and are often recounted in religious
rituals. They contain enduring social lessons and truths. Lauri Honko writes:
The reenactment of a creative event, for example, the healing wrought by a god in the
beginning of time, is the common aim of myth and ritual. In this way the event is trans-
ferred to the present and its result, i.e., the healing of a sick person, can be achieved once
more here and now. In this way, too, the world order, which was created in the primeval
era and which is reflected in myths, preserves its value as an exemplar and model for the
people of today.3
There are alternative meanings of the word myth—any invented story, imaginary/fictitious
thing, unproven collective belief—that do not apply to our studies of religion. Unfortu-
nately, these definitions of the term myth are frequently used in our society in a negative
sense to mean stories that are false; that is not the sense in which we use the term here.

Changes do occur in written texts, but they are usually deliberate changes that are the con-

and a secular power. During this time, the Church was seen as the intermediary between

problem because the Church existed to interpret God’s word. Later, the Protestant Refor-
mation would emphasize the Bible, and not the Church, as the source for true Christianity.
Among the central beliefs of Martin Luther, who in 1517 began the Reformation, was that
laypeople should read the Bible for themselves. This meant that everyone needed to learn
to read and also that the Bible had to be translated into the local languages.
Perhaps one of the most famous translations of the Bible was that ordered by King James
I of England and published in 1611. The King James Version of the Bible is still widely
used today. Many Bible scholars, however, note what they consider to be inaccurate trans-
lations of certain words and passages, resulting from the knowledge and political atmos-
phere of the early seventeenth century.

are often written in a “religious” lexicon, using words and phrases not used in everyday
speech. Some societies even have distinctive dialects or languages that are reserved for the
recitation of religious narratives. For example, consider the excerpt from Psalm 8 that

Understanding myths
Myths exist in all human societies. They have been collected and analyzed by countless
anthropologists, folklorists, and other scholars. Many different theoretical orientations
are used as tools to study and explain myths. While myths show an astonishing degree of
variability, certainly common themes occur that have become the focus of scholarly study.
In this section, we will look at some approaches to the study of myths and survey some of
the common themes.
Approaches to the analysis of myths
The analysis of myths can be approached from many different perspectives. Are myths
literal or symbolic? If they are symbolic, how should these symbols be interpreted? How

[Page 110]
Mythology 83
are myths tied to the rest of culture? Do myths serve functions, and if so, which ones?
Do myths reflect the way that the human mind works, the way a specific culture works,
or both? Many different ways of analyzing religious narratives are based on the different
theoretical approaches to the study of religion discussed in Chapter 1.
Searching for myth origins in the nineteenth century
The nineteenth-century evolutionary school in anthropology saw a unilineal progression
from more “primitive” societies to more “civilized” ones. Accompanying this progression
was a similar proposed progression from magic to religion to science. Myths were seen as
belonging to the “primitive” period, with sacred myths being replaced by secular folktales
until finally dying out altogether in “civilized” societies. (It is important to remember that
anthropologists no longer believe in such an evolutionary progression or that cultures can
be classified as “primitive” or “civilized.”)
Theorists from the evolutionary school assumed that modern people living in small-
scale societies lived and thought the same way that earlier European societies had. They
compared myths found in many different cultures, looking for common elements from
which they could reconstruct an assumed “original form” of myth from which all others
had derived. It was believed that doing this could help explain puzzling aspects of modern
European society.
The work of British anthropologist Sir James George Frazer (1854–1941) is a good
example of this approach. He collected many examples of myths and magical practices
from around the world and published them as a thirteen-volume work entitled The Golden
Bough (1890). Although modern anthropologists criticize the information in The Golden
Bough as taken out of its cultural context, the book is still widely read. As an example of
the comparative approach, Frazer analyzed the story of the fall of man in Genesis by look-
ing at other origin-of-death myths cross-culturally:
The story of the Fall of Man in the third chapter of Genesis appears to be an abridged
version of this savage myth. Little is wanted to complete its resemblance to the similar
myths still told by savages in many parts of the world. The principal, almost the only,
omission is the silence of the narrator as to the eating of the fruit of the tree of life by the
serpent, and the consequent attainment of immortality by the reptile . . . If my interpre-
tation of the story is right, it has been left for the comparative method, after thousands
of years, to supply the blank in the ancient canvas, and to restore, in all their primitive
crudity, the gay barbaric colours which the skilful hand of the Hebrew artist had sof-
tened or effaced.10
Since Frazer’s time, further studies of myths have found that no single myth exists cross-
culturally, but characteristic versions of a story may be found in specific areas. For exam-
ple, in Africa, origin-of-death myths revolve around a failure to deliver a message, whereas
among Native Americans, the story centers on a debate on the subject of death.
Around the turn of the twentieth century, a new approach to the study of myth was pop-
ular. This approach argued that myth is derived from earlier ritual practices. Based on this

idea, theorists tried to reconstruct these rituals. Of course, this approach is not a complete
answer to the question of origins because if myth came from ritual, where did ritual come
from? This approach to myth, however, did encourage scholars to look beyond the text
for the connection that myths have to the cultures in which they are found. Most scholars
today acknowledge that myth and ritual are closely related and parallel expressions, as
opposed to believing that one is derived from the other.
Fieldwork and functional analysis
Early studies of myth and religion, such as those of Frazer, were undertaken by people
who read myths collected by missionaries, travelers, and others from the comfort of their
own libraries. This changed in the early twentieth century with a new emphasis on scien-
tific study, ethnographic fieldwork, and participant observation by anthropologists such
as Franz Boas and Bronislaw Malinowski.
The Father of American anthropology and an avid fieldworker, Franz Boas felt that
mythology could be read almost like an autobiography written by the culture itself.
Working with Indigenous informants and interpreters, Boas collected and transcribed
many Native myths. He used myths as sources of ethnographic data about kinship sys-
tems, housing types, division of labor, hunting techniques, and other cultural traits.11
Some of his students later challenged this approach; they felt that myths are more than
just a literal reflection of what goes on in a culture. For example, in her study of Zuni
mythology, Ruth Benedict noted that myths are often idealized descriptions of things that
do not happen in real life.12 However, Boas brought to the anthropological study of myth
the importance of phonetically transcribing full texts and of relating myths to the rest of
culture.
Like Boas, Malinowski favored a literal interpretation of myth. While neither anthro-
pologist at this time favored the idea that myths could be purely symbolic, they both
emphasized in-depth study of one culture at a time as opposed to comparing myths cross-
culturally. Malinowski was a founder of the functional approach in anthropology, previ-
ously discussed in Chapter 1, which turned from the nineteenth-century interest in the
origins of myth to a focus on how myths function in a culture. Malinowski wrote that
myths are seen as a force to help maintain the society:
Studied alive, myth, as we shall see, is not symbolic but a direct expression of its subject
matter; it is not an explanation in satisfaction of a scientific interest, but a narrative
resurrection of a primeval reality, told in satisfaction of deep religious wants, moral
cravings, social submissions, assertions, even practical requirements. Myth fulfills in
primitive cultures an indispensable function; it expresses, enhances, and codifies belief;
it safeguards and enforces morality; it vouches for the efficacy of ritual and contains
practical rules for the guidance of man. Myth is thus a vital ingredient of human civi-
lization; it is not an idle tale, but a hard-worked active force; it is not an intellec-
tual explanation or an artistic imagery, but a pragmatic charter of primitive faith and
wisdom.13

Malinowski conducted long-term fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands and described how
the Islanders themselves distinguish between folktales, legends, and myths. Myths are not
told for entertainment or to impart historical information but are used to justify and
explain religious rituals as well as social and moral rules. For example, before the Tro-
briand annual feast of the return of the dead, myths are told that explain why humans
die, why the spirits of the dead have to leave the village, and why they return once a year.
Trobriand Islanders also engage in extensive ceremonial trading (called kula); the rules for
such trade, the magic used to prepare canoes for the voyage, and even the geographical
routes taken are all related to specific mythology.
Critics of the functional approach point out that it focuses only on benefits, not institu-
tions or practices that may be oppressive or exploitative. Functionalism also focuses on
consensus building, not, for example, the potential conflict caused by competing versions
of a myth. Because of this emphasis on consensus and function, the approach does not
deal well with cultural change.
Structural analysis of myth
Structural analysis, as the name implies, focuses on the underlying structure of the myth.
This approach is based on the work of Claude Lévi-Strauss, who pointed out that humans
tend to think and categorize the world in common ways using terms of binary opposites,
such as black and white, nature and culture.14 Lévi-Strauss believed that the human brain,
as a biological organ, was the same across cultures, and so mythology, the study of myths,
was a window into universal forms of human cognition.
The division of the world into binary opposites can be seen cross-culturally in myths,
such as the story of Genesis.15 Examples of binary opposites in Genesis creation accounts
are light/dark, day/night, heaven/earth, man/animal, and man/woman. Binary opposi-
tions can be stated directly (both parts expressed in the myth) or implied (where one
part points to another). In the following portion of Genesis 1:4–6, the binary opposites
of light/dark as well as heaven/earth are clearly stated, while good/bad and divide/join
are implied:
And God saw the light, that it was good: and God divided the light from the darkness.
And God called the light Day, and the darkness he called Night. And the evening and
the morning were the first day.
Structural analysis focuses on the structure of religious narratives by analyzing the
content. It demonstrates that stories that seem very different on the surface may have
a similar underlying structure. We can also apply structural analysis to a story from
the Gururumbas of New Guinea (Melanesia culture area). The primary binary opposi-
tion in Gururumba culture is that of nature versus culture. We can see this opposition
being expressed in several ways in the Gururumba myth of the origin of women, told
in Box 3.3.

Psychological symbols in myth
Another approach to the analysis of myths interprets them as symbolic and sees this sym-
bolism as rooted in human psychology. This approach is based on the work of Sigmund
Freud and Carl Jung.
Freud described various psychological defense mechanisms, such as projection, in
which one’s own unconscious attribute is perceived and reacted to by another per-
son or thing.16 For example, Freud saw individual dreams as symbolically expressing
unconscious wishes and a similar process occurring with myths for groups. Myths are,
therefore, a type of “shared dream.” Freud also emphasized the importance of early
childhood experiences, such as the nature of the parent-child relationship. He then
proposed a relationship between early experiences and adult projection systems such
as myth. Although such processes as projection and symbolism were considered uni-
versal, the actual symbolic content would be expected to vary as childhood conditions
varied.
The narrative most associated with the psychoanalytic approach is the Greek story
of Oedipus, the man who unknowingly kills his father and marries his mother. Freud
argued that this story represents a deep psychological conflict experienced by all boys.
Because he considered this case reflective of universal developmental issues, he expected
similar stories to be found cross-culturally. In fact, Allen Johnson and Douglas Price-
Williams, in their research, have found Oedipus-type stories from cultures around the
world.17
Similar to Freud in its emphasis on human psychological processes is the work
of Carl Jung.18 In contrast to Freud, Jung felt that myths stemmed from something
beyond the individual unconscious. Just as individuals have an unconscious mind,
Jung believed that humans as a group share a collective unconscious, inborn elements
of the unconscious that are manifested in dreams and myths. The main characters
of these dreams and myths are termed archetypes. Oedipus is just one example of

Another example of an origin myth is the emergence myth, which is common through-
out North America. The initial acts of creation take place under the earth, which is often
organized into a series of layers. The lower layers are dark and cramped. As the story
progresses, it moves up from one world to the next. This movement is an evolutionary
progression as acts of creation occur, and knowledge is imparted to various creatures,
including humans. Finally, they emerge onto the surface of the earth.

built a fire, cutting at the Monster’s heart until he killed him. At death, the Monster’s
bodily orifices all opened and out flooded all the animals and the various Indigenous

Finally, the earth diver myth variant is a creation myth with Old World parallels in Europe
and Asia. It is commonly found in North America, except in the Southwest and Arctic. At
the beginning of time, a primordial body of water covers the land. Various creatures—tur-
tles, crawfish, ducks, muskrats—take turns descending to far depths to find the bottom.
Then, returning to the surface nearly drowned, this inauspicious creature produces the
small portion of mud from which solid land is formed. Oftentimes, a creator figure shapes
land from the mud. A motif found within the earth diver story is the occurrence of a world
flood.
In Mesoamerica, earth diver creation stories are well known in Maya cultures and in
the Central Highlands of Mexico. Creation occurred when the earth and mountains rose
from the primordial sea. In the Quiché Maya variant, the gods Tepeu and Gucumatz uti-
lized speech to create the mountains and earth that rose from the sea. This is the first of
multiple creations, a common Mesoamerican theme. A crocodile represents the primordial
earth, a “nascent landmass” floating on the sea. Aztecs perceived the crocodile’s back as
the “peaks and valleys” of the earth’s surface, and Mayas recognized the crocodile as the
surface of the earth and named a god Itzam Earth Crocodile.
Apocalyptic myths
Many myths found across cultures tell of the catastrophic destruction of the world, an
apocalypse. The destruction takes many forms, one of which is by flood. A disciple of
Freud once explained this as related to dreams that happen when a person has a full
bladder. An alternative explanation lies in the fact that floods are likely to be frequently
experienced, as people need to live near a water source. The Judeo-Christian flood myth
is the story of Noah’s ark, in which God sends the flood to rid the earth of the wickedness
of man.
In many societies, we see a myth cycle of creations and destructions, called apocalyptic
myths. The ancient Aztecs of Mexico tell of four worlds that existed prior to the present
world, the fifth world. The following story tells of the destruction of the fourth world:
During the era of the fourth sun, the Sun of Water, the people grew very wicked and
ignored the worship of the gods. The gods became angry and Tlaloc, the god of rains,
announced that he was going to destroy the world with a flood. However, Tlaloc was
fond of a devout couple, Tata and Nena, and he warned them of the flood. He instructed
them to hollow out a great log and take two ears of corn—one for each of them—and
eat nothing more.
So, Tata and Nena entered the tree trunk with the two ears of corn, and it began to
rain. When the rains subsided and Tata and Nena’s log landed on dry land, they were
so happy that they caught a fish and ate it, contrary to the orders of Tlaloc. It was only
after their stomachs were full that they remembered Tlaloc’s command.
Tlaloc then appeared to them and said, “This is how I am repaid for saving your
lives?” They were then changed into dogs. It was at this point, where even the most
righteous people were disobedient, that the gods destroyed the world, ushering in the
present era of the Fifth Sun.23

Entire religious systems may be based on a hero story. Buddhism is based on the story of
Prince Siddhartha Gautama. Gautama leads a very sheltered life in his father’s palace until

meditating. He learns all he can from various teachers, but nothing seems to appease his

the answers he has been seeking. He is attacked by Kama-Mara, the god of love and death,
but is victorious. During his time under the tree, Gautama gained knowledge and enlight-
enment and is thereafter referred to as the Buddha or the “Enlightened One.”
