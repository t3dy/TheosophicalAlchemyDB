The Liber de orbe,attributed to Masha'allah (fl. 762-ca. 815) in the list
of Gerard of Cremona's translations,' stands out as one of the few identi
fiable sources for the indirect knowledge of Peripatetic physics and cos
mology at the very time Aristotle's works on natural philosophy them
selves were translated into Latin, from the 1130s onward." This physics is
expounded in an opening series of chapters on the bodily constitution of
the universe, while the central section of the treatise covers astronomical
subjects, and the remaining parts deal with meteorology and the veg
etal realm. Assuming that Gerard of Cremona's translation of the Liber
de orbe corresponds to the twenty-seven chapter version that circulated
especially during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries," it was, how
ever, not this version, but a forty-chapter expansion thereof that became
influential as early as the 1140s. It may have originated in Spain, as

What role did the Liber de orbeplay in the putting together of De secre
lis philosophie? Undeniably, from the very prologue to its closing chap
ters, this cosmography was put to use massively. But the indebtedness
of De secretis philosophie to the Liber de orbe is not limited to its having
served as a source for additional material on given subjects. Indeed, the
impact it had involves a fundamental conceptual issue: it prompted the
adoption of the Peripatetic cosmological model. What this model had to
offer, beyond the then prevailing Platonic cosmological tradition, was a
new, strict division between the immutable celestial sphere and the cor
ruptible infralunar world. The Liber de orbethus inspired a detailed phys
ical exposition of the noblest part of the created world, the immutable
firmament. By contrast, the majority of medical works are centered on
the infralunar world and the interaction of the four elements.
As a consquence, one of the main questions De secrelis philosophie
raises is why its author gave preference to Peripatetic cosmology over
the four-element model that generally prevailed during the twelfth cen
tury both in cosmographical and medical works. He does not go into any
explicit justification for his having assimilated so completely the cosmo
logical model of the Liber de orbe. However, the fact that he adheres to
a medicine that takes into consideration celestial influences strongly sug
gests that he might have considered, in parallel to such sources as Galen's
On Critical Days, the inalterability of the Peripatetic celestial sphere to
offer a guarantee for the reliability of predictions relating to the infralu
nar sphere. The first chapter of the Liber de orbe, in which the immu
tability of the "natures" of heavenly bodies and the regularity of their
movements are addressed at some length, also helps put into perspective
De secrelis philosophie. In the Liber de orbe, these characteristics of the
heavenly sphere and bodies serve as a proof that the firmament was cre
ated and divinely governed, thus making possible a rational investigation
of the heavenly sphere. Foreknowledge with regard to the movements of
celestial bodies per se is grounded in their immutability and in the regu
larity of their movements. Corresponding predictions concern, above all,
the rising and setting of constellations, the precise moments and dura
tions of the apparently irregular courses of planets, and solar and lunar
