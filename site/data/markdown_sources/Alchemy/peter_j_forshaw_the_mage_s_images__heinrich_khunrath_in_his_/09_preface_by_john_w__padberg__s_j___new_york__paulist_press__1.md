Comparing the Amphitheatre’s 1595 figures to eyes, Szulakowska suggests
that their circularity ‘may signify that they are intended to be “mirrors”, evok-
ing a meditative state of mind,’ adducing Stephan Michelspacher’s discus-
sion of his own circular engravings in Cabala, Spiegel der Kunst und Natur in
Alchimia (Cabala, Mirror of Art and Nature in Alchemy, 1616) in support.842
Michelspacher’s Cabala, on whom Szulakowska believes Khunrath’s ‘alchem-
ical catoptrics’ exerted the most immediate influence, describes a sequence
of three visions of differently coloured mirrors, displaying, respectively, phil-
osophical Mercury and Salt, philosophical Sulphur, and a great ‘arcanum’.
Significant support for the suggestion that the Amphitheatre’s Circular Figure 4
of the Oratory-Laboratory is the summation and perfectly unified vision of the
previous three figures is found in the discovery that in Michelspacher’s text,
when his three mirrors are combined they produce a fourth in which he sees
God perfectly.843 Although Szulakowska is extremely vague concerning how
these ‘alchemical catoptrics’ were to be practised, the idea that the circular
engravings could be considered meditative mirrors finds some support in
Johann Valentin Andreae’s above-mentioned description of Circular Figure 1
as the ‘Speculum Legis’ (Mirror of the Law). The most convincing justification
for such speculation appears in a manuscript dated to the seventeenth century
in the British Library’s Sloane collection, titled Tabulae theosophicae cabbalis-
ticae (Cabbalistic Theosophical Tables), where we find large reproductions of
Circular Figures 1 and 2, conforming closely to the colours of the 1595 edition,
with their central images stencilled or gilded onto mirrors, clearly suggesting
that their owner intended to use them as objects of reflection similar to the
way they appear to be used in Figure 4.844

The bovine figure with mortar, pestle, scissors and bellows (bearing the
adage ‘Let the cobbler stick to his last’) on the far left of the Calumniators is
presumably one of the unkempt ‘Mal-Chemical savage fools’ (Argchymischen
wilden jecken) whom Khunrath dismisses in the engraving’s text as ‘bunglers
and daubers’ (Hüdlern und Südlern).869 As the mortar and pestle were also
used by apothecaries, it might also represent bungled preparation of medicine
from, for example, antimony.870 As well as carrying allusions to the gold-greedy
folly of Midas, his ass-like neighbour with bellows and axe likely represents one
of those empiric alchemists commonly dismissed as ‘puffers’, whom Khunrath
dismisses as course idiots lacking any understanding;871 the axe perhaps imply-
ing crude preparation of materials.872 In his Alchimistisch Watzenbaumlein
(1605), for example, Joachim Tanckius, professor of anatomy and surgery at the
University of Leipzig, writes in a similar way of the errors committed by ‘coarse
and crude Ass-heads’.873 The foppish hare-headed figure, perhaps a courtier, is
undoubtedly one of the ‘boorish hare-brained dimwits’.874 Georg Graman, like

52 Conclusion
The Amphitheatre engravings were for their time some of the most detailed
and sophisticated images to have ever appeared in Western occult literature,
manuscript or print, and contributed to the Baroque flowering of esoteric
emblematic discourse in the seventeenth century. It is true that alchemical
images, for example, had already appeared in print in the Pretiosa Margarita
Novella (New Pearl of Great Price, 1546) and the Rosarium Philosophorum
(Rose-Garden of the Philosophers, 1550), and illustrated books of emblems and
hieroglyphs, such as Andrea Alciato’s Emblematum liber (1531) and Horapollo’s
Hieroglyphica (1505) had been available for a century, as well as publications
