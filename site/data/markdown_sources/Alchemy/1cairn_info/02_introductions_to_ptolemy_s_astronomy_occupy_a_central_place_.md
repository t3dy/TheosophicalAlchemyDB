WILLIAM OF CONCHES’ADAPTATIONS FROM THE LIBER DE ORBE
Apart from the above-mentioned arguments relating to the characteristics of
zodiacal signs 111, William of Conches adapts two principal series of arguments
from the Liber de orbe :
1) The first series concerns the sphericity of the earth. Corresponding
arguments are taken from chapters seven and seventeen of the long version of the
Liber de orbe. 2) The second set, about the apparent retrogradation of Saturn, is
common to the short and the long version of the Liber de orbe (ch. 23/ch. 32).
The transcriptions of the relevant passages are based on Florence, Biblioteca
nazionale, MS Conv. Soppr. J. I. 132 (F). Readings of Paris, Bibliothèque natio-
nale de France, MS lat. 15015 (P), are preferred to those of F in the few cases of a
minor scribal error. This copy breaks off at chapter twenty-five.
On account of its difficult reading, the third manuscript, New York, Columbia
University Library, Plimpton MS 161 (N), was consulted punctually for the
chapters on the sphericity of the earth; concerning chapter thirty-two, on the
stations and retrogradations of Saturn, the readings of F have been checked
systematically against those of N.
The texts of the Dragmaticon philosophiae and the Liber de orbe are
reproduced in parallel columns and related to each other by underlined key words
or phrases. The lengthy arguments of the Liber de orbe have been abbreviated.
Letters referring to figures are written in bold capital letters for reasons of

The sphericity of the earth
Chapter seven of the long version of the Liber de orbe lists the following in
support of the earth’s sphericity :
1) Rainwater flows instead of forming one permanent expanse of water.
2) Traveling north and south from the center of the earth reveals stars that have
not previously been seen.
3) In different parts of the earth are seen different portions of the firmament.
4) The sun always illuminates the half of the earth that is opposite to it, while
its other side is always in the shadow. Everything that is said to be beneath the
earth is the earth itself or parts of it, and vice versa. But God’s creatures are all
above : the heads of people are always up and their feet down.
5) If the earth were a plane, the rising sun would seem smaller than when high
up and larger when nearer to the observer. But it has always the same size. To
make these proofs « more clearly understood » is being drawn a first figure.
6) Should people still persist in arguing in favor of changing sizes of the sun
and thus of the flatness of the earth, this is explained with reference to atmospheric
phenomena such as the denseness of mist (to which the Irish text adds the example
of the person submerged in water and of reading glasses).
7) A further argument, an argument ad absurdum against the belief of the
earth’s flatness, consist in inviting the listener, or reader, to imagine two cities. It
is part of the argument directed against those who believe that the earth is a level
plane :
– People in an eastern city would see the sun rise as a large body and would see
it setting in the west as a small body and vive versa.
– The first half of the day would seem shorter to people in an eastern city than
the latter half; the latter half would seem shorter to people in a western city than
the first half.
– To this is being added that the learned can prove that the day is of equal
length at equal distance from the middle point of the day at those two cities, and in
every place of the world. And in order to illustrate the argument is being drawn a
second figure, which consists of a straight line representing the surface of the earth
and a curved line above it, symbolizing the sun path. At the extremities are
located, respectively, the eastern and the western city.
8) As further proof that the sun does not set in the same place in regard to any
two cities are given differing positions across the ocean (Item dico quod quelibet
ex universis civitatibus solem in alio loco quam alia aliqua occumbere conspicit
[…]). The Irish version alters and expands the last section, adding an imaginary
journey with differing sunsets in regard to the observer. The locations involve
Jerusalem and Rome; Rome and the west of France; the west of France and the
west of Spain, until the hypothetical observer is back in Jerusalem 113.

Chapter seventeen gives a variant and much more detailed version of the last
section of chapter seven by proposing a circular figure of the earth with a
surrounding circle representing the course of the sun. The eastern, western and
southern places of sunrise and sunset are represented, respectively, by the regions
of Babylon, Egypt, and Africa.
From chapter seven, William of Conches retains arguments one and seven,
and from the latter not it’s main example, namely that of the size of the sun that is
the same for any location, but the argument illustrated by the figure : for any two
cities the time-span between morning and noon, noon and evening is the same.
Accordingly, the first half of the day does not seem shorter to people in an eastern
city than the latter half and vice versa.
From chapter seventeen he adopts the main developments but omits any
precise local identification, adds a paragraph that does not correspond to anything
in the long Latin versions of the Liber de orbe, and concludes the chapter by
giving the initial phrase of the Liber de orbe : « Quod est argumentum rotunditatis
terrae ».
Further, the last argument in favor of the sphericity of the earth refers to Egypt
concerning the visibility or invisibility of constellations. It is however indebted to
the Latin geographical tradition and not to the Liber de orbe, where the
corresponding argument does not include any precise locations. A comparison
with the corresponding argument in Pliny’s Natural History illustrates William of
Conches’virtuosity in freely adapting arguments 114.

The station and retrogradation of Saturn
The chapter of the Dragmaticon that precedes the argument about Saturn’s
retrogradation, and which is attributed to the Chaldeans, appears to be an
extremely condensed and at the same time altered rendering of chapters twenty-
five, twenty-seven, and especially thirty-two of the Liber de orbe, where the moon
and Saturn are attributed, respectively, four spheres. However, the great sphere is
left out in William of Conches’adaptation, which merely retains three spheres or
« circles », namely the zodiacal and excentric circles, and the epicycle 117.

CHAPTER-HEADINGS AND INCIPITS
In order to provide a synoptic view of the subject matter of the Liber de orbe
will be listed the chapter rubrics and incipits of the long and the short version.
1) The long version is represented primarily by F (Florence, Biblioteca
Nazionale, MS Conv. Soppr. J. I. 132). Straightforward scribal errors will be
corrected and these corrections are enclosed in square brackets. Readings of P
(Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, MS lat. 15015) will be given only in case
they differ substantially from those of F. Chapters of P tend to begin by « Dico
quod/quia […] », while those of F rather begin by « Sciendum est […] ». From
chapter thirteen onward all chapter-headings of P are preceded by titulus 1 (these
variants are not listed). By contrast, the Florence copy has only one occurrence of
titulus, namely in chapter twenty. P breaks off at chapter twenty-five. N
(Columbia University Library, MS Plimpton 161) is taken into consideration
merely with regard to chapter one. No chapter-headings have been included into
the text of this latter copy. As to the initial list of chapter-headings, it has not been
possible to read the text on the microfilm 2.

English translation (= I/E) 3. The Latin incipits that follow the titles are in small
capitals.

text of the 1549 Nuremberg edition 4.
