## Section: INTRODUCTION

INTRODUCTION
T
he history of the assimilation of newly translated cosmological writings
during the twelfth century is yet to be written, just as the ongoing
transmission of Latin cosmology remains to be acknowledged and investigated.
More specifically, any attempt at tracing the history of twelfth century cosmology
needs to envision the entirety, and not merely chosen aspects of then prevailing
ideas about the structure and the functioning of the universe. Thus, while
speculations on its corporeal, elementary constitution have become an object of
research ever since Alexandre Birkenmajer discovered the impact of medical
texts translated by Constantine the African 1, questions that concern the heavenly,
rather than the sublunary sphere tend to be neglected. This neglect concerns both
textual and pictorial documents and is due, above all, to the scarcity of reliable
chronological and geographical data. Unequivocal evidence about the circum-
stances in which the new science of the heavens was transmitted and assimilated,
let alone modes of transmission and assimilation, is rare as well.
In view of this situation, William of Conches’acquaintance with the
astronomy of an abundantly illustrated cosmological treatise 2 usually ascribed to
M¬sh¬’all¬h (fl. 762-c. 815) when writing his Dragmaticon philosophiae in
Normandy c. 1144-1149 3 stands out as an all the more remarkable event. It adds a
new dimension to our knowledge about the introduction of Arabic learning into
the Latin world, while also raising the question as to where and how William of
Conches came into contact with this otherwise little known cosmology, which
appears to have originated at least in part within the first generation of
astronomers and astrologers of the newly founded Baghdad 4.

Although William of Conches’status as a major intellectual figure in the early
stages of the twelfth-century renaissance has long been acknowledged, his
astronomy has not been studied in any detail except by Dorothy Elford 5, and even
less so with regard to its indebtedness to either the Latin school tradition 6 or to
recent translations. Instead, historians focused their attention on the novel aspects
of his elementary physics, which is indebted to Salernitan medical writings 7.
Since both the early Philosophia, which was written in the twenties, and its
revised version (1141?) 8 are as yet unaffected by translations from the Arabic as
far as astronomy is concerned, William of Conches must have become acquainted
with M¬sh¬’all¬h’s cosmology sometime during the fifth decade of the twelfth
century. From it he adapted, above all, arguments aimed at proving the earth’s
sphericity as well as an explanation of the apparent irregularity of Saturn’s
revolution, together with three pictorial representations (fig. I, II, III). These are
William’s most conspicuous borrowings, but other passages betray his familiarity
with the treatise in question as well. The first one concerns a theory he opposes,
namely that the zodiacal signs are divided according to elementary qualities 9, and
the second one is about the relative size of the sun 10. Thus, when William of
Conches states with regard to the celestial part of the universe that he will deal
only « with matters relevant to the reading of the philosophers who are studied in
today’s schools and omit the rest », he includes the reading of, or lecturing and
commenting on, at least one recently translated Arabic cosmological text 11.
The cosmology in question bears the title Liber de orbe and has first been
ascribed to M¬sh¬’all¬h in the list of Gerard of Cremona’s translations 12. The

THE SHORT AND THE LONG VERSION OF THE LIBER DE ORBE
In order to be able to venture a few preliminary hypotheses regarding the
circumstances in which William of Conches might have become acquainted with
the Liber de orbe, it will be necessary, first of all, to outline the more prominent
characteristics of its short and long versions both with regard to vocabulary and
content. Differences in style should be considered as well. However, in the
absence of a critical edition of the Liber de orbe this aspect cannot be taken into
consideration. As a matter of fact, the short and the long version are divergent to a
point that separate critical editions are needed for further, detailed analysis 38.
The lack of critical editions affects both research on the origin of the
respective versions and investigations into the identity of the translator, or trans-
lators. Thus, the following questions remain to be addressed : Does the short
version indeed go back to the very first stage of the history of Arabic cosmology
and astronomy, or is its ascription to M¬sh¬’all¬h entirely spurious? Which one of
the versions of the Liber de orbe represents the original tract, or at least something
approaching a hypothetical original version? In other words, is this version
represented by the twenty-seven chapter treatise or, rather, was it excerpted from
the forty chapter treatise 39?
Was Gerard of Cremona really the translator of what appears to be the twenty-
seven chapter version in the list of Gerard’s translations? 40 But, if this is indeed
the case, who was the translator of the long version? Was it somebody who also
translated into Latin astrological works by M¬sh¬’all¬h, such as John of Seville?
Moreover, what are we to make of Plato of Tivoli’s mentioning M¬sh¬’all¬h in his
list of major representatives of Arabic astronomy?

Turning to the question of content of both the short and the long versions of the
Liber de orbe, it becomes immediately clear that the long version puts more
emphasis on theological issues than the short one : it adds a prologue about the
creator and the possibility for man to know him through his creation 57. The
creative and governing activity of an all-powerful god is also stressed in the first
chapter, as is the case in several subsequent passages. In the first chapter the short
version inveighs against ancient Indian philosophers who denied divinity the
knowledge of its own creative act 58. Instead, the long version criticizes a gens
daharia (daharzia) for believing that the firmament and the stars have a creative
role.
Chapters two to six of both versions deal with the elementary composition of
the world, the movements of the elements, and the central position of the earth.
Then, while chapter seven of the short version goes on discussing the
distribution of the elements within the spherical world in accordance with their
dry or moist qualities, their weight, upward and downward motion 59, the long
version gives, instead, a series of arguments proving the sphericity of the earth.
Chapter seventeen is a continuation of the last part of chapter seven. William of
Conches took two arguments from chapter seven, and a third one from chapter
seventeen together with two figures.
Chapters eight to twelve continue the series of chapters that have been added
in the long version. They deal with meteorological and geographical issues : seas
overflow due to accumulated solid material, a phenomenon which is caused by the
moisture coming from the clouds, which roots up the soil. Chapter nine lists the
effects of the actions of cold and heat, explaining earthquakes in terms of their
clashing in the center of the earth. Further are discussed the different tastes of
different waters, and their saltiness. Chapter ten is about burning sulphur, which
causes the eruptions of mounts Etna and Vesuvius 60. Chapter eleven deals with
the effects of the moon on moist things such as cucumbers, and especially it’s
causing ebb and tide, explaining the causal relation with the help of a « geome-
trical figure ». Chapter twelve explains the flooding of the Nile in terms of
atmospheric phenomena; winds prevent its waters from flowing into the sea.

attributed to the heavenly sphere merely on condition that the four elements are
confined to the sublunary sphere.
The next chapters of the long version (fourteen to sixteen) discuss motion per
se, insisting on the perfection of god’s creation and, in particular, of the circular
motion, and then in relation to the celestial spheres. Although the spheres of the
firmament have separate motions, there is no contrariety between these and the
firmament, which is not of an elementary composition and therefore never
undergoes change. The natures of natural bodies are not known by qualities such
as taste, odor or color, but by their positions and motions. Chapter sixteen explains
the three positions of three orbits as conceived, respectively, starting from the
eastern, northern, and western points.
Following chapter seventeen, the short and the long version converge again,
discussing in chapters eighteen to twenty-four the relative size of the sun, which is
being demonstrated by way of the form of shades cast by spherical bodies. The
differing shapes of the waxing and waning moon are then explained; chapter
twenty-two deals with lunar eclipses. It is demonstrated that the light of all stars
comes from the sun and that solar eclipses are caused by the interposition of the
moon between the sun and the earth. Finally the visibility of the moon is
discussed. No less than fifteen diagrams are used to help explain the topics
covered in this series of chapters.
From chapter twenty-seven (fifteen of the short version) onward subjects are
being exposed that were new to the Latin West. They concern, first, the four
spheres of the moon, as « related by the learned » : the great sphere which carries
along the moon in its daily revolution from east to west. The second sphere is the
« sphere like the sphere of the signs » insofar as the moon never leaves the belt of
the zodiacal signs. The third, eccentric sphere is used to help account for the
moon’s being near or distant from the earth. Finally, the eccentric sphere com-
prises a « small sphere » (meaning epicycle) which moves eastwards and which is
used to account for apparent accelerations and slowing and backward revolutions
of the moon. The sun is being attributed two spheres, the great sphere which
moves from east to west and an eccentric sphere which moves in the opposite
direction. Referring frequently to Ptolemy, chapter twenty-nine (eighteen)
distinguishes between natural and forced movements, the eastward motion of
planetary spheres and of the sphere of the fixed stars being considered to be
natural. By contrast, the « great sphere » and the sphere of the signs move

aimed at showing that the earthy glove is of a spherical form, there is also a figure
