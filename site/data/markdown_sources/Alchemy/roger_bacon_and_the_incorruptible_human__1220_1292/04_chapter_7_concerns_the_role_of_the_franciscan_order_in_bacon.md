# Roger Bacon and the Incorruptible Human, 1220–1292

## Section: Chapter 7 concerns the role of the Franciscan order in Bacon’s under-

Chapter 7 concerns the role of the Franciscan order in Bacon’s under-
standing of the ability of alchemy to supplement medicine. Bacon was
unique in that he was the first to say that a patient healed by a corpus equale
will have a body similar to that which we receive after the resurrection. In
fact, the resurrection body would be a corpus equale itself. By properly
preparing alchemical medicines, it was possible to prolong life by several
hundred years, even outliving the oldest of the patriarchs. Bacon believed
that all of this was possible because despite mankind’s current sinfulness,
humans still had the possibility to be immortal, as evidenced by the fact
that man will live forever after the resurrection. This idea of an incorrupt-
ible resurrection body is fundamental to understanding the body enhanced
by Bacon’s alchemical medicines. First, it gave Bacon the idea that the
human body has the possibility to be improved, because it was a given that
the human body will eventually reach a state of incorruptibility.

As Bacon explained in the Opus maius, God created the rational souls
of men, which are immortal. However, the body that mankind has in this
life is not immortal, which is unsatisfactory to the immortal soul, which
desires to be joined to an immortal body. The union of immortal body and
spirit is promised after the resurrection, when the “animal body will
become spiritual, and the whole man will be glorified, and will live with
God and the angels.”11 However, Bacon explained, it is impossible for
mankind to have such a body in this life, because there are too many mis-
eries in this life that are not compatible with the goodness of human
nature. The resurrection body, however, will “be free from all misery; and
since that state is perfect, there must be a glory of both body and soul.”12
The perfection of mankind required the unification of both immortal
body and soul, because man is not a soul housed in a body, but “in reality
is composed of soul and body, so that the essence of man is formed of soul
and body, and that his essence is not soul alone in a body…therefore they
have maintained that the body is at length united with the soul, so that
both may be perfected in accordance with its special character.”13 If the
human body would eventually be immortal, then it certainly had the
capacity to be improved in this life.
Additionally, the notion of an incorruptible resurrection body allowed
Bacon to introduce alchemy into his medical system. The incorruptible
resurrection body will be given by God, but a comparable incorruptible
substance—that is, gold—could be found on earth in this lifetime.
Alchemy taught how to make substances incorruptible like gold, by bal-
ancing out their elements and recombining them in the proper propor-
tions. Alchemy therefore provided the best insight into the resurrection
body that mankind could have in this world, aside from knowledge
received through divine revelation. The one part of Bacon’s ideas sur-
rounding alchemy and the prolongatio vitae that appeared consistently
throughout his career, and the area where I believe Bacon was unique, was

CHAPTER 2
Roger Bacon and the (Un)Natural State
of Man
In order to understand how Bacon thought that alchemical remedies
could prolong human life, we must first understand how Bacon believed
decay happened. What caused the body to die, either at its appointed time,
or before? What was the mechanism by which the body became corrupt?
Understanding how Bacon thought these natural processes occurred is
vital to understanding how and why Bacon believed that it was not only
possible to extend human life, but how alchemy provided the techniques
to do so. In this chapter, I will first demonstrate that Bacon was Galenic in
his understanding of human decay, and that there was no radical break
from thirteenth-century medical theory. I will highlight the distinction
Bacon made between accidental decay, which leads to the current human
lifespan of 70–80 years, and natural decay, which allows humans to live
much longer lives (150+ years). The fact that Bacon saw no fundamental
difference between the decay of the biblical patriarchs versus the decay of
modern man—that is, the decay of modern man was accidental and not
mandated by God or nature—was what allowed him to argue that life
could be extended at all. Because it was the result of human, not natural,
failings, it could be remedied using human sciences and tools.

1 Disease anD the Four humors
In the Galenic tradition—the dominant medical philosophy in Late
Antiquity and the Middle Ages—the body was conceived of as being a
combination of the four humors: blood, phlegm, bile (sometimes called
choler), and melancholy, which were each created from a combination of
the four elements: fire, air, water, and earth. Each humor contained all
four elements, but was dominated by one, which gave it its characteristic
qualities. The human body was composed of the four humors, although
each individual had their own balance of humors that was proper to them.
Everyone had a predominance of one humor, which influenced their
behavior and appearance.1 It was the job of the doctor to diagnose the
body’s state based on the complexion and the relative abundance of the
humors in relation to each other and the person’s baseline.2 For Galen,
equal complexion was not a specific point, but rather a scale relative to the
proper nature of a thing.3
In medieval nosology, disease occurred when the body entered a state
outside of its natural inclination. When a person’s humors were in proper
balance, (s)he was healthy. But if the person’s humors fell out of balance—
too much or too little of one or more humors—disease set in.4 The impor-
tant implication of this conception of disease was that there could be no
disease outside the body, because illness was just a bodily state. There was
no room in Bacon, or any medieval nosology, for causative agents in the

The symptoms of a disease reflected its humoral nature: bad health was
characterized by limpness of the body, laziness, excessive saliva, stomach
pain and wind, shivering, and loss of appetite.10 Many of these were also
signs of old age, which also included thinning of the blood and the greying
of hairs.11
As disease was considered to be a result of the imbalance of the humors,
it follows that health was the natural state of the body, when all four
humors were in proper balance, though the proper balance of humors was
not the same for every person, but was unique to each individual.12 One
person’s proper proportion or constitution, therefore, could be another’s
state of sickness.13 It was possible to adjust the four humors and bring
them back into proportion, and this was the duty of medicine. But Bacon
believed that all types of wise men, not just the doctors, had been given
wisdom by God to ensure that humans were able to correct their constitu-
tions.14 Though Bacon never made the explicit connection between
humors and medicine in his works, it is almost certain that he believed the
practice of medicine was possible because people lost their proper com-
plexion by accident, not through any law of nature. As will be shown, this
was certainly his opinion on why the prolongation of life was possible—
that is, people died at a young age because of accident, not nature.
In the Opus minus, Bacon described how complexions could be altered
to bring them back to a state of temperance, which was best for
human nature.

The lamp metaphor and the ideas behind it were not unique to the
thirteenth century. Avicenna famously also used the lamp metaphor,
although he disagreed that all types of aging could be prevented. According
to his Canon of Medicine, there were two types of death caused by the fail-
ing of the radical humidity: that which happened when the radical humid-
ity was corrupted, and that which happened when the radical humidity
was outright destroyed or consumed. The physician could prevent the
corruption of the radical humidity, but he could not prevent it from even-
tually being consumed by the innate heat. Physicians could replenish the
radical humidity to a certain degree, but not forever.23 Thus Avicenna’s
Canon taught that, like the proportion of the four humors, each individ-
ual had their own proportion of radical humidity to innate heat which
would determine their baseline for health (and ultimately the span of
their life).24
There were other theories to account for variance in lifespan: Bernard
of Gordon (d. 1330) and Albertus Magnus (d. 1280) both held that inevi-
table death occurred when the innate heat was damaged in the process of
consuming nutriment in digestion. Arnald of Villanova (d. 1311) wrote
along the same lines as Avicenna, that the ultimate determinant of death
was the proportion of the innate heat to the radical humidity.25 Ultimately,
there were many explanations for how exactly an inescapable death
occurred, but almost all relied upon some interaction between the innate
heat and the radical humidity.26 In terms of restoring the radical humidity,

3 the regimen oF health
Bacon considered bad habits and sins to be the primary causes of the
human body’s deterioration. Repeated sin led to an error in judgment,
which ultimately resulted in being unable to follow the regimen of health
and minding the six non-naturals. Traditionally, these were defined as air,

after food, it increased the body’s natural heat, aiding digestion. All six

4 natural anD acciDental Decay
If all bodies decay, what then was the difference between the decay of
contemporary man and that of the biblical patriarchs? Was it merely a
question of speed, or was there something fundamentally dissimilar about
the two modes of decay? The answer to this question is important, because
it has implications on if and how the shortening of life could be remedied.
If the decay of the patriarchs was fundamentally the same as thirteenth-
century decay, then it is possible to prolong life. It would be much trickier
to argue that such prolongation was possible if decay were different.66
Bacon believed that the men of the Old Testament lived to be about
1000 years of age, but in the thirteenth century, men barely lived to 80,
and their life was one of pain and toil. But this decay was not ordained by
God, and was instead accidental and against nature, and came from the
neglect of the regimen of health.67 This meant that it was remediable. In
Part 6 of the Opus maius, Bacon repeated the idea that the shortening of
life is accidental. However, his argument here was circular, for he says that
people are able to lengthen their lives, so early death must not be natural.68

There are several reasons why Bacon could have changed his mind. Could
it be from his exposure to the Secretum secretorum? This is unlikely for two
reasons: (1) Bacon was already aware of the Secretum secretorum when
writing the Opus maius, and (2) the Secretum secretorum did not discuss
alchemy in the context of the prolongatio vitae: as others have already
pointed out, the Secretum secretorum had a different perfect medicine: a
compound called the gloria inestimabilis.80 Gold was a minor component

produced either via alchemy or via experimental science.81

reading more alchemical works, such as pseudo-Avicenna’s De anima
(Scientia majoris alkimie), which forged the link in his mind between
medicine and alchemy, and was cited in the section of the Opus tertium

wanted physicians to learn about alchemy, he did not want this informa-

works, in the event that one of the three was intercepted. Bacon made
references to practices such as dispersa intentio in his works, and it is
entirely possible that the sections on alchemy were an example of it in
action.83 Ultimately, there is no conclusive evidence for either theory,
though they stand as the best options.

Bacon’s interest in the ability of human arts to extend human life
endured for almost three decades until his own death. It was not a mar-
ginal interest, but occupied a great deal of his time, and resulted in a
number of works, specifically on the ability of alchemy to extend life.
Bacon understood contemporary thirteenth-century medical theories
regarding the nature of the body, namely the theory of the four humors
and the relationship between innate heat and radical humidity. However,
he largely rejected the role of radical humidity and innate heat, and thus
broke with those university-trained physicians who tried to balance the
two. Instead, Bacon placed a great deal of significance on the four humors,
but also the six non-naturals. Mankind was no longer able to follow the six
non-naturals, and thus keep their humors in balance, because sin had
clouded their ability to follow these rules. As the sins were compounded
over the generations, the average lifespan became shorter and shorter.
Bacon was consistent in this view: lifespan had come to be shortened, not
by an act of God or a law of nature, but because of mankind’s own faults.
However, this accidental nature of death meant that it could also be rem-
edied using human science, namely alchemy.

CHAPTER 3
Learning to Prolong Life
Roger Bacon taught that not only was the extreme prolongation of life
possible, but that there were a number of different fields that could teach
this process, with varying degrees of success. The first, of course, was the
field of medicine. This chapter will analyze the different sciences that
taught about the human body and its relation to the natural world. By
examining Bacon’s concepts of how these fields interacted, and their
respective strengths and weaknesses, it becomes evident that Bacon
believed that even though traditional medicine had many faults, it could
not be rejected in the prolongatio vitae. Much like sin had clouded man-
kind’s ability to follow the regimen of health over the generations, poor
translations, excessive reliance on authority, and lack of solid theory meant
that medical errors were passed from one generation to the next. And,
much like life could be prolonged despite mankind’s sins, so too could
medicine be revived and reformed through the use of alchemy.
Despite its problems, Bacon continued to believe that the art of medi-
cine was the first cause of health.1 But medicine neglected many things that
were necessary for its completion, to the detriment of the doctors and their
patients. For this reason, Bacon wrote De erroribus medicorum, in which he

made of humors.84 They were only called blood, choler, phlegm, and mel-
ancholy when found in animals or people, but they were the same material
in all things. The fact that this sentiment appears in so many works, both
those devoted to alchemy and medicine specifically, but also to natural phi-
losophy more generally, highlights its importance to Bacon. Thus specula-
tive alchemy forms the root of medicine, in that it teaches about the
properties of not just the human body, but all the medicines that can fix it.85
As speculative alchemy taught about the generation and corruption of
substances, it likewise taught of their potential incorruptibility. Bacon,
loosely borrowing from Aristotle, wrote that incorruptibility was the result
of what he called a corpus equale—that is, a body with an equal complexion.
If the strength of each element was in the perfect proportion to the rest,
then the body would be incapable of decay. Aristotle had taught in his
Metaphysics that this was a result of the inability of one element to act upon
another: Bacon agreed, but also said that decay was impossible because the
prime matter finally had the proper form—that of equal complexion.86
Some things already had a corpus equale: gold, which cannot be destroyed
by air or fire; the immortal resurrection body granted by God; and finally,
things produced through art and nature (alchemy and experimental
science).87 Speculative alchemy could then provide the key to making the
human complexion more balanced, and thus making it less corruptible and
extending life. Indeed, Bacon wrote that the secret of secrets is being able
to make an equality of the qualities in the humors.88

In Bacon’s presumed earliest work on the prolongatio vitae, the Opus
maius, alchemy was not a stand-alone science, but was in fact part of a
larger program of natural philosophy. The Opus maius listed three ways of
acquiring knowledge: authority; reasoning, which drew conclusions, but
did not make the conclusion certain; and experience, which made the con-
clusion certain and removed any doubt. Of the three, experience was the
best, reason was acceptable, and authority was not to be trusted.
“Experience” itself could be further broken down into two types: external,
which happened through the senses, and internal, which was the result of
divine revelation. The patriarchs were given all the sciences by God, who
also gave them long lives so that they could test the conclusions for
themselves.

prolongatio vitae was scientia experimentalis, or experimental science.127

which Aristotle placed in his book on the regimen of life.”128 This secret,
