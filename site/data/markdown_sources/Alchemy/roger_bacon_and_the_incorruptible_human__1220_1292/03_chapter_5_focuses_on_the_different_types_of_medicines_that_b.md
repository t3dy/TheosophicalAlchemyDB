# Roger Bacon and the Incorruptible Human, 1220–1292

## Section: Chapter 5 focuses on the different types of medicines that Bacon

Chapter 5 focuses on the different types of medicines that Bacon
believed could extend human life. Bacon’s medicines can be organized
into three categories: orthodox medicines, which were prescribed by phy-
sicians and made by apothecaries; prepared alchemical medicines, items
which had been altered using speculative and practical alchemy to bring
their proportion of elements closer to the corpus equale; and true alchemi-
cal medicines, which had an innate corpus equale. Although most of the
medicines, including the prepared and true alchemical, were taken from
earlier writings, Bacon went beyond his sources by ascribing their semi-
miraculous powers to their equality of elements.

In Chap. 6, I establish the relationship Bacon had with the professional
medical field and university medicine in the thirteenth century. By placing
Bacon within a number of increasingly heated debates on the state of med-
icine and its professional status and popular appeal, I demonstrate that
Bacon was typical of his time, and is responding to fears about the untrust-
worthy nature of monetized medicine.
Bacon’s main qualms about physicians were that they did not under-
stand natural philosophy, and so were unable to understand the workings
of the human body.10 I show that he was concerned with the lack of theory
in medical practice, as exemplified by the physicians who did not under-
stand how the four elements combine to create the four humors; and
practice, in which the physicians relied on apothecaries to make their
drugs. Bacon felt that the doctors were obligated to learn alchemical the-
ory, for it formed the basis of medical practice, and taught how to sustain
the body, restore health, and prolong human life. Doctors (and apothecar-
ies) who were ignorant of alchemical theory were dangerous to patients.
However, I also demonstrate that Bacon’s medical theory was fundamen-
tally Galenic, in that it maintained that health comes from a proper bal-
ance of the four humors—an equal complexion. His only rejection of
Galenic theory was in disregarding the importance of the natural humidity
in the aging process, a point hitherto ignored in scholarship.
