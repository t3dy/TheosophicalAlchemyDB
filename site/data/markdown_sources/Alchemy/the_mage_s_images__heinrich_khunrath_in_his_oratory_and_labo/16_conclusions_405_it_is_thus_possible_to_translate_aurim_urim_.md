# The Mage’s Images: Heinrich Khunrath in His Oratory and Laboratory

## Section: Conclusions.405 It is thus possible to translate Aurim/Urim as either ‘Fires’ or

Conclusions.405 It is thus possible to translate Aurim/Urim as either ‘Fires’ or
‘Lights’, thereby allowing the themes of Creation and Revelation to coincide
in the same ‘symbolic’ word.406 In Hebrew Urim is technically the plural of
Or (Light), as, for example, רוא יהי [yehi ʿor], the ‘Fiat Lux’ of Genesis 1:3.407 In
the Amphitheatre, Khunrath gives us a literal gloss of the singular form of the
word in the phrase: ‘LIGHT (in Hebrew AOR) […] author of splendour and
illumination.’408 It is tempting to read this phrase as an allusion to the two
most important works of Jewish Kabbalah, the Bahir (Illumination) and Zohar
(Splendour). It could also possibly be alluding to astral influences, as when
Khunrath describes the stars as Aot (the Hebrew for ‘sign’, ‘omen’, or ‘letter’
being תוא [ot]).409 He could certainly have encountered Ricci’s note concern-
ing תוא in Pistorius’s Artis cabalisticae, where he explains that it is pronounced
‘Oos’ and means ‘sign’.410 It is worth bearing in mind, too, that the French
astrologer and librarian to Cardinal Richelieu, Jacques Gaffarel (1601–1681),
interested in Cabala, though critical of Khunrath, created an engraving of the
stars as Hebrew characters in a celestial map.411 There is even the possibility
that when Khunrath writes of the ‘Micro-cosmic Moon of the Cabalists,’ the
image of the human soul basking in the reflected light of God as the ‘Father of
Lights’,412 there could also be an allusion to the Shekhinah, the female aspect

23 Urim and Thummim
In De Igne Magorum Philosophorumque (On the Fire of the Mages and Sages),
Khunrath provides his intepretation of Urim as ‘Fires’ when he claims that
the Roman goddess Vesta, with her eternally-burning fire, derives her name
from אי שא ESCH-IAH (God’s Fire),414 and that the Persian god Orimasda is
essentially a foreshortened form of the Hebrew words םירוא URIM (Fires) and
ESCH-IAH (God’s Fire).415 Johann Arndt’s Judicium makes a similar associa-
tion, declaring that ‘UR means fire in Chaldaean, and therefore this town in
Chaldaea got its name from the fact that the Philosophers, who had this won-
derful Fire, lived there,’ and that this was a ‘Magical, Wonderful, Mysterious,
hidden Fire, a Secret of all Nature.’416 Arndt is of course refering to the famous
city of ‘Ur of the Chaldees’, alleged birthplace of the patriarch Abraham.417 The
association is also made with the mysterious Urim of the Old Testament, con-
nected with the םימות [Thummim] revered for their oracular powers.
As Moshe Idel writes, ‘opinion is divided’ as to their natures. Abraham
Abulafia, whose thought exerted such an influence on Christian Cabala, sug-
gests that the Urim and Thummim are two distinct types of mystical experi-
ences, the former the lower, involving visions of lights;418 the latter being the

The eleventh-century French rabbi Rashi noted that in gematria Urim and
Thummim equal the Shemhamphorasch, the Name of 72 Letters. Such can also
be found in the Zohar, where it is written ‘and it is customary in the 72-letters
inscribed, which are the secret of the Holy Name, and all of them are called
Urim and Tummim.’421 In the following century, the Spanish Rabbi Abraham
ibn Ezra (1089–1167) suggested that they alluded to the seven planets.422 A
manuscript copy of the 1608 Amphitheatrum in Amsterdam, made in 1702 by
‘F. Bruno Eremita’, includes a marginal note next to a reference to the Urim and
Thummim in relation to the Divine Philosophers’ Stone in Isagoge 3, that pro-
vides several useful interpretations of their meaning: ‘An explanation or inter-
pretation of the Urim and Thummim, that is Illumination and Perfection – or
knowledge and purity of life or according to our common saying Doctrine
and Truth’.423
