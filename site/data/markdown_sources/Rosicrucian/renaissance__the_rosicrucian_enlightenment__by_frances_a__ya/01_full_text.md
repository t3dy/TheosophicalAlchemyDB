The Rosicrucian Enlightenment. By Frances A. Yates. London: Routledge &
Kegan Paul, 1971. Pp. xvi+269. £4.50.
With her new work, Frances Yates extends the investigations into Renais-
sance Hermeticism which she began in Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic tradition.
The Rosicrucian enlightenment attempts to establish the existence of a distinct phase
of Hermetic belief in the early decades of the seventeenth century, which centred
upon the court of the Elector Frederick of the Palatinate at Heidelberg and was
a direct product of the writings of John Dee. Its characteristics were the central
position accorded to alchemy, practical and mystical, and emphasis on cabalistic
teachings, and a strong anti-Catholic (and particularly anti-Jesuit) bias. This
movement, she claims, may be most accurately described as 'Rosicrucian', since
the manifestos of 1614 and 1615 lay at its heart. Further, their symbolism and
prophecies were absorbed by Christian of Anhalt, the leading councillor of
Frederick, to supply an ideological programme for the Palatinate crusade
against the Catholic, Hapsburg Empire launched when Frederick accepted the
throne of Bohemia. Even when this adventure collapsed in disaster at the Battle
of the White Mountain in November 1620, Rosicrucian ideas continued to be
influential through the writings of Andreae, Comenius, Hartlib, Ashmole, and
even Francis Bacon. From 'the great reservoir of spiritual and intellectual power,
of moral and reforming vision', which the Rosicrucian manifestos had expressed,
many later developments are seen to draw sustenance. The reform movement in
England during the Interregnum, the Royal Society, speculative free-masonry,
alchemy, chemical medicine, and the turn of interest towards the natural world
are all argued to owe something to the Hermetic tradition and specifically to its
Rosicrucian phase.
Because of the range of her perception, the intrinsic fascination of her
material, and the extraordinary places into which she leads her readers, Dr
Yates has always been an exciting writer. The present work is no exception.
Pleasantly written, agreeably printed (with the footnotes where they should be at
the bottom of the page), and amply illustrated, the book compells attention and
is highly stimulating. Unfortunately it is not very convincing. A part of the
excitement of Dr Yates's writing is always her readiness to speculate when the
evidence fails. Used with control, this technique can be of great value, but, as
Dr Yates builds up the details of her argument about Rosicrucianism, the
evidence fails too often. Time and again when clinching proof is required,
nothing is forthcoming but probablities. Consider for example Dr Yates dis-
cussion of Khunrath. The fact that John Dee's monas symbol is illustrated in
Henricus Khunrath's Amphitheatrum sapientiae aeternae (1609) and that two of his
works are cited in the text, is good if not necessarily conclusive evidence for
Dee's intellectual influence, and it is reinforced by the fact that the two men
had met at Bremen in 1589. The fact, however, that 'in Khunrath's work we
meet with the characteristic phraseology of the manifestos, the everlasting

emphasis on macrocosm and microcosm, the stress on Magia, Cabala, and
Alchymia as in some way combining to form a religious philosophy which
promises a new dawn for mankind', does not make Khunrath's work a link
between Dee's philosophy and that of the Rosicrucian manifestos unless a
similarly precise literary influence can be demonstrated. Dr Yates's suggestion
that one engraving in the Amphitheatre 'may well have suggested imagery in the
Rosicrucian Fama' and that another is 'suggestive of the outlook both of John
Dee and the Rosicrucian Manifestos', is not proof. When three works emanate
from a common tradition, they may be expected to show similarities. That one
influenced the other, however, demands the evidence of close parallels and
verifiable citations.
That Dee's writings influenced works intimately associated with the
manifestos, such as the Secretions philosophiae consideratio brevis a Phillip a Gabellae
(1615), is evident. That there was a personal influence by Dee, and that the
'Rosicrucian movement in Germany was the delayed result of Dee's mission in
Bohemia over twenty years earlier, influences from which became associated
with the Elector Palatine' is, as Dr Yates says, 'strangely exciting'. It is also very
doubtful. Doubtful, not simply because Dr Yates fails to produce firm evidence
for her proposed chain of transmission from Bohemia to Christian of Anhalt
via Peter Rozmberk, with whose elder brother Dee had lived at Trebona from
1586 to 1588, but also because one remains unconvinced that the Rosicrucian
manifestos were anything more than a literary exercise in a mystical genre,
drawing on other familiar works in the genre, which gave expression to an
idealistic and fantastic programme of political wish-fulfillment, entirely lacking
a basis in political reality. That the Hermetic writings of Dee were used by
Protestant writers working within the same mystical and Hermetic tradition
for their own purposes (which on occasion were political) does not make Dee
the architect of a Protestant political ideology.
The crucial problem raised by this book, as it is by Dr Yates's earlier works,
is how far the ideas that she exhumes from literary sources were present and
influential in the actions of those who might have read them. Assessing the
influence of Rosicrucian ideas is more than usually difficult, however, since it is
not always clear how to distinguish the products of the Rosicrucian phase of
Hermeticism from those of the tradition in general. Of the characteristics Dr
Yates lists, alchemy and cabala had always found a place in Hermetic writings,
and, if they became more strongly emphasized during the early years of the
seventeenth century, this may have been caused as much by events external to
the tradition such as a greater exploitation of the appropriateness of alchemical
symbolism to express mystical experience and independent developments
within the cabala, as it was by the influence of the Rosicrucian association of
these subjects. The fact that Dr Yates argues that after 1620 the Rosicrucian
manifestos ceased to have any specific meaning, but took on a more general one
as a call for a general reformation, tends to confirm this impression. This
reformation would 'find its strength in Evangelical Christianity with its emphasis
on brotherly love, in the esoteric Hermetic-Cabalist tradition, and in an accom-
panying turning towards the works of God in nature in a scientific spirit of
exploration, using science or magic, magical science or scientific magic, in the
service of man.' That such a change was desired in mid-seventeenth century
Europe, and an end to the chaos engendered by political insecurity and rapid
socio-cultural change, is indisputable, but that it should be labelled specifically
Rosicrucian and assumed to derive from the manifestos, and from John Dee's
form of Hermetic practice, seems too restrictive. Dr Yates's location of Rosicru-
cianism historically in Heidelberg with the Elector Palatine seems plausible, if

In contrast with the contents of the fourth volume of Dr Whiteside's
admirable edition of Newton's mathematical papers, in which many of the
writings were fragmentary but in which Newton's enormous range was well
exemplified, the contents of the fifth cover a narrow and coherent theme. These
contents came to the notice of Newton's public more than two decades after
they were first written, and then in a restructured form and under Whiston's
title, Arithmetica universalis. This fifth volume of the Whiteside edition has as its
basis ninety-seven Lucasian lectures of the period 1673-83, which Newton
himself put into the Cambridge archives, presumably in fulfilment of a statutory
duty. As before, the Latin text is faced with a translation into English. The
first part of the new volume is supplemented by some shorter pieces—prelimin-
ary drafts and subsequent attempts at solutions to problems raised in the lectures
—while in part 2 the editor prints the author's unfinished revision of those parts
of the algebraic lectures (now entitled Arithmeticae universalis liber primus) which
differ significantly from the deposited text of the lectures. He also prints in
part 2 an isolated regula fratrum, a loosely expressed but expertly applied rule
for so choosing conjugate quantities (such as the semi-sum or semi-difference of
of sides of a triangle, rather than the sides themselves) that they simplify the
solution to a particular problem.
The first edition of the Arithmetica, put out in 1707 by Whiston, who quali-
fied the title with the words 'in usum juventutis academicae', was delayed for
reasons that are now a matter for speculation. Dr Whiteside surmises that
Wallis's voluminous algebra of 1685 deterred Newton from making his own
studies available, whilst Halley's visit to Cambridge in 1684, to discuss the
unsolved problem of elliptical planetary motion, probably turned Newton's
thoughts in other directions. The Arithmetica had a complicated publishing
history, which the editor very effectively sorts out in his excellent Introduction.
(He might usefully have mentioned that the Castiglione edition of 1761 includes
material by G. F. Baermann, G. Kaestner, and R. J. Boscovich, over and above
the seven articles appended to the 'sGravesande edition of 1727.) At all events,
the mathematical world found the book, in its several manifestations, useful as
recently as the early years of the last century.
