Conclusion
Although relatively few "emblems" (according to narrower definitions)
are immediately evident in this scheme, that is because in certain cases the
building contains either figura or lemma, the complementary part being pro
vided by allusion elsewhere, usually in an already published text or in a
related dramatic performance. Furthermore, the buildings themselves or
their representation also serve as figurae or as subjects of emblems (or other
genres of symbolic image). This case thus illustrates well how buildings
could assume an active part within the complex web of associations spun as
a result of the emblematic mode of conception, in the context of an early
modern society.

Hieroglyphs in the Street:
Architectural Emblematics and the ldea of
the Façade in early Sixteenth-Century Palace Design
CHARLES BURROUGHS
Binghamton University
My subject, first of all, is an aspect of the pre-history of the emblem; 1 wish
to discuss certain visual cultural phenomena that accompanied the emergence
of the canonical impresa.1 By the end of the fifteenth century, the composite
device or impresa, fashioned both of a textual and a pictorial element, had
become a familiar, even ubiquitous phenomenonon in the material culture
of ltalian and other European elites. Around the middle of the sixteenth
century this earlier phase of innovation and experimentation, involving a
range of practices and sites, began to yield to a process of codification and
the application of norms in a succession of more or Jess prescriptive texts,
as well as anthologies flattering the prominent individuals whose devices
were included, and prompting the admiration and emulation of others. From
this point, the history of the impresa and related forms was closely
associated with developments in typography and page layout, becoming,
indeed, a rather significant part of the early history of the printed book and
its spatial economy. The form of the impresa itself was, of course, not
unaffected by its insertion into literary, or at least bookish, contexts. In
particular, this fostered the establishment of the highly successful three
part format, in which each device-now composed of motto, image, and
explicatory verse-is typically assigned an individual page. With its white
margin the page frames the resultant composite entity, uniting its parts and
detaching it from the visual contexts in which simpler devices proliferated.2
Of course, the constitution of the canonical impresa and of emblematics as
a genre of discourse in the later sixteenth century and afterwards did not
prevent, indeed it encouraged, the creation of new kinds of hybrid formation
both in frontispieces, especially in works devoted to the discussion of
architecture itself, and beyond the printed page.3 To institute a frame is to
invite trespass.
The proto-emblematic devices of the early Renaissance-often called
"hieroglyphs"-had appeared in a range of sites and in multifarious forms,
but especially on items of clothing and apparel. 4 They were thus directly

associated with the ornamentation and enhancement of the body.
Hieroglyphs also adorned objects and surfaces in domestic environments, the
furniture and furnishings that functioned as extensions of body decoration
and provided sites for such important early modern social rituals as the
recognition, explication, and exchange of personalized tokens. Consistent
with the background of imprese in chivalric practices and fashions, these
might have to do with relatively ad hoc and temporary social, especially
amatory, purposes. 5
Hieroglyphs and devices are related to, but distinct from, heraldic
insignia, which had a much longer history of deployment in various
situations, notably on palaces and other elite residences, identifying and
distinguishing the occupant's family and alliances, especially through
marriage, and celebrating status. The frequent exterior display of coats of
arms, e.g., on a door surround or in a portico, had obvious implications for
architectural design. 6 Persona! devices could easily be added to such
markers, and doubtless often were, though by their very nature their
reference was more circumscribed and temporary. But with the growing
fashion for painted or sgraffito ornament, sometimes spreading over the wall
surface of a house, opportunities for such decoration were expanded, though
their poor rate of survival makes generalization hazardous. 7
Sorne extant houses preserve in stone permanent versions of decora
tion doubtless more commonly executed in paint, stucco, or carved wood.8
In Vicenza, for example, a mid-fifteenth-century palace façade carries the
chivalric motto "Il n'est rose sans espine" [No rose without a thorn]
accompanied by appropriate sculptural ornament, even executed in rose
colored stone.9 This has the character of a persona! rather than a family
motto. The famous façade of the Palazzo Rucellai in Florence (Fig. 1)
displays both the arms of the Rucellai family and the persona! device of the
individual responsible for the large-scale transformation of a group of family
houses into an apparent single palace; here the appearance of persona!
devices bears witness to shifts in kinship patterns and affective relations far
wider than Giovanni Rucellai's disputes with certain less affluent relatives.10
The Rucellai palace occupies an important place in a crucial process
in early modern residential architecture, the constitution of recognizably
classicizing façades, in the almost complete absence of authentic models.11
The late fifteenth and early sixteenth-century experimentation with various
façade schemata has something in common with the experimentation with
hieroglyphs and related forms of visual communication to which 1 alluded
above. Conventions emerged and became established, and in practice the

façade became a compositionally unified and conceptually distinct element
of house design. 12
Such a façade lent itself to representation in graphie form on a single
page-an early case is the engravings of façade designs for Achille Bocchi's
house-cum-academy in Bologna-a phenomenon that coincided quite closely
with the appearance of books of imprese or emblems, like Bocchi's own
Symbolicarum quaestionum ... libri of 1555.13 By the end of the century the
parallel between book and building was familiar, e.g., as manifested in
architectural frontispieces that provided entry into a spatially conceived
text.14
The flexible covering or clothing of a building's front with painted or
sgraffito decoration continued in the sixteenth century;15 but especially in the
most prestigious residential architecture we encounter a marked demand for
a specifically architectural façade, a rigid carapace deploying the newly
formulated canonic classical vocabulary and compositional schemata. The
Rucellai façade occupies an intermediate position; it is both open-ended and
classicizing, because based on the superimposed orders of the Colosseum,
which, of course, as an oval building, has a façade without limits. The
famous jagged edge to the right of the Rucellai façade indicates, probably,
the planned addition of further bays. As we will see, façade types
characterized by a comparable degree of flexibility, some derived from
Trajan's Market, gained particular currency in early sixteenth-century
Italian palace architecture.
The Rucellai palace proved of limited significance as a model for
façade design in Florence, though an important early classicizing palace in
Rome, the Cancelleria (Fig. 2), clearly derives from it. Superimposed
orders, in the guise of shallow pilasters, appear on the main façade above
a contrasting basement; 16 this opposition of basement and upper zone, rather
than the motif of superimposed orders, was to inspire later designs. The
classicizing revetment, however, clothes a traditional Roman type of
towered palace complex, though the somewhat residual towers, which
project from the extremely long front of the building and carry prominent
heraldic insignia, do not break the roofline.
The treatment of the façade as a high-style cultural artifact expressive
of transcendent though certainly ideologically charged values could not
easily be reconciled with the expression of persona! interests and claims, not
least through insertion of imprese or hieroglyphs. In Serlio's Libro
extraordinario (1551), a series of orthodox portal designs undergo invasion
and contamination by rough blocks; more radically intrusive, however, are

The Cancelleria brought the classical façade to Roman palace
architecture. lt was soon imitated, though with important modifications, in
a site that, as 1 have maintained elsewhere, was crucial in the development
of the early modern classical type and indeed concept of the secular façade.
The via Alessandrina in Rome, founded in 1500, was a planned parade
street, designed to enhance the entrance to the papal palace (Fig. 3).19 But
it soon evolved into a court enclave, with its remarkably diverse façades
constituting a veritable laboratory of architecture. The Cancelleria schema
was adopted for the grand Palazzo Castellesi, which fronted onto the Via
Alessandrina, while also forming one side of a piazza extending to the old
main road through the district, the Borgo Vecchio, and a large cardinal's
palace, the Palazzo Domenico della Rovere, constructed late in the previous
century (Figs. 4, 5).20 These two palaces displayed in emphatic proximity
the two major, contrasting palace types of quattrocento Rome: one
exemplified by the still somewhat exotic Cancelleria; the other the
indigenous towered type with lines of cross-mullioned windows and
impressively framed portais opening in expanses of stuccoed wall.
That this contrast of palace types was evident to contemporaries is
clear from a passage in the De Cardinalatu (c. 1510) by Paolo Cortesi, who
had already attracted notice through his intervention in the debate about the
merits of contrasting literary styles. 21 Cortesi praises the Cancelleria and the
Castellesi façades, both modelled on the Colosseum and faced with stone,
and as such altogether preferable to the stucco surfaces of the Palazzo Della
Rovere, painted to create a fictive architectural effect. 22 He does not mention
the use of devices or hieroglyphs in façade decoration, but may well have
disapproved ofthis, at least on a cardinal's residence, since he recommends
the decoration only of relatively secluded and intimate spaces with what he
calls aenigmata.23 The emblem, in other words, belongs in the private
sphere, but perhaps only in the context of patrons holding high office and
very much in public view.
For Cortesi the Castellesi and Della Rovere façades, one of real and
one of fictive stone, exemplify the contrast of nature and art. 24 In

compressed form the contrast of nature and art articulates the façade of a
third palace on the square, the famous though shortlived Palazzo Caprini
(Fig. 6), erected shortly after the Palazzo Castellesi was begun, just across
the via Alessandrina.25 In his façade design the architect, Bramante,
developed a theme adumbrated in the Castellesi façade: he set a single
columnar order above a rusticated basement, a contrast that Serlio would
soon characterize as one of nature and art, in which for Bramante and
Serlio, unlike Cortesi, art is superior to nature.26 This hierarchic relation
was reinforced by the social associations and even functions of each story:
the basement with its artisans' shops displayed the occupants' labor and
engagement in commerce; the upper story's grand Doric columns and high,
elaborately framed windows fronted spaces for the social rituals of the elite,
notably the surveillance of the street beneath, at least during the processions
that the street was built to stage appropriately.
The design of the Palazzo Caprini included no emblems, though a
well-known drawing in London (Fig. 6) shows heraldic shields over the
windows of the side façade; this is not indicated in other representations,
and seems unlikely to have been part of Bramante's design.27 But there is a
case for regarding the façade as a whole as deeply emblematic, as a kind of
hieroglyph succinctly symbolizing the hierarchic character of the city or
state. In other words, Bramante's façade constitutes an architectural
statement of the familiar principle, enunciated most clearly by Alberti, of the
house as an analogue of the city. 28 Bramante himself, of course, was deeply
interested in hieroglyphic writing and even proposed an inscription in
"hieroglyphs" (rather simple pictorial equivalents for syllables) for the
Vatican palace.29 It is plausible to see the Caprini façade as a kind of
hieroglyph; certainly its conventional symbolic quality was emphasized by
its manifest failure to correspond-indexically, in Peircean terms-with the
interior (the façade lacked an architecturally defined attic, but viewers were
hardly expected to miss the windows of the servants' quarters tucked into
the Doric frieze in the place of certain metopes), while the sign-value of the
rustication was emphasized by its apparently obvious facture out of stucco,
simulating stone.
The Caprini façade was to be extremely influential: the basic idea,
already adumbrated in the Castellesi palace, of a contrast between rusticated
substrate and smooth, becolumned elevated section, occurs with some
regularity on palace façades throughout early modern Europe. The design
was perhaps literally emblematic in terms of the semantic field of the term
"emblema" in the original Greek: contemporary writers, fascinated by

etymology, noted the meaning "graft," the artful joining into a living
organism of a rootstock and a whip from distinct species, one robust but
crude, the other fragile but splendidly fruitful and/or floriferous thanks to
human improvement or at least selection. 30 The opposition of art/culture and

of the concepts "art" and "nature. "31
The grafting in the Caprini façade of representations of culture (the
architectural order) and of nature (the simulation in stucco of rough stone
in the basement story) raises important issues of interpretation. The order,
a privileged marker of the observance of classical design principles,
symbolizes a realm of culture identified not only through inherent
associations but also, perhaps especially, through its position relative to its
contrary, its "other," the rough rocky surface beneath.32 But the latter, a
zone of nonculture, is nevertheless included within what had seemed to be
defined through its exclusion. Here rustication is not only positionally
defined as nonculture within a specific cultural domain, it is also itself a
significant and necessary constituent of that domain. 33 In addition, needless
to say, the general validity of the architectural langage of classicism, as
asserted in treatise after treatise, depends on its quality of naturalness, here
a matter not of formal mimesis, so muchas of organic growth process. To
return to the metaphor of grafting, it is as if the order, rather than quarried
violently from "living rock," had grown and drew sustenance from its rustic
substrate; indeed, Bramante's rustication design subtly reconciled the
otherwise sharply disjoined strata of the façade. 34
lt is not surprising that imitations of the Palazzo Caprini shed the
condensed complexity of the original. For an early case we have only to cast
a glance across the via Alessandrina. The Palazzo Jacopo da Brescia,
designed by Raphael for a papal doctor, certainly constitutes a tribute to
Bramante's model, but not without significant differences, in particular the
restoration of an indexical relationship between interior and exterior-there
is now a quite prominent attic story-and the triumphal arch motif on the
side façade that addresses, not the transcendent social values seemingly
addressed by Bramante's façade, but the ceremonial aspect of the city in the
theater state of Pope Leo X. 35 lt also acts as an especially conspicuous
framing motif for the main façade.

under Leo X expressed in architectural terms the sophistication, love of

the convex motif of each column. Over the windows was a line of portrait
medallions, tentatively identified by Frommel as popes.40 The eagle in the
center referred to L'Aquila, birthplace of Branconio, whose passion for
numismatics was surely indexed by the medallions, perhaps as a major part
of the evocation in the palace's ornament of the patron's cultural horizons,
or even-like the imagery on contemporary medallions discussed by Russell
-casting the façade itself as a kind of emblematic portrait of Branconio.41
The latter was one of Raphael's closest friends; the originality and
magnificence of his palace therefore may have testified not only to a desire
to enhance the Medicean Rome of Leo X (the palace conspicuously carries
the Medici arms), but also to the friendship between architect and patron,
indeed to the general significance of amicizia in their milieu.42
Above the richly stuccoed zone of the medallions, the attic story
contained fiat panels, appropriate for frescoed scenes, between the
windows.43 We are now in a position to appreciate not only the formai
complexity of the overall composition, but also more interesting

[Page 88]

complexities. First, there was a contrast of different media. The niches in
the piano nobile contained statues, so that this floor was distinguished by
three-dimensional figurai sculpture.44 Above the niches, the band of
medallions displayed portrait busts in low relief. Finally, the attic panels
were decorated with frescoes, such as were by now highly fashionable in
Rome, though not on the most prestigious façades.
Such a contrast of media, if fully executed, would have accompanied
a contrast of modes of representation. The portrait busts, the only part of the
decoration we can be sure was executed, refer to individuals, presumably
historical figures linked in some way. If statues were foreseen for the
niches, they were surely not further embodiments of such individuals, but
rather allegorical figures, perhaps virtues exemplified by the heroes and
paragons in question. Finally, the attic panels would most naturally have
contained histories, perhaps events from the lives of the individuals
concerned, in which their virtues were displayed or put to the test. 45 All this
is highly conjectural, but involves only a localized concentration of types of
façade decoration, just as the via Alessandrina had already displayed a
localized concentration of façade types.
Especially remarkable about the Branconio façade, on this reading, would
have been its juxtaposition of distinct orders of expression or rather
semiosis, evidently intended to produce an overriding meaning. Such a
juxtaposition recalls the working of the canonical impresa, a correspondence
that it is not surprising in a cultural milieu marked, e.g., by the presence of
Paolo Giovio, whose importance in the history of the impresa or indeed
emblematics needs no emphasis. It is further, significant that writers on
emblematics, whose predilection for etymology 1 have noted, in particular
stress the sense of "emblem" as "inlay," the insertion into a composition of
varied elements of varying origin and type to produce a new aesthetic effect
transcending the inherent values of the constituent parts. 46 Already Alberti
had used the model of mosaic of the appropriation and recombination of
shards of a shattered antiquity involved in classicizing architectural design. 47
The contrast of two senses of "emblem," graft and inlay, within the
semantic field of the term provides a useful basis for the discussion of the
iconographical, or rather semiotic, dimension of contrasts in architectural
expression. On the one hand, the Caprini façade signifies through the
representation of a social hierarchy spatially expressed within the palace and
metonymically obtaining, according to the current belief system, within
society, if not the universe, as a whole. In this semiotic economy the façade
embodies, as much as signifies, the signified, and architecture is centrally

[Page 89]
The Emblem and Architecture 65
and reflexively implicated in the production of meaning through its
synecdochic representation of itself as a emblem of culture in general; we
have to do here, in short, with the illusion, at least, of a natural sign.48
The Branconio façade, on the other hand, excludes the architectural
sign-the column-from the work of signification, for which it merely
provides a scaffold, a supporting framework. Though the façade design still
privileges the piano nobile, it presents a surface for the artfully composed
inscription of visual elements that signify, as we noted, in diverse ways,
thus drawing attention to the conventionality of the ope ration of signification
of the façade as a whole. Shortly before Raphael's invention of the
Branconio façade, indeed, the conventionality of linguistic terms was
emphasized, for instance (but not for the first time), in the De inventione
dialectica of Rudolph Agricola, published in 1515.49 Agricola's highly
influential text and Raphael's conspicuous, even polemical façade appear
both to be symptoms of what Regina Stefaniak has characterized as a general
shift in early modern European culture from the natural to the conventional
sign;50 both privilege rhetoric, in the sense of effective discourse, over the
expression of ultimate truths.
The Branconio and Caprini façades, for all their differences, shared
a formal and semiotic density that seems to have inhibited emulation. Far
more influential than either was a more flexible, less complex schema
developed by Raphael's contemporary Antonio da Sangallo. Sangallo
conceived of an expansive but relatively unadorned façade centered on a
portal emphasized by rustication or an aedicula. Otherwise the blank
stuccoed wall was articulated solely by lines of impressive windows and
framed by emphatic quoining on the corners. This schema conferred
Florentine elegance and proportionality on the basic Roman façade type of
the fifteenth century, that of the Palazzo Della Rovere. lt might be used as
a relatively undifferentiated field for façade decoration, as at Sangallo's
Palazzo Baldassini (this decoration, now lost without trace, may of course
not have been intended by Sangallo).51 In the most important example, the
Palazzo Farnese, it seems clear that façade decoration was never executed
(Fig. 8). Nor did this façade schema admit emblematic material or even
character; indeed, this may have been a factor in the famous altercation
about Michelangelo's monstrous comice for the Palazzo Farnese, which was
surely not simply a matter of dis agreement about formai design elements.
This comice included such solecistic elements as lions' heads and heraldic
lilies, and was roundly condemned by the supporters of the recently
deceased Sangallo. 52

The intensity of the opposition to Michelangelo's comice was rooted,
in my view, in an intense debate about the expressive character of
architecture, especially secular architecture, and the place of broadly
emblematic and other signifiers. Michelangelo and Sangallo came to
represent extreme positions in this debate, though the issues involved had
gained urgency, as we saw, in the development of the via Alessandrina.
Again there are intimations of the debate in Alberti, whose intense interest
in hieroglyphic or emblematic devices ran counter, in his architectural
thinking or even practice, to his hierarchical distinction of beauty and
ornament (in general, emblems fall under the latter category), and in
particular the insistence on purity and the strict control of ornament in the
design of shrines, the highest task of architecture.53 In Rome, Bramante
maintained a similar principle, to judge at least from the austere choir of S.
Maria del Popolo;54 Sangallo, the consummate architectural professional and
upholder of specifically architectural solutions in design projects, extended
the principle to conspicuous commissions in the secular domain.
The altercation over the Palazzo Farnese façade exacerbated, then, a
long-evolving friction or even contradiction within Renaissance architectural
practice. Architectural classicism, as raised to canonicity in the Renaissance,
deployed a powerful formal system encoding and legitimated by general,
indeed transcendental, values, though certainly fraught with ideological
resonance. On the other hand, the realization of architectural norms in
determinate functional and social contexts often involved the assignment to
architecture of a supplemental role as support for signs, including emblems,
conveying and celebrating particular(ist) interests and pretensions. Though
largely absent from the Renaissance reflection on architecture, the issue is
at least implicit in the portal designs of Serlio's Libro extraordinario. These
stage, as noted above, a collision of nature and art/architecture, most vividly
in the portal embodying what Serlio calls a "bestial order," in which
architectural elements suffer invasion by raw rocks, pulsing with life and the
grotesque grimaces of monstrous beings, in ablatant departure from the type
of the harmonious house-as-portrait realized, as we saw, in Roman courtly
residences (Fig. 9).55
If the invasive rusticated blocks of Serlio's portals symbolize the
resistance of nature to architectural ordering, no less "natural" are the social
dynamics evoked by the tablets and cartouches that also encroach on
Vitruvian proportion and formal values. In the face of social exigencies and
cultural fragmentation, Serlio presents architecture as a utopian discourse,
concealed within-but also, as he defensively stresses, in principle

[Page 91]

recuperable from-execrescences that can no longer be contained or
accommodated in the Vitruvian system. 56 Bramante' s reconciliation of art
and nature collapses, and the model of a harmonious house-as-portrait yields
to visual intimations of fragmented and conflicted subjectivities lurking
beyond the imagined portals of Serlio's text. But in retrospect we can see
how the latter also hints, paradoxically, at the prospect of the absolutist
assignment to architectural classicism of new emblematic and rhetorical
dimensions, concomitant with a more assertive and straightforward
trenchancy in the inscription of ideological values in the built environment.

The Baroque refectory of the Archabbey of Pannonhalma, in those

and drinking; in fact, the refectory was as sacred a place as the oratory.2

Strong contrasts characterize not only Baroque art but also Baroque

enough food. On one occasion, the monks locked themselves in the chapter

zation, although he <lied with a psalm on his lips. His life was not unlike
that of his fellow monk, poet, scholar and lexicographer, Gergely Czuczor,
who was imprisoned in irons for six years after the Hungarian revolution of

The third factor was education in Hungary. In 1802 the Austrian
emperor and Hungarian King, Francis I. readmitted the Benedictine Order
in Hungary, which had been suppressed by Joseph II. in 1780. The order

was given the mission of teaching and operating schools. Since that date
Benedictine Ortler has maintained secondary schools in Hungary, and every

comparatively large and up-to-date library, not only, or even primarily, in
theology, but in the sciences and humanities as well. At present the library
has about 350 000 volumes.
The oblong ground-plan of the neo-classicist building of the library in
the 1820s resembles that of a Roman Basilica with aisles and galleries. In
the 1830s, a transverse arm with semicircular termination was added like a
transept. The library building happens to point eastwards as did medieval
Christian churches. In the middle of the "transept," there is a lantern with
windows and light-reflecting mirrors, which create the ray of light, symbolic
of the enlightment and the function of the library. Under the lantern, there
are four allegorical female figures, which represent the traditional university
faculties. The allegorical figures have the appropriate attributes: theology
with cross, Bible and the two tablets of the ten commandments; jurispru
dence with sword, scales, scrolls and a book with the letters PP, referring
to the paragraphs or sections of the law (Fig. 10); philosophy with globe,
telescope; medicine with a Aescalapius' snake entwinned staff and a bee
hive. The allegorical figures befit a church of sciences and scholarship. Yet,
the fact that the "transept" was added later, and that the famous contempo
rary writer and critic, Ferenc Kazinczy, referred to the library as
"Bibliotheca palotâja" [the palace of Bibliotheca] while his friend, the
Benedictine monk Izidor Guzmics, called it "fünséges uj kônyvtâr" [the
magnificent new library ]26 makes it clear that neither the monks nor the
architects consciously set out to build the library as a church of sciences.
Nonetheless that was the outcome.
On the tunnel vault of the oblong "nave" of the library, in contrast to
the oval cupola of the Baroque refectory, we find the fresco of Minerva or
Pallas Athene, "the intelligence of Olympos," "reason," the goddess of
enlightenment, of sciences, crafts and abundance (Fig. 4). Three young boys
surround her, one of them with the cornucopia, another with a tablet with
geometrical figures on it, and yet another with a book. On the two gables
are full figures of Greek and Roman philosophers and writers with symbols
and with their names. On the two axial walls are portraits of Hungarian
Kings, statesmen and writers.

Here I should mention that classicism became widespread in Hungary

abandonded as a required second language in high schools.

The Relation between Decoration and Books in Early
Modern Libraries: Three Examples from Germany and
Austria
ERIC GARBERSON
Smith College
It is often asserted, or merely assumed, that from the Middle Ages through
the eighteenth century the decoration of libraries was linked directly to the
arrangement of the contents. As such, the decoration is said to serve as a
sort of "pictorial catalogue" or guide for the location of books. This view
is taken in one of the few general surveys of library decoration, André
Masson's The Pictorial Catalogue: Mural Decoration in Libraries.1 It also
appears as an unsupported claim in studies of individual libraries. In many
instances such a direct correlation did exist, as demonstrated by the
well-documented examples cited by Masson (such as the Bodleian at
Oxford). In other instances, however, no such correlation existed and the
decoration served instead to make a broader statement about the nature and
goals of the scholarship conducted in the library. This was especially true
in later libraries where a direct correlation was rendered impractical by the
increased size and scope of the holdings, the unequal sizes of individual
classifications, and changes in furnishings and decorative conventions. Like
the use of decoration as a "pictorial catalogue," this polemical use of library
decoration was based, at least in part, on the authority of ancient textual
sources.
To demonstrate these points within a restricted region, I will discuss
three libraries, two of them monastic, constructed in Germany and Austria
during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: the libraries of the
Augustinian canons at Eberhardsclausen and the Premonstratensians at
Roggenburg and the castle library at Windhag. These examples represent the
sum of what we know of decorative programs executed in German and
Austrian libraries prior to the late seventeenth century. 2 Furthermore, in
each instance it is possible to establish, on the basis of internai evidence and
textual sources, whether or not the decoration corresponded directly to the
books.
A number of widely scattered passages in diverse but easily accessible
ancient texts provided much of the basis for early modern conventions of
library decoration. By 1656, the majority of these had been assembled and

[Page 132]
108 Eric Garberson
interpreted by Edmund Figrel in his book on statues of famous Romans, in
a chapter that was republished in 1702. 3 As yet there is no systematic study
of how the se sources might be reflected in the decoration of the few libraries
that survive from the preceding centuries, an undertaking well beyond the
scope of the present discussion. In general, the passages assembled by Figrel
and his running commentary indicate an awareness that the images of
famous and learned men and of deities placed in ancient libraries were used
to convey a message more complex than simply where to find the books.
According to Figrel, these portraits served in most instances to honor those
portrayed. In other instances, such images were placed in libraries to signal
the owner's "love of good letters" and to inspire others to acquire the virtue
and learning of those depicted, while images of deities presided over the
activities conducted in the library. 4 Indicating the location of the contents
appears in Figrel' s text as a secondary function of portraits and figura!
decoration, one also discharged by symbols on the cases.
While Figrel cannot be used to interpret fifteenth- and sixteenth-century
libraries, his compendium of sources suggests that we examine their decora
tion for the broadest possible array of meanings. In fact, the polemical and
inspirational functions cited by Figrel do often appear in the decoration of
medieval and Renaissance libraries throughout Europe, which included por
traits as well as other images. Whatever its link to the arrangement of the
books, such decoration seems nearly always to have conveyed a specific
message, directed to readers and visitors alike, about the nature of the
library' s collection and the scholarship it was to serve. 5 This is certainly the
case in the three libraries selected for discussion here.
The Augustinian library at Eberhardsclausen near Trier is a small room
constructed and decorated just after 1500. 6 The frescoes are located high on
the walls in the lunettes, well above the level of the original furnishings
which were probably lecterns rather than the wall shelves seen in photo
graphs published in 1930. 7 The principal elements of the frescoes are
figures, mostly portraits, grouped three or four to a lunette. Each is
identified by a titulus and surrounded by banderoles with inscriptions, many
now illegible. Beginning on the north wall, these figures represent Moses,
David, and John the Evangelist; Emperor, Pope, and Thomas Aquinas. On
the south wall are grouped Augustine, Jerome, and Gregory; Ambrose, John
Chrysostom, and Bernard of Clairvaux. On the west wall appear Gerson,
Francis, Dominic, and Nicholas of Tolentino, then the historian Flavius
Josephus, the Virgin and Child in a mandorla, and an Augustinian canon

with an inscribed tablet. Between the windows on the east wall traces of the
crucified Christ are still faintly visible.
These figures cannot have had any direct correlation with the books,
whatever their arrangement in the original furnishings may have been. The
portraits are grouped in a manner that does not evidently correspond to book
classifications8 and include both individuals who left no writings (David,
Christ, the Virgin) and generic figures representing an office or station
(Emperor, Pope, monk). Furthermore, the many inscriptions do not signal
the location of books and make only general references to specific classifica
tions. Instead, they, like the figures, are part of a broader statement about
the library's role in the life of the monastery.
The role of study in monastic life had been highly contested throughout
the centuries and would continue to be until the end of the eighteenth
century. The Augustinian canons at Eberhardsclausen were clearly taking a
position in the debate by quoting on the monk's tablet a passage from Saint
Bernard's thirty-sixth sermon on the Song of Songs, a well-known text by
an important figure in the polemics on monastic learning.9 In this passage,
Bernard asserted that the benefits of intellectual activity to religious life were
dependent upon the manner in which one approached such activity:
And what does that manner imply? It implies the order, the
application, and the sense of purpose with which one approach
es the object of study. The order implies that we give prece
dence to all that aids spiritual progress; the application that we
pursue more eagerly all that strengthens love more; and the
purpose that we pursue it not through vain-glory or curiosity,
or any base motive, but for the welfare of oneself or one's
neighbor. 10
The polemical-inspirational function of the decoration was reinforced by
placing this passage in the hands of a figure who could stand generically for
the monks of the house. In this way it signaled to the visitor the manner in
which the community approached its study, while providing a persona!
reminder to members of the community not to go astray in their studies.
The debates about monastic learning were directly related to another
important current in Christian thought, the sapiential or wisdom tradition,
stretching back through Thomas Aquinas and Augustine to the Pauline
Epistles and ultimately to the wisdom books of the Old Testament.11
Central to this tradition was the opposition between mere human knowledge

[Page 134]
110 Eric Garberson
and the true or divine wisdom revealed to the virtuous and faithful. The
New Testament transformed the personified wisdom of the Old Testament
into the salvation to be obtained through faith in the supreme mystery of
Christ' s incarnation and crucifixion, notions developed and expanded in
subsequent generations of Christian thought.
Sapiential images were an important part of early modern library
decoration and were juxtaposed with other elements to demonstrate the
opposition between acquired knowledge and revealed wisdom. 12 Two of the
most important of these images in bath libraries and the wisdom tradition
were precisely the two included at Eberhardsclausen, the crucified Christ
and the Vir gin. They were privileged among the many "sources of wisdom"
[fontes sapientiae] listed in Claude Clément's library treatise of 1635 as
appropriate for the decoration of libraries. Clément supported their inclusion
with extensive citations from several centuries of wisdom literature and
proposed inscriptions to signal their significance. The figures at Eberhards
clausen may have borne inscriptions similar to those proposed by Clément:
for the Virgin "seat of wisdom" [sedes sapientiae], for the Crucifixion "in
which is contained all sources and science of God," a tag from Colossians
2:3.13
The remaining legible inscriptions on the banderoles reinforce the
program's polemical and inspirational message. While the works of some of
th ose portrayed may have been found in the library, the inscriptions suggest
that these individuals were selected as examples of scholars who sought
divine wisdom through scholarship properly conducted and employed. For
example, over Moses we read an abbreviation of Deuteronomy 8,3: "man
does not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the
mouth of the Lord. "14 Above Nicholas of Tolentino we read: "in all our
studies ... by the grace of God ... we seek the edification of our neighbors." 15
Sorne of these individuals were obviously selected to associate the library
with the foundations of Church scholarship (the four Latin and one Greek
Father, Thomas Aquinas), and some reflect the specific interests and
allegiances of the community. For example, Augustine was the Order's
supposed founder and Bernard's position on study in monastic life was
quoted at length elsewhere in the decoration. The rationale behind the
grouping of some figures is traditional (the Church Fathers), of others less
clear. The most unusual grouping, that of Emperor, Pope, and Aquinas,
could possibly refer to the three authorities under which any monastic
library operated: the religious authority of the Church, the political authority
of the Empire, and the intellectual authority of Thomism. 16 The images of

Emperor and Pope may also have had particular associations, now unrecov
erable, with the current holders of those offices.
Thus, the portraits and other elements of the se frescoes served to inspire
and educate the library's monastic readers and to make an important polemi
cal or propagandistic statement about the library to outs ide visitors. In this
case, the more prosaic function of indicating the location of books was
probably discharged by other means, such as tituli or other images placed
on the furnishings themselves or lower on the walls. The next example, the
contemporary library at Roggenburg, will show that all three functions could
be carried out by a single decorative program, one that also drew the all
important sapiential opposition between human knowledge and revealed
wisdom.
The frescoes in the Premonstratensian library at Roggenburg in Swabia
were painted in 1520. Though long since destroyed, the library is known
from two detailed manuscript descriptions composed around 1700 and a
printed description of 1734-1736, all apparently based on earlier texts .17
The library was a rectangular room on an upper floor, with frescoes on at
least three walls and desks for storage and consultation along one side. On
one short wall appeared an image of the Crucified Christ flanked by veiled
figures of Synagogue and Ecclesia; the desks most likely faced this wall, so
that the monks had this important image constantly before them as they
studied. On the long wall opposite the desks was the equally important
image of the Virgin as Mother of God flanked by Saints Paul and Catherine,
two individuals who, as the inscriptions noted, combined eloquence and
learning in the service of religion. 18
On the other long wall, above the lecterns, were eleven separate
frescoes. According to the descriptions, each consisted of portraits relevant
to the branch of learning housed below. The classifications, standard for a
medieval library, were the following, beginning from the desk closest to the
image of Christ: Bibles, Church Fathers, Scholastic Theologians, Moral
Theologians, Canon and Civil Lawyers, Historians, Mystic Theologians,
Preachers, Philosophers, Orators, Poets, Grammarians, and finally
Miscellanea. Most of the groups consisted exclusively of representative
exponents of a given branch of learning, such as Moses, David, Matthew,
and John for scripture, the Four Church fathers for themselves, or Aristotle,
Albertus Magnus, Hypocrates, and Ptolemy for the philosophers. Pliny
alone stood for the historians, but was accompanied by personifications of
time and truth. 19

Furthermore, each of the eleven frescoes bore two inscriptions, one a
biblical or classical citation, the other a Latin verse that made direct
reference to the class of books housed below. On the first the citation was
from John 1,17: "The Law is given by Moses, Grace only by Christ." The
accompanying verse read: "In the foremost chest lie sacred volumes/ which
drive out idleness and give the means to salvation" [Prima iacent summis

statement about the role of that class in a program of monastic scholarship.
Optional even in the Middle Ages and Renaissance, the direct correla
tion between decoration and books seems to have become increasingly rare
as libraries expanded in size and scope following the ad vent of printing. By
the late sixteenth century in ltaly and Spain the medieval lecterns, presses,
and rudimentary wall shelves had been replaced with a comprehensive
system of furnishings more efficient in the storage of large numbers of
books. This transition occurred somewhat later in Germany and Austria.21
While the direct correlation between books and decoration seems to have
been easily abandoned in ltalian libraries and at the Escorial, 22 the three
rooms of the library at Windhag, decorated between 1642 and 1673, show
a slow realization that this correlation had become untenable, leading to its
graduai abandonment.
The library at Windhag was constructed by Joachim von Enzmilner, a
migrant from Swabia who made his economic and political fortune in the
service of the Hapsburg Counter-reformation. 23 The castle was destroyed
shortly after his death by his sole heir, a daughter, who tore it down for
materials to build a convent, which, in turn, was soon destroyed by light
ning. The appearance of the library and most elements of its decoration are
documented in the two editions of the Topographia Windhagiana, a pains
takingly detailed and illustrated description composed by Enzmilner's
Dominican librarian, Hyacinth Marian. 24
The library consisted of three interconnecting rooms. In each the walls
were completely covered by simple, unornamented cases. As a result, figur
ai decoration was restricted to fields of various sizes defined by stucco
frames on the vault and in the window lunettes (Figs. 1 and 2).
From Marian' s convoluted description in the Topographia Windhagiana,
I have been able to reconstruct, albeit partially, the general arrangement of
both the books and the decoration. 25 The books were divided by publication
date, with those published before 1550 in the first room, those published
between 1550 and 1650 in the second and those published after 1650 in the

third. Within each room books were shelved according to a system based on
the traditional faculties and which varied slightly in each. The application
of this system in the first room is shown schematically in figure two. No
diagram is provided for the second room as Marian's description cannot be

In the first two rooms an author portrait was painted inside the lunette
over each window, with further portraits in the window embrasure below.
These portraits related to the book class in the adjacent shelves, although
they often fell between classes, as shown in figure two. In the third room
author portraits appeared only in the window embrasures. The portraits in
the window embrasures appear to have been portable, probably painted, and
varied in number from class to class, reflecting an ongoing project to
assemble the portraits of authors represented in the collection. In general,
the life-dates of all the individuals portrayed suggest that their books would
most likely have been housed in that room.
Only in the first room, constructed between 1642 and 1656, did the
decoration of the ceiling relate to the book classes. That this relationship
was intentional is clear from Marian's description, which was written as a
running monologue addressed to a visitor strolling through the library with
him. The four large fields on the axis of the vault contained images relevant
to and thus "representing" the principal book classifications: the Council of
Trent for Theology, the Imperial Diet or Reichstag for Law, Herodotus
reading to an assembled crowd for History, Archimedes blithely working
through the fall of Syracuse for the Liberal Arts. In this room portraits
lower on the vault were also associated with the book classes and included
two personifications of activities associated with History (painting and
typography).
As the schematic diagram shows, the book classes varied greatly in size
and thus occupied differing numbers of cases. Within the structure of the
room, no book class occupied a clearly delineated area as had been the case
at Roggenburg where each class occupied a single lectern. Consequently, the
relationship between the books and the decoration was neither direct nor
very evident. The decoration thus indicated the books' location only approxi
mately and not without ambiguity. Even the portraits in the window embra
sures could, in some cases, relate to more than one classification, especially
when the individual portrayed was active in more than one area.

ln the second room, constructed between 1656 and 1660, the decoration
was applied to an identical set of fields on the vault. Aside from the
portraits in the lunettes, the decoration bore no direct relation to the books.
lnstead it consisted of two sets of images generically appropriate to the
library of an intellectually engaged Catholic nobleman: new discoveries and
inventions alongside images from the sapiential or wisdom tradition. While
individual images could conceivably have related to specific book class, both
series were spread out over the entire ceiling and were meant to refer to the
library as a whole.
The rectangular fields along the center of the vault contained depictions
of new discoveries intended to demonstrate the accomplishments of past
scholars and to inspire future generations. The theme was announced by the
inscription "nova reperta," the title of a series of prints published by
Stradanus from which the images were borrowed.26 These included the
discovery of America by Christopher Columbus and Amerigo Vespucci with
the discovery of the "lapidus polaris" (compass) by Flavius Amalfitanus; the
invention of gunpowder; the invention of clockwork; the discovery of the
medicinal plants guaicum and sassafrass and the invention of distillation; the
invention of glass manufacture, cutting, and painting; the discovery and
cultivation of silk worms with the smuggling of silk out of China; and the
invention of the saddle and stirrup.
The sapiential images were painted in nine of the twelve fields between
the window lunettes. These included the two prime images seen at Eber

Child (the Word made flesh), and the crucified Christ. Each bore an inscrip

wisdom and most divine book" [Sedes sapientiae ac Divinissimus Liber],
while Christ's inscription reminded the viewer that "the divine library is in
the cross" [ln Cruce divina bibliotheca]. The remaining seven fields repre
sented the Seven Gifts of the Holy Spirit in Old and New Testament
narratives.
Only the images in the three remaining fields between the lunettes seem
to have had any relation to the books below. Over the medical books was
painted an anatomical theater. Over the history books was an image of the
duel at the Reichstag of 929. The final image, a pontificating bishop sur
rounded by musical instruments, seems of a kind with these two, although
its relationship to the philosophy and ethics books probably housed below
it is less clear.

The decoration of the third room, executed sometime between 1668 and
1673, was divided into three unevenly distributed series completely indepen
dent of the book classes below. The first, painted mainly along the center
of the vault, consisted of contemporary events showing the triumph of the
Catholic faith: the abdication and conversion of Queen Christina in 1654;
the Bohemian uprising of 1618 and the assassination of Wallenstein; the
English Civil W ar of 1649 with the assassination of Charles 1 and Charles
Il's coronation and the execution of Cromwell in 1660; and the abdication
of the Polish king John II Casimir in 1667. Two events from this se ries, the
Peace of Westphalia of 1648 and the Turkish Peace of 1665 were painted
in the window lunettes. The other four lunettes held scenes showing the
history and customs of China borrowed from Athanasius Kircher's China
/llustrata of 1667 and Martino Martini's De Bello Tartarico of 1654.
Between the lunettes were four images showing the principal European
nations and their most outstanding inventions, natural inclinations, and
characteristics.
The successive stages of decoration in the three rooms at Windhag
reflect a realization that the relation between ceiling decoration and books
housed in wall furnishings was not viable. By the third room, this was given
up entirely, except for the author portraits hung in the window embrasures.
Although the decoration changed slightly as it was adapted to the new
situation, it maintained the basic conventions of library decoration seen in
earlier libraries such as Eberhardsclausen and Roggenburg and as set forth
in Clément and Figrel on the basis of readily available ancient sources. In
the first room all the elements of the decoration were chosen in the spirit of
ancient author portraits, whether they were in fact portraits or not. Here the
images indicated the location of a given class of books (albeit approxima
tely), conveyed something about its contents, and associated the library with
the classically grounded scholastic-humanist tradition propagated by the
Catholic Reform, in the service of which Enzmilner had made his fortune.
The decoration of the second and third rooms gave up the direct connection
of non-portrait images with the book classes and focused instead on convey
ing a broader message about the library's place in the scholastic-humanist
tradition of the moment. Here Enzmilner proclaimed his allegiance to the
sapiential opposition between acquired knowledge and revealed wisdom
staunchly defended in Catholic thought. He also proclaimed his pride in
Catholicism's victories while at the same time demonstrating his awareness
of the expansion of knowledge through invention and discovery.

Subsequently, most libraries abandoned a direct correlation between
books and decoration, except in some cases when the decoration consisted
principally of author portraits.27 For example, in the sumptuous monastic
libraries constructed in the South German and Austrian monasteries during
the eighteenth century the decoration conveys a much broader message about
the nature and scope of monastic scholarship. The sapiential opposition
between human knowledge and revealed wisdom remained of fundamental
importance, as did the need to engage issues raised by increasingly heated
polemics about the validity of monastic scholarship and the role of
monasticism in contemporary society. 28
These three examples demonstrate the main issues in library decoration
during the early modern period, at least in Germany and Austria. Further
investigation of libraries in ltaly, where more are known from the fifteenth
and sixteenth centuries, should confirm and expand the basic picture
sketched here. The correlation of decoration to book classes was possible,
although not obligatory, in libraries so long as the number of books was
limited and each class restricted to a single lectern. Thereafter, the
decoration most often served its other functions of inspiring readers and
making a statement to visitors about the nature of the library and the
scholarship conducted there.

Castle Bevern
In 1667, Duke Ferdinand Albrecht of Braunschweig and Lüneburg took pos
session of the castle of Bevern, renovated, and reconstructed it. 1 It served
to accommodate his library as well as his art collection and precious pieces
for a Kunstkammer. Most of these had been collected during his travels.2
Without any political power, in constant conflict with his family, the Duke
retired to Bevern and devoted all his time to the care and the enlargement
of his collections. 3 The interior decoration of the castle was completed
according to his precise instructions. It followed a programme of pictures
and proverbs that was to a great extent emblematical and that was meant to
express his self-understanding and form a self-portrayal.
The decorative programme of Castle Bevern described here does not
exist any longer, but it can be reconstructed thanks to a thorough description
provided by the Duke himself. Castle Bevern, however, is still mainly pre
served in its substance, and it is considered to be one of the finest examples
of the so-called Weserrenaissance in Niedersachsen. The castle was erected
between 1603 and 1612 for Statius von Münchhausen, who was a diplomat
and financing agent for the Dukes of Wolfenbüttel. Münchhausen went
bankrupt in 1619 and the castle was taken under sequestration by Duke
August but Münchhausen was allowed to live there until his death in 1633.
Later the secluded "Fürstlich Braunschweig-lüneburgische Ambt- und
Jagthaus" was used as a hunting seat by Herzog August.4 In 1667 Duke
Ferdinand Albrecht moved to Bevern and the castle was renovated under his
direction. 5 The interior decoration was carried out by the court painter and
decorator Johannes Fabarius6 but the Duke himself was responsible for the
inventio of the emblematical and allegorical programmes. U sually, a cour
tier, the court chaplan, or the Hofmeister would have been charged with the
task of inventing a panegyric programme.7 However, Duke Ferdinand
Albrecht had all the skills to do it himself, since he was well-educated and

Function and Meaning
After Ferdinand Albrecht's death, during later renovation work the
decoration of the castle of Bevern was radically changed. In particular, the
emblematic decoration was completely destroyed, because the following
generation no longer understood and appreciated it. Unfortunately, no
contemporary copperplates of the interior remain, there is only Merian's

[Page 153]
The Emblem and Architecture 129
exterior view of the castle.30 This means that the description made by the
Duke himself is the only testimony that we have for the emblematic
programme and its meaning. He had published it because he evidently
considered it an essential part of his collection.
Duke Ferdinand Albrecht' s description shows that the pictures and
emblems had a fixed relation to the function of the room. The texts
proverbs and bible quotations-played an overall important role. Comprising
different colours and displayed so as to be noticed at once, they required of
the visitors-and, of course, of the inhabitants of the castle-that they read
and reflect the inscribed doctrines. Probably the circles brought together in
the castle were meant to reflect on these proverbs, on their meaning, and on
their moral and edifying qualities. Thus the emblems and Bible quotations
had a communicative function similar to that of the emblems in the
Nuremberg town hall31 or to other emblematic programmes in academies
or universities. 32 But whereas there an academic competition could be
carried out through the invention of new inscriptiones or complete new
emblems here the aim was the communication of edifying thoughts.
This privilege of the text over the picture is reflected in the Duke's
description. Whereas the picturae-that are not reproduced33-are just
described in brief, all the text elements are printed word for word. It can be
assumed that in the rooms themselves the concrete layout of the texts had
precedence. Probably the picturae of the emblems were not composed very
artistically. What was at stake was the recognition of the depicted res, not
the artistic rendering. Thus the picture served the text, it reproduced the
object to which the reflection referred.
This complies perfectly with the strict theory of images described by
Protestant doctrine.34 The pictures were never to stand alone but were
always to be accompanied by interpretation or commentary provided by
inscriptiones or subscriptiones. Even the depicted objects and figures belong
to a syntax through their emblematical or allegorical meanings: they are
open to reading and interpretation. 35 Even where they are picturae they are
first and foremost texts, regarded as ornaments-in the sense a decorum
they serve rhetorically to explain the functions of the rooms and their
meanings.

The Description of the Emblematic Decorations
The description begins with the inscriptions on the gates that were
meant to introduce the visitor in the general to the castle and of its
proprietor. From the outset the castle focusses perception through emblems
and allegories on a fundamental theme. The inscriptio EVOLEMUS
INEVITABILE [lnevitably we must die] introduces two emblems that on the
one hand show a youth rising above a city with winged feet holding cross
and Bible, and on the other hand death with a scythe from behind whose
ribs a child appears. This symbolizes the omnipresence of death that from
birth on is the telos of all human life and it reminds us of the vanity of all
earthly values.
The castle court is paved with 706 large square stones from the nearby
hills of the Sôlling (" grossen Sôllingschen Quater Leichsteinen [large square
gravestones]").36 A memorial stone calls this to our attention. Its inscrip
tion gives both the interpretation and the application in the form of a
memento mori.
Behold! Such stones are seals of steady mortality
Die hourly, die blessed, certainty harms the soul
To die gently suits those without the heavy stone of sin
May the Redeemer's treasures be the comfort of such souls.37
Thus everyone who enters is immediately confronted with the main theme
of the allegorical decoration of the castle. All the rooms, from the church
to the library, from the Kunstkammer, the picture gallery to the public
rooms and the private rooms are covered with inscriptions, emblems,
pictures that are an endless variation on a single theme: the lament over the
instability and fragility of the world, the admonition about the vanity of
earthly glamour and glory, and the futility of all human action, all of which
require one to lead a consistently Christian Protestant life under the sign and
in the face of death.38

The Kunstkammer
The focus of the Duke's possessions, apart from the library and the
picture collection, was his Kunstkammer. Duke Ferdinand Albrecht acquired
numerous valuable and rare pieces, especially during his travels.53 His
description constitutes a systematic inventory, which is probably recorded
in the order of aquisition. 54 Ferdinand Albrecht visited several famous
Kunstkammern on his travels through Europe, for example in Strasbourg,
Basle, Ambras, Bologna, Milan, Venice, Florence, Torino, and Rome.55
As his descriptions show, he was well aware of the contemporary interpreta
tion of the Kunstkammer as a representation of the macrocosm and its secret
composition. The individual and disparate pieces that in a Kunstkammer
collection are brought together in an apparently artificial order in fact
represent a higher order or system. They form a model of the world,
examples of all that exists in the mundane sphere. All items of the micro
cosm stand for the secret and hidden order of creation and thus allegorically
represent the macrocosm. As parts of visible reality those fragments and

[Page 158]
134 Sabine Môdersheim
"curiosities" indicate another meaning beyond the surface and thus function
like emblematic res.
Thus every Kunstkammer in itself forms an emblem of the world, an
allegory of the cosmos as a microform of the microcosm. The collection
stands as an emblem of the visible world in the diversity of its
appearances.56 lt points beyond itself because the collected and documented
singular appearances-which only document particular and outstanding
forms57-refer to finitude. Their order is a passing one. In the context of
the Kunstkammer an artificial order, representative of the real world, can be
set up. This order in miniature symbolizes the order of a complete
cosmos.58
The contract drawn up for the heirs of the castle and the Kunstkammer
stipulated that both the form and order of the collection should not be
changed and that it should be passed on from generation to generation,
untouched in perpituity. 59 On the one hand, Ferdinand Albrecht repressed
the fact that this project of an eternal Kunstkammer was destined to fail, on
the other hand, he thematized this failure in his emblematic programme.
There again the main theme is vanitas. The entrance to the room that shows
the whole of the world in miniature stands under the memorial sign of
general decay.
The room itself becomes the framework of the collection. Its archi
tecture and decoration show what the displayed pieces are intended to
express. The simulation of the microcosm is represented in reminders of the
vanitas of all earthly reality compared to the reality of another world.60
The moral lesson is the following. All things perceived stand for more than
the visible. The whole collection serves as a memento mori and reminds us
that we should detach ourselves from earthly reality, even from art, as well
as from earthly power and wealth. Ferdinand Albrecht's comments, expres
sed through the choice of emblems, comments on the pieces ofhis collection
and in the collecting itself, is at the same time a statement about the rooms
of the castle. The architecture and the interior decoration are placed in the
context of vanitas and decay. The emblematic comments refer to the unso
luble paradox of perdurance and decay.
Genealogical Inscription
The entries in the books of the Bibliotheca Albertina give a clue to
Ferdinand Albrecht' s self-understanding as a collector. 61 They record the
date of acquisition, the place, the occasion of the purchase, e.g. on a given

[Page 159]
The Emblem and Architecture 135
journey. Ferdinand Albrecht often records his age at the time of the acquisi
tion, and there are frequent references to bis father, his wife, or his
children. 62 Thus the collection is ordered by a chronology that is dictated
by the biography of the collector. These recordings are not just fragments
of a biography63 rather they mark a genealogy. They relate the buyer and
proprietor Ferdinand Albrecht to his father, to his marriage or to the sons.
At the same time they make the books of the library-as well as the pieces
of the Kunstkammer-into witnesses of the travels and residences in the
sovereign domains of the Dukes of Braunschweig and Lüneburg.
All of this serves to legitimate an authority and a rule that Ferdinand
Albrecht did not really have but which he considered himself entitled to.
The construction of the collection and of the library as well as his education
and his travels constitute his bid, against his brothers, to qualify for a ruling
position in politics and representation. Ferdinand Albrecht thus followed the
example of his father, Duke August the Y ounger, 64 and his brother, Duke
Anton Ulrich,65 though his collection cannot compare in size and value
with theirs. He did not have sufficient means at his disposai. While his
father and brother fulfilled their positions of power in an examplary way and
presented their collections in such a way as to set standards, 66 collecting
was for Ferdinand Albrecht a substitute occupation. His collection could
only partly fulfill its representative function. Even though Ferdinand
Albrecht tried to attract visitors to his collection with the description he
published in the Sonderbare Andachtige Gedancken there is no record of
visitors or illustrious guests actually visiting his collection as is the case for
most other Kunstkammern. In contrast, the art collection of Duke Anton
Ulrich and the library of Duke August were well-known and were consi
dered to be worth seeing. The collection of Ferdinand Albrecht paled in
comparison with the collections of his relatives.
This brought Ferdinand Albrecht to concentrate all the more on the
connection between individual biography and the overall collection. The
pieces are marked as memorabilia for the stations of bis travels and more
generally of his life. But the aim of these inscriptions and of this individual
biographical order is much less the marking of his private life than the
staging for his request of public recognition commensurate with his rank. In
this sense Ferdinand Albrecht's existence is determined by a genealogical
connection. Exterior form and order in space and in the architectural context
are meant to represent this, followed by his request concerning bis place in
the rank.

The Programme as Self-Inscription
In the decoration of the rooms housing his collection the Duke
inscribes himself in a literai sense. One of the emblems in the Kunstkammer,
the central emblem of that room, shows the Duke himself. On the back of
the door he is depicted opening a covered cup out of which a snake crawls
towards him. The motto underneath the picture reads PEJORA LATET [Evil
lurks]. Over the door there was a cloth with the inscription VIVE UT POST
VIVAS. MEMENTO MOR! [Live to live on. Remember that you are moral]
in mirrored writing. The writing could be read in a mirror across from the
door when one entered the room. This inscription can be read as a comment
on the collection as well as the inscription to the portrait of the ruler. In the
material itself there is a further emblematisation: playing on a Pauline
quotation, "Wir sehen jetzt durch einen Spiegel in einem tunckeln Wort"
[We now see through a word darkly (l.Cor. 13.12.)] This refers to the
impossibility of knowing the world or of comprehending God, and to the
relevance of the coming world, but also the vanitas of earthly existence.
Such a mirror-reflection multiplies the comments on the whole collection,
suggesting both a further relativisation and rupture. If the collection is a
mirror of the world as cosmos, then the mirrored reflection of the otherwise
unreadable inscription is a reading device for the cosmologial message of the
Kunstkammer. Looking into the mirror, which is explicitly also the mirror
of self-knowledge, since the mirror opposite the door shows the picture of
the Duke himself in the emblem or the reflection of the spectator, provides
the interpretational key that allows the deciphering of the secret messages
of creation in the book of nature whilst at the same time a recalling the
limits of human understanding in the face of divine order.
The decoration of the rooms of the castle reflect the general attitude
of the collector and proprietor towards the power he himself established
within. W ord and image create the order for this collection as a universal
lesson pertaining to the decay of the world and the earthly, the decay of
power and of human existence. lt portrays the Duke as the God-fearing ruler
that he would like to be but in fact was not.
In the rooms that accommodate his collection-and quite generally in
the castle that frames the programme of his collection-the Duke marks his
self-understanding and self-representation. As he completes the books in his
library with entries about his stations of his life and its genealogical relations
he inscribes himself in the emblematic decor of his Kunstkammer, visible for
all to see.

This emblematic programme accompanied the Duke and his court
every day in every activity and in every room of the castle with a world
view oriented towards the hereafter, a melancholy view in the old sense of
that word. The dominant tone is that of memento mori, a constant reminder
of vanitas. Every thought is accompanied by, in fact, determined by the
unrelenting comment that all human life is in vain. In the rhetorical form of
the allegory and the emblem · the emblematic decor in the castle gives an
interpretation for the collection of the Kunstkammer and the library, and thus
it provides an interpretation of the Duke's life, of his status within his
family and of the political situation of the country. First and foremost this
rhetorical form is a self-representation of the Duke as a melancholic.67

The Stuccoes of J.C. Hansche at Horst and Modave:
Applied Emblematics and the Reception of De Passe's and
Rollenhagen's Nucleus Emblematum Selectissimorum in the
Low Countries
MARC V AN VAECK
University of Leuven
In 1671 the stucco-artist Jan Christiaen Hansche finished some remarkable
stucco-work in relief (with representations of the Evangelists, the Church
Fathers and with scenes from the life of saint Norbert) on the ceiling of the
library in the Norbertine abbey of Park near Louvain. The impressive
stuccoes were one of the largest and most prestigious projects in the artistic
career of Master Hansche. His activities belong to the period between 1653
and 1685 and one finds his works in Flanders, Brabant, the prince-bishopric
Liège and the adjacent Rhineland. His clients were members of the lower
gentry and the higher clergy. A survey of Hansche's stuccoes, often in a
state of precarious preservation, would mark a clear stylistic development.
As a demonstration of his artistic virtuosity and his technical skill, Master
Hansche consciously tried to optimize the historiated stucco in high relief in
such a way that it actually appeared as an image in full round. Precisely this
technical ability made him famous as an artist. When the eighteenth-century
Liège historian De Saumery studied Hansche's work in the Castle of
Modave, he remarked: "Ce sont de beaux morceaux, et d'autant plus
estimés qu'ils paraissent entièrement détachés du plancher." And he praised
the Hercules images there saying that "presque toutes les figures sortent du
fond, ou en tout, ou en partie. "1
On one of the tympanums over the side walls of the library, Hansche
depicted the vision of the crucifix in Prémontré from a copperplate
engraving by Theodoor Galle (1571-1633/4).2 In the bottom left hand
corner, Hansche had put the coat-of-arms of his client, the abbot Libert de
Paepe,3 and in the opposite right band corner a similar coat-of-arms that has
undeniably the form of an impresa (Fig. 1). The pictura can be connected
with the activities of the stucco-artist. From the top of a set-square, a band
holds a plummet. The motto reads: "Ex his immobilis veritas" [From these
(stuccoes appears) invariable truth]. When applied to the iconographie
programme in the library, this impresa refers to the didactic character of the

ceilings in the castle of Horst in Sint Pieters Rode, a small village between
Louvain and Aarschot.5 Little is known about Hansche's client, Maria-Anna

new chatelaine was herself a member of a famous noble family. On her
father's side-the knight Louis van den Tympel, bailiff of Brabant and
sheriff of Louvain-she was related to the dukes of Brabant. On her
mother's side, she was related to the counts of Aarschot and the lords of

received the title of countess of Mulert and Hautreppe. Mulert, who <lied in

chamberlain and body-guard of the archduke Leopold. In 1658, the widow

Applied Emblematics in the Castle of Modave
In 1651 the Castle of Modave had been almost completely destroyed
by fire. It was rebuilt quickly by order of the very wealthy nobleman,
succesful strategist and talented diplomat Jean-Caspar-Ferdinand de
Marchin, at the time commander-in-chief of the Spanish troops in the
Southern Netherlands.19 Hansche applied a series of impressive stuccoes.
ln the large audience room the ceiling was covered by stuccoes representing
a detailed genealogical tree with the count's thirty-two quarters. In the
smaller adjacent room, there are similar stuccoes with the genealogical tree
of de Marchin's wife, Marie de Balzac d'Entragues. The representations in
bath rooms express De Marchin's constant concern, his noble descent. His
letters often deal defensively with this subject, and in 1659 he paid the
genealogist and chronicler Albert de Launay to draw up his flattering
pedigree, which served Hansche as a model, and which was later printed at
De Marchin's own expense.20 But Hansche also decorated two rooms with
stuccoes representing the labours of Hercules after engravings by Cornelis
Cort. 21 The theme at the time was fashionable, the figure of Hercules was
in vogue in the great European courts. Rulers liked to represent themselves
(as their precursors had done in antiquity) as a new Hercules, embodying
propagandistic intentions not only as a war hero, as a protector of their
country, as the defendor of the common good, and as a messenger of peace,
but also as the personification of virtue and supreme wisdom, and as the
patron of arts and sciences.22 De Marchin's royal airs and dynastie
ambitions are closely related to this. In the church of Modave, De Marchin
had a chapel built for the counts De Marchin; he also had the mortal
remains of his ancestor Nicolas de Marchin moved to this chape!. ln
addition, he paid for the marble mausoleum erected on the grave of his
parents. 23 Hansche himself may have suggested the Hercules theme for the

lt is important for our argument to note that in the smaller Hercules
room Hansche also applied stuccoes in the lacunars above the three doors.
The lacunar next to the chimney depicts the infant Hercules strangling the
snakes set on him by the jealous and revengeful Hera. The second lacunar
depicts Hercules' death at the stake. Above the third door there is no
Hercules scene but the representation of a genius resting on a skull, while
the whole scene is framed within the circle of the ouroboros (Fig. 20). The
text reads: "Nascentes Morimvr Finisque Ab Origine Pendet" [Born, we die,
our end hangs over our head from the beginning]. The stucco goes back to
emblem 45 of Rollenhagen (Fig. 21) and Hansche quotes the first line of the
subscriptio. But Hansche changed the deictic gesture of the genius, who no
longer points with his left hand towards the skull, but to the place where the
snake bites its own tail, where the beginning joins the end. This representa
tion accords with the Hercules scenes in the room and with the two other
lacunars concerned with the newly born Hercules threatened by snakes and
his death in Oite.
Hansche also made other stuccoes in the castle, in three rooms on the
first floor. Two of them are historiated stuccoes. On the ceiling in the
spacious room above the present family drawing room, we find five scrolls
each with a still life of weaponry: drums, trumpets, suits of armour, canons,
halberds, parapets, an army tent, etc. Representations of this kind are quite
often used in allegories of peace, but here they probably refer to De
Marchin's military activities in the field. Important for our argument,
however, are the stuccoes Hansche made in the room right above the small
Hercules room. In the corners of the ceiling, four scrolls were applied
representing genii with the usual attributes of the four seasons (Fig. 22). The
room was oriented towards the South. Spring is depicted by a genius holding
a bouquet in one hand and a garland in the other. A corn sheaf represents
summer, the vine-tendrils and grapes denote autumn, and the brazier and the
dry sticks refer to winter. Later the room was divided to make two apart
ments and a passage was installed. The new dividing wall severely damaged
the genius who represented Time in the central scroll. The upper part of the
body with winged hour glass, watch and reaping-hook is visible in one room
while the lower part belongs to the other room (Figs. 23-24). The alterations
did still more damage. It appears from the construction of the ground floor
that the room must originally have had three doors, but only two of them
have been preserved, above which each was a stucco. Over the door next

shovel on the ground. The anchor identifies the figure as Hope, one of the
three theological virtues. The badly damaged lacunar over the opposite door
represents a genius with its left foot on a globe, the right hand raises a cup
and the left hand bears the remains of a cross (Fig. 26). Both attributes
depict the other theological virtue Faith. Though the constellation of Hope,
Faith and Labour, and more specifically the representation of Hope with the
shovel as its attribute, is not unusual in the iconographie tradition,25
Hansche's representations in this room can also be linked to Rollenhagen's
and De Passe's emblem book. In the sixteenth emblem of the second part,
Hope is represented holding an anchor and a shovel, which refers to Labour
(Fig. 27). But also the way Faith is represented might be linked to an
emblem in the Nucleus (emblem 69; with cross and cup, Fig. 28). In spite
of the strong similarities between the stuccoes and the Nucleus picturae,
there are also a few striking differences. Whereas Faith in the Nucleus
emblem stands on a square socle-" Constante fiducia" [Steady with faith ]
Hansche' s genius kicks the unstable globe. The shovel in the opposite
lacunar functions in the same way: the genius also kicks this attribute. In the
Nucleus emblem the shovel still had a positive meaning: "In Spe Et Lahore
Transigo Vitam" [ln Hope and Labour I spend my life]. Another significant
change resides in the fact that the women in the Nucleus emblems were
replaced by genii in the stucco representations. In this way, Hansche makes
both stuccoes agree with the iconographie programme of the ceiling whose
meaning can now be identified. The stuccoes advocate the practice of the
theological virtues by contrasting them with transient life on earth, depicted
in the shovel, the globe, Time, and the four seasons.
The stuccoes in the castle of Modave demonstrate again how freely
Hansche treated the original meaning of the Nucleus emblems. His starting
point is the language of word and image in motto and pictura, and he inter
prets pictoral elements not so much according to their specific meaning in
the Nucleus as according to tradition. This procedure illustrates the different
way in which both bi-medial genres functioned, and may be indicative of the
assumed attitude of Hansche's clients and/or his intended public. Hansche's
patrons apparently did not expect the original meanings of the emblems to
be replicated. They may even not have been aware of them, nor did they
need to. The intended public also did not have to know about the often
idiosyncratic interpretations presented by the Nucleus, but a knowledge of
the traditional meanings of the images was necessary. Provided with this
