## Section: Conclusions

Conclusions
This source material, mostly in manuscript and hitherto unexplored in the
study of the history of emblematics, allowed us to examine the network of
relationships that existed between Jesuit education and emblematic practice
at a regional level. This rolling network served to make emblematic works
familiar and accessible, ensured both the acquisition and the communication
of knowledge and learning, and also acted as a spur to individual creativity.
The Jesuits taught theory and practice systematically at various levels over a
long period of time and linked them to particular disciplines within what they
termed the ars litteraria. Jesuit practice ensured that humanist emblematics
reached Hungary, albeit with some measure of delay, and, once this kind
of material was known, adapted and developed it during the course of the
seventeenth century very much along the same lines that we see in the rest
of Europe so that, in time, it came to exert a significant cultural influence.
Starting in the 1630s, Jesuits throughout Europe paid increasing attention
to the propagation of emblematic forms. The first results of their endeavours
appear on the grand scale during the last decades of the seventeenth century.
The delay in their appearance in Hungary constitutes one reason why em-
blematics appeared as a mature separate discipline or area within the ars
litteraria, linked to the teaching of poetry and rhetoric in Hungarian Jesuit
secondary schools only in the early eighteenth century. There is no new de-
parture in Jesuit educational practice after the 1730s and emblematic forms
of expression decline rapidly towards mid- century.
Education differed in the various kinds of houses run by Jesuits and in
different geographic locations. Our researches show that, in Hungary as in

IV The typology of emblem books
and emblematic prints
To get a comprehensive picture of the emergence and subsequent spread of
the literary emblematic tradition in Hungary, we decided it was necessary
first to compile a list of emblem books and emblematic prints associated with
Hungary. This list, which contains a total of some 150 items from the period
1542-1826, contains not only emblem books in the strict sense of the term,
but also prints which show the germ of emblematic form and also the way
emblematic habits of mind were influential in a number of other literary
genres. The main criterion for including an item in the list is the presence of
an emblematic mode of expression. We take the tripartite structure of the
emblem proper to be the paradigm but have also taken into consideration
the various expanded and compressed forms of the emblem, as well as a
number of marginal cases. With few exceptions, the items described are ones
we have examined personally. The list does not include emblematic manu-
scripts, printed thesis sheets (usually with accompanying picture), alba ami-
corum, and works with just the occasional emblematic feature, but we do
list prints that have no pictures but contain text emblems or descriptions of
emblematic décor of an ephemeral nature. Of the countless publications that
have an emblematic frontispiece we have examined only those where the
work itself consist of an interpretation of that frontispiece.
We have grouped the material in the list using both generic and functional
criteria. The aim of this chapter is to develop a typology and to give an
overview of the principal peculiarities of the history of emblematics in Hun-
gary and of those features which can be usefully compared with characteris-
tics we find in other parts of Europe. We are not concerned here with works
and genres that are not included in our list but are discussed in detail later:
for example, works of emblem theory, emblematic stage decorations, the bio-
graphies of saints, and sermons. It follows from the nature of the material
we are examining that there are no strict genre categories in play, and that
the same print may often be assigned to two or more of our groupings.

>Protoemblematics<
Among the publications that one can properly think of as protoemblematic -
as, that is, being the forerunners of the emblem form in the stricter sense
and leading up to it - are the fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Tabula Cebetis
editions. The pictorial elements of the Tabula and its moral precepts had long
played a role in school education, beginning in the Renaissance; in Hungary,
too, there were several editions of it. The close connections between the
Tabula and emblematics is indicated by François Ménestrier's dubbing both
Philostratus's Imagines and the Tabula tableaux emblématiques<. Gilles Cor-
rozet produced an illustrated version of it; Valeriano made use of it in his
Hieroglyphica, and George Wither's collection of emblems (1635) comes
complete with a frontispiece, the iconography of which is based on Renais-
sance illustrations of the Tabula} The Tabula is an important precursor (or
source) of emblem books of the via recta type. Early editions of the Tabula
tended to be published with a frontispiece which served to introduce the
picture concerned, and that same illustration was then often re-used, appear-
ing on the first page of works of a quite different stamp, such as pictorial
cycles and individual pictures.
The first Hungarian edition of the Tabula, dated 1542 and ascribed to
Johannes Honterus, is known only from a single, truncated copy.2 Since the
missing sheet in that copy happens to be the first one, we cannot know if the
edition had a frontispiece. There is no known copy of the next edition, which
dates from between 1554 and 1557;3 indeed, its existence is only hypothetical
and is based on information that comes from the eighteenth century. Evi-
dence for the popularity of the Tabula in Hungary at a later period is pro-
vided by the two surviving eighteenth-century Latin editions of the work, as
well as by Sámuel Liptay's >free translation of 1826.4 Although this has noth-
ing to do with protoemblematics, it is interesting that the front page of the
1826 edition is decorated by a woodcut, made by the translator and depicting
Fortune's wheel, while on the opposite page one can see Sámuel Lehnhardt's
own symbolic-allegorical composition, the centre of which is occupied by a
figure of Minerva. This frontispiece is only indirectly connected with the
usual iconographie tradition we find in the Tabula: the main figure sitting
under a palm tree, holding the Hungarian coat of arms in her left hand and
pointing with her right at a beehive, and surrounded by other symbols and

Mirror of Princes (Fürstenspiegel) and associated material
The inclusion at a relatively early date of emblematic material in works asso-
ciated with the tradition of the Mirror of Princes is no coincidence. Firstly,
the fashion for emblems started up during the age of Humanism, and several
of those who indulged in it were also actively interested in what we have
come to think of as the Mirror of Princes tradition. One important source
for emblematic material is the art of the impresa, and this, like the Mirror of
Princes, is concerned with teaching the rules of behaviour that govern courtly
culture and the life of the courtier. There was a rich variety of forms in both
emblem and impresa that allowed for the discussion of much the same topics
in works with very different structures and genre affiliations. Of course, the
autonomy of the emblematic Mirror of Princes as a genre is only relative:
the boundaries are often hazy and there are strong links with other genres,
such as the emblematic panegyric, the political novel, and >bourgeois< em-
blem books with subject-matter similar to that we find in the Mirror of
Princes.5
Works of the Mirror of Princes type, as well as collections of advice for
rulers, usually sprang up in Europe in places where courtly culture was espe-
cially highly developed. As Emil Hargittay, who worked on the history of this
kind of literature in the seventeenth century, was to discover, the increase in
the number of such productions in Hungary at the beginning of the century
went hand in hand with a transformation in their quality; by the eighteenth
century the fashion for such works had begun to die out - or, rather, had
grown in a different direction. There are examples of works originally written
in Hungary and of others that were translated into Hungarian; most of those
that are of Hungarian origin are associated with Protestant political thinking
or with bourgeois and aristocratic ideology.6
The first Hungarian work in the Mirror of Princes tradition that can be
considered emblematic is by Kristóf Lackner. It was published in 1615, a
mere three years after the first seventeenth-century example of the genre,
the translation of Antonio de Guevara by György Szepsi Korotz.7 Dedicated

As Emil Hargittay pointed out, behind this work with its peculiar system
of symbols we can glimpse the personal ambition and double professional
life of Weber, the physician and the judge. Further indications of this come
in the salutatory poems introducing the publication, the structure of the
work, and the symbolic interpretation of Psalm 101 that follows the greeting
to the reader, and in which Weber assigns verses to a part of the body or an
organ. This interpretation acts as an outline of the rest of the 21-chapter
work: each chapter is headed by one (or two) parts of the body or organs of
the prince or one of his physical characteristics, and there follows a politico-
moral interpretation larded with the usual biblical, classical, medieval, and
more recent quotations.10 By >prince<, Weber means not only kings and rulers
but also the magistrates of a city. The excerpt from a speech provided in the
Appendix summarizes, under twenty-six headings, the similarities between
physicians and rulers, while other sections of the appendix detail the three
biblical topoi of the wise ruler: the ruler as shepherd, helmsman, and head of
a family. The texts chosen are closely tied in with the details of the pictorial
illustrations in the work and act as a gloss on them. Further peculiarities of
this compilation are the continual tension between personal, accidental con-
cerns and messages of supposed universal validity, and the system of sym-
bolic-allegorical references that permeates the entire work.

the de luxe variant of the work, with its twenty-six princely virtues; the latter

the text simpler to remember.

city's coat of arms engraved and set above a townscape of Eperjes (Presov).
The eight chapters that follow interpret the elements of that coat of arms.13

Apart from these three works by Weber, there is a courtly, ethical work
published in 1674 in Latin and Hungarian by István Csáky. This survives in
several manuscript versions and was composed after an unknown original
published in Prague. As Emil Hargittay has demonstrated by comparing the
textual variants, the Hungarian translation was composed in all probability
by Csáky himself between 1664 and 1674; none the less, there are a number
of discrepancies between Csáky's manuscript and the printed Hungarian ver-
sion of it.15 The work, which is divided into fifty chapters, follows the division
into logic, physics, and metaphysics which was standard in the philosophy
departments of Jesuit universities, and explains individual philosophical
propositions as moral and ethical rules for social and courtly life.
The reason for including here a work like this, which can best be assigned
to the genre of the Mirror of Princes, is that, in addition to many historical
examples and references, it contains no fewer than forty descriptions of tex-
tual emblems. The most frequently quoted emblem author, among the many

One peculiar type of Mirror of Princes is represented by the compilation
entitled Idea boni principis. The work of Johannes Sinapius, a Lutheran pas-
tor from Trencsén (Trencín) living in exile in Germany, was published in 1682
in Halle, in the last year of the author's life. Dedicated to Margrave Friedrich
of Brandenburg, this twelve-leaf German and Latin work is divided into
seven so-called >interpretations< (see Fig. 20). At the beginning of each
comes an edifying story about the forebears of the Margrave, demonstrating
one of the virtues of a ruler. These are then linked to references to authori-
ties, entitled >Analecta moralia<, and wise sayings dubbed >Dicta regalia<. All
seven compositions conclude with a regular emblem, called here a >symbo-
lum<, complete with a motto in Latin and in German, a circular pictura, and
a Latin epigram interpreting the two. The work can be considered as a pocket
version of the Mirror of Princes, amplified by the inclusion of emblems, and
having links with both panegyrical literature and the art of the printed book.
There is another work by Sinapius to which we shall return later when we
come to discuss religious emblematics.

basically neo-Stoic in inspiration, is divided into a total of one hundred chap-

Also connected with the emblematic Mirror of Princes tradition are the
symbolic advice collections intended for rulers. An early example of this
small sub-genre comes in the form of a congratulatory publication of 1618,
compiled by the Jesuits living in the Nagyszombat (Trnava) college to mark
the coronation of Ferdinand II.19 The work lists the kings of Hungary from
St Stephen right down to the beginning of the seventeenth century and then
proceeds to proffer advice, based on the symbolic representation of the kings
involved, about how best to rule. At the centre of each symbol stands a king.
After naming the ruler, the lemma refers to the moral lesson in question and
this is then followed by an explicatio in distichs. The inspiration behind Illés
Berger's compilation from 1637 to honour Queen Mary is not dissimilar:
using symbols, he lists ten queens of Hungary.20 The engraved title-page de-
picts a number of flowers and, in the course of his discussion, Berger links
each of these with a particular queen. After her name and a short introduc-
tion, we learn of which royal virtue the queen in question is seen as the
embodiment. Lastly, there follows a description and detailed discussion (elu-
cidatio) of the symbol. A pioneering example of publications of this kind is
the Symbola imperatorum of the renowned humanist author of emblems,
Nicolaus Reusner, of which the Hungarian edition was published more than
a hundred and fifty years after the first appeared in 1588.21 Reusner collected
the best-known actions and sayings connected with famous Greek, Italian,
and German rulers and appended to them symbolic interpretations enriched
by quotations from classical authors. The Buda edition follows exactly the
tripartite division of the original and its text and was published on the occa-
sion of János Sigray's examination in philosophy.

and originally published in 1621, Argenis ran into more than fifty Latin edi-
tions and was translated into almost all European languages. Many of the

background depicts the moral that can be derived from it and which is then
put into words in the subscriptio. The two parts of the picture are not always

Collections of meditations and prayers, and theological tracts
Only gradually did the religious use of emblematic material take an upward
swing in seventeenth-century Europe. The principal features of emblematic
material employed for religious ends are the conscious adaptation of themes
and forms from the Late Middle Ages, a marked penchant for compiling
anthologies, and a richly varied typology. Emblem books with religious
content amount in Europe, on average, to about one-third of the entire em-
blem output. In Hungary that ratio is somewhat higher, and yet some of the
specialized types of emblem we find in the rest of Europe are missing. When
one begins to look closely at publications in this category, it quickly becomes
apparent that works by Hungarian authors are in the minority and that most
of the prints produced were translations and re-editions of originals pub-
lished outside Hungary. We shall look first at those that are Hungarian and
then at the imports, grouping them by religious denomination and in chrono-
logical order.

Drexel's other work of an emblematic character to be translated into
Hungarian was the Heliotropium, first published in 1627. The central theme
of this collection of meditations, divided into five books and then further
subdivided into chapters, is how to direct human thought and action so that
it is in harmony with the will of God. The opening of each book is marked
in the majority of editions by a symbolic engraving giving advance notice of
the central theme of the book concerned. Drexel consistently applies the
image of the sunflower to Man. The content of the five books is as follows:
(i) how God's will can be known; (¿i) the harmonization of human will with
that of God (conformitas)\ (HI) what is meant by harmonizing these two wills;
(/ν) the obstacles to harmonization; and (v) factors assisting harmonization.

Laudatory writing
A feature of Hungarian emblematic literature is the high proportion of
printed material produced for special occasions and fulfilling what is basically
a laudatory function. No attempt has yet been made at a comprehensive
exploration of early-modern panegyrical literature in Hungary, except in the
case of funeral orations;53 even on the international front, we have only par-
tial studies of the phenomenon. One example of a promising research project
that has started up only recently is the systematic study of neo-Latin pane-
gyrics glorifying the Habsburg monarchs (and specifically of emblematic
prints showing the influence of classical antiquity) undertaken by the Insti-
tute of Classical Philology at the University of Vienna.
Laudatory writings with emblematic elements can be classified in a
number of ways: by reference to occasion, genre, appearance, the social
standing of the persons addressed, and sources. Because of the topically de-
termined quality of the message and the similarity in content, construction,
language, and appearance of prints composed for different occasions, it
would seem best to approach such works by summarizing their content, and
then to concentrate on few typical examples. The genres and forms of expres-
sion encountered most often are laudatory orations, poems, and biographies,
as well as emblem (or symbol) series of different kinds and lengths. One
fundamental characteristic of laudatory writings is the way they constantly
