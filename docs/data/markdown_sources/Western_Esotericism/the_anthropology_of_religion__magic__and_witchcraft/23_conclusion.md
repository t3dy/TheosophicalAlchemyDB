# The Anthropology of Religion, Magic, and Witchcraft

## Section: Conclusion

Conclusion
The human mind sees intention in the nonhuman world and the ability to interact with
it directly. This gives rise to the seeming ability to directly influence and, to some degree,
control the nonhuman world through ritual activity. This is the basis of magical thinking.
Although magic at first appears to be an exotic topic practiced by those in foreign places,
in reality, magical thinking is a very human way of thinking and is practiced at some time
or another by every one of us. The logic of magic is not only the result of our normal men-
tal processes but also answers our need to have some control over our lives. Magic gives
us control, and divination gives us knowledge—two of the major functions of religion.
Magic deals with supernatural forces and thus is a religious phenomenon. In Chapters 8
and 9, we will turn to what may be a more familiar domain of religion: anthropomorphic
supernatural beings.
Summary
Magic refers to activities by which a person compels the supernatural to behave in certain
ways. Key components of magical acts are the words that are spoken or the spells and
objects that are manipulated in set ways. Magical rituals usually can be performed only at
special places and at special times. The performer must often observe certain restrictions,
such as abstention from sexual intercourse and avoidance of certain foods.
Sir James G. Frazer articulated the Law of Sympathy, which states that magic depends on
the apparent association or agreement between things. There are two parts to the Law of
Sympathy. The first is the Law of Similarity, which states that things that are alike are the
same. The second is the Law of Contagion, which states that things that were once in con-
tact continue to be connected after the connection is severed. The Law of Similarity gives rise
to homeopathic or imitative magic, and the Law of Contagion to contagious magic.
Techniques for obtaining information about things unknown, including future events,
is known as divination. Inspirational forms of divination involve some type of spiritual
experience, such as direct contact with a supernatural being through an altered state of

Magic and divination are based on magical thinking, or how the human brain perceives

on presumed correlations. Tylor addressed the question of why people believe that magic
works. The answer is that magic appears never to fail. There are several reasons for this.
Because magic always works, failure must be due to the inadequacies of the magician.
Magic usually attempts to bring about events that will naturally occur; people do not gen-
erally ask impossible things of magic. Finally, there is the issue of selective memory.

Chapter 8
Souls, ghosts, and death
Late-nineteenth-century anthropologist Edward Tylor introduced the concept of animism,
a belief in spirit beings that animate all living things (see Chapter 1). Although living
things can be thought of as having a diffuse supernatural power (“spirit”), they are most
frequently expressed as distinct supernatural beings of various kinds living either within
animals, plants, and natural physical features—or—living as independent beings. Some
are closely associated with humans, even originating as humans, while others may be sepa-
rate and distinct from humans.
This chapter examines supernatural beings who mirror animate beings, primarily
humans, or are transformed human beings. Important among these are souls and ghosts—
supernatural manifestations of individuals, living and dead. We conclude with an exami-
nation of death rituals that frequently interface with the supernatural entities discussed in
this chapter.
Souls and ancestors
The belief in the existence of a spirit entity residing within a person appears to be a
natural one that grows out of simple observations about life. A human being con-
sciously acts and interacts with people in certain ways and has a distinct personality.
On the other hand, at death, during sleep, or in a faint or coma, action appears to
cease. And in dreams and trances, when a person enters an altered state—what hap-
pens in these different states?
The term soul is used to label the noncorporeal, spiritual component of an individual.
Although many people are familiar with the presence of spirits within nonhuman animals,
most scholars reserve the term soul for the spirits that inhabit the human body. Com-
monly, across religions, there is a belief that a soul takes on the personality of the indi-
vidual (or perhaps the individual takes on the personality of the soul). The soul usually has
an existence after death, at least for some period of time.
When one dreams, it can appear as if one’s soul leaves the body and travels rapidly
through space and time, meeting all sorts of people, including relatives who have died.
In some groups, it is considered dangerous to wake someone up suddenly, as there
might not be enough time for the soul to return from its travels, and the soul might
be lost.

When a shaman enters a trance, it is believed that part of the shaman’s soul has left their
body to travel to a supernatural world. Similar views are espoused in parapsychology.
Parapsychology is the study of psychic or psi phenomena, which is believed to refer to
exchanges of information between living things (mainly people, of course), or between
living things and the environment, or are influences of living things on the environment,
which occur without the use of what we call the “normal” senses, and laws of nature.1
Parapsychologists are interested in two major types of psi interactions related to paranormal
activity: ESP (extrasensory perception) and PK (psychokinesis). ESP, described as “receptive
psi,” or “informational psi,” involves the transfer of information, usually through telepathy
and clairvoyance. PK, called “expressive psi,” or “mind over matter,” refers to “true mind
over matter, or the ability of the mind to influence material objects or process without the use
of known physical forces.”2 However, it should be noted that all attempts to demonstrate the
existence of psychic phenomena in a controlled, scientific setting have so far failed.
According to anthropologist/parapsychologist Loyd Auerbach, the soul is “tethered” to
the body “by a silver cord of some ectoplasmic variety.” The phenomenon when part of
the mind, soul, or spirit splits off and leaves the body is known as an out-of-body experi-
ence.3 Illness may be caused by the soul having left the body, and death is the permanent
withdrawal of the soul from the body. The existence of a soul that survives death is rein-
forced when a loved one feels the presence of the deceased or experiences visits from the
deceased in dreams.
Variation in the concept of the soul
Although the concept of a soul can be found in all cultures, the soul is perceived in differ-
ent forms. The soul may be envisioned as a full-sized duplicate of the living individual, or
it may be small and reside somewhere in the body. Depending on the culture, the soul may
be found in the liver, chest, heart, or brain. Or the soul may exist as a person’s shadow or
be reflected in a mirror, which is why beings without souls, such as vampires, are believed
to have no reflection. When seeing a photograph for the first time, some people might see
the image as the soul captured by the photographer.
Some cultural groups believe there are different kinds of souls that reside within the body.
One soul might be responsible for a person’s animation and will disappear at death. This
soul, or life force, may be reincarnated into other living beings. Another soul might be a spirit
that is that individual’s personality. Different souls may be associated with different parts
of the body, or one soul may come from the father’s family and another from the mother’s.
For example, in Haitian Vodou (Chapter 12), there are three spiritual components associ-
ated with the physical being. One of these is a spirit known as the mét-tét, or “Master of the
Head.” The mét-tét may possess the individual and is believed to be responsible for the per-
son’s personality. The other two spiritual components can be seen as souls. The ti-bonanj, or
“little angel,” is a person’s consciousness and ego. When the body dies, the ti-bonanj stays
nearby for a while and then moves on to heaven, where it has little more to do with the liv-
ing. The gwo-bonanj, or “big angel,” comes from the ancestral spirits and is returned after

Classic Mayas believed in way (pronounced why, pl. wayob), companion spirits that were
conjured through bloodletting rites. In the Mayan languages, way is derivative of the words

In some cultures, you may be born with multiple souls, and other souls may be acquired
during your lifetime. The Jívaros of Ecuador (Amazon culture area) believe that people
all have three souls. Every person is born with a nekas, or the soul that is the life force.
The second soul, the aruntam, has to be acquired through a vision. Jívaros see this life
as false and the spiritual world as real. Only by acquiring an aruntam can a person enter
into the real, spiritual world. Acquiring this soul is also believed to give a person power,
intelligence, and self-confidence. The third soul, the miusak, is the “avenging soul.” If
a person’s aruntam is killed, the miusak will avenge the death. It is this belief that gives
rise to the practice of headhunting. The miusak is believed to reside in the head; captur-
ing and ritually shrinking the head are believed to neutralize the miusak of the enemy.
In some cultures, the soul is believed to be created uniquely for each child. Beliefs differ
as to when and how the soul enters into the child. For example, the Roman Catholic belief
that the soul enters the child at conception has influenced their position on such issues as
cloning and abortion. In other cultures, the soul may have a previous existence before it
starts a new life.
Souls, death, and the afterlife
The concept of a soul is very closely tied to ideas about death. As Nigel Barley writes,
notions of what it means to be dead are always part of a more general idea of what it
means to be a living human being in the first place and funerary behaviour and beliefs
around the world read like an extended discussion of the notion of the person.6
Most cultures believe that the soul survives the death of the body, at least for some
period of time. Where there is a belief in multiple souls, the different souls may have dif-
ferent destinations after death, including surviving for different lengths of time. In most
cultures, the idea of the soul after death is based more on continuity with life than on
immortality, as is common in the West.
Souls that live after death may even spend some time near their family, often until the
funeral is completed, and then may travel someplace else or be reincarnated. One of the
primary functions of funeral rituals is to aid the soul in its journey. Sometimes, these

journeys are dangerous and difficult, and a soul might perish on the journey or might end
up in a not-so-nice place. In some religious systems, the duration of a soul’s residence after
death is finite, and the soul experiences transmigration, rebirth into the body of an animal
or another person, such as a relative.
The ability of a soul to survive its journey to the land of the dead may also depend on
the quality of the person’s life. In such societies, the threat of failing to “pass the test” to
enter paradise can act as a means of social control. On the other hand, in other groups, all
souls arrive where souls go after death, or, in other cultures, only souls who have particu-
lar rituals or elaborate funeral rituals can transition.
Where is the land of the dead, and what is it like? The final destination of the soul is
usually some distance from the place where the person lived. It is often located at a known
geographic place, such as a mountaintop, island, or a place that is “over the horizon.” It
may be located in a place not considered a part of the normal physical world, such as in
the sky or underground. Nineteenth-century Kiowas buried their dead with the feet facing
east and the head west to facilitate the spirit’s journey to komtokya, or gomtokya, “dead
people’s home,” where tipis faced to the west as opposed to the east, because they were
confused. Indigenous Chipewyans, caribou hunters of the Subarctic culture area whose
homelands bordered the western shores of Hudson Bay, believed that, following death,
the soul crosses a river in a stone canoe: a good soul lives on an island in the middle of the
river, whereas one that was bad sinks in the canoe.
Although we in the West tend to think of the physical afterlife as a paradise, in many
cultures, it is surprisingly similar to the physical community on Earth. In the afterlife, the
dead socialize, hunt, and have sex. However, there is no illness, and the dead interact with
their ancestors.
Not all souls may go to the same afterlife. Some cultures believe there are special places for
souls depending on certain attributes. For example, warriors who have died in battle, women
who have died in childbirth, or persons who have died by suicide might go to specific places.
Pre-contact Aztecs believed the souls of deceased warriors and sacrificial victims apotheosized
as butterflies and hummingbirds and lived in a flower world paradise. There may be special
places for the souls of certain social classes or occupational groups, such as shamans. For
example, Valhalla was a special place for Viking warriors who died a good death—that is, died
in battle. In this case, both social status and manner of death were important.
Examples of concepts of the soul
In this section, we will examine soul beliefs in several societies, including the Yup’iks of
Alaska, the Yanomamös of South America, the Hmongs of Southeast Asia, and Roman
Catholic, Hindu, and Buddhist beliefs. The concept of the soul is highly diverse across
cultures and reflects markedly different cultural beliefs and values.
Yup’ik souls
Many religious systems believe in the recycling of souls. Among the Yup’iks of western
Alaska, a newborn is believed to have the soul of someone who has recently died in the

grandparental generation. The child will be named after this ancestor. The Yup’iks believe
that, at death, the soul remains nearby for a period of time and then leaves to await rebirth.
In Yup’ik culture, animals also possess immortal souls that are part of a cycle of birth
and rebirth. However, this cycle is based on a reciprocal relationship between humans
and animals, reflecting how each treats the other. For example, if a seal perceives that the
hunter is adhering to the rules of Yup’ik society and its relationship to the animal world,
the seal will permit itself to be killed. Its flesh will provide food for the hunter and his fam-
ily, and the seal’s soul will, if treated properly by the hunter, return to the sea to be reborn
again, continuing to provide nourishment to the hunter.
On death, the soul of the seal is believed to retract to its bladder. Yup’iks collect all of
the bladders from the seals killed during the year. They are inflated and hung throughout
a five-day festival and then are shoved through a hole in the ice into the water, where the
souls are eventually reborn.
Yanomamö spirits and souls
Yanomamös (Tropical Forest culture area) believe in a complex of souls. The main part
of the soul becomes a no borebö at death. The Yanomamö cosmos is composed of four
layers. The living Yanomamös live on the third layer, and on death, the no borebö moves
up to the second layer, where it travels on a trail until it encounters a spirit named Wad-
awadariwä. The spirit will ask the soul whether it has been stingy or generous. If the soul
replies that it has been stingy, it is sent to a place of fire, but if it has been generous, it joins
the ancestors. One would assume that the possibility of being sent to a place of fire would
act as a constraint on negative behavior during life, but Wadawadariwä is thought to be
somewhat naive and will accept what the soul tells him. As a result, everyone is deemed to
be generous and is sent to the village of the ancestors.
Another aspect of the soul is the bore, which is released during cremation. It remains on
earth and lives in the jungle. Some bore possess bright glowing eyes and will attack people
who travel through the jungle at night. The third aspect of the soul is the möamo, which
lies within the body near the liver. Shamans use their powers to remove the möamo from
the bodies of their enemies, who then become sick and die. Much of the activity of sha-
mans is divided between stealing the souls of enemies and recovering the souls of members
of their own community.
In addition, everyone has a noreshi, or animal, which is born each time a human child is
born and will develop and grow along with the child. The animal is the person’s double,
and what happens to one will happen to the other. When either the individual or their
noreshi dies, the other one also dies. The most common noreshi for males are large birds,
and for females, they are land animals.
A person’s noreshi lives far away. Therefore, a person has no physical contact with their
own noreshi. Still, it is possible for someone else living where the noreshi lives to kill the
noreshi, usually by accident, causing the person associated with it to die. If possible, the
dead person’s relatives will seek vengeance by killing the murderer. When hunters encoun-
ter an animal that shows unusual behavior, it is believed to be an evil spirit or someone’s
noreshi. It is never killed.

Hmong souls
Hmongs are a people living in the mountainous regions of Southeast Asia, although large
numbers of Hmongs from Laos immigrated to the United States after the end of the Viet-
nam War. Hmongs believe that a person possesses a number of souls—some sources say
as many as 30. Health is the result of a balance between the physical body and its souls.
When one or more of the souls are lost or stolen, the person falls ill.
A soul may be frightened out of the body by a traumatic event, or it may be stolen by
a spirit. In her book The Spirit Catches You and You Fall Down, Anne Fadiman tells the
story of a Hmong family living in Merced, California.7 The story centers on a little girl,
Lia. One day, Lia’s sister came into the house and slammed the door, frightening Lia’s soul
out of her body. The loss of the soul resulted in an illness, which Hmongs call a phrase
that translates as “the spirit catches you and you fall down.” The spirit being referred
to is a soul-stealing spirit. This illness is diagnosed as epilepsy in Western medicine (etic
perspective).
Curing such illnesses falls to the shaman, who enters an altered state of consciousness
to search for the soul and, if it is found, will return it to the patient’s body. If the soul was
stolen by a spirit, the shaman then negotiates with the spirit for the return of the soul.
Gifts are offered, and the soul of a sacrificed animal is offered in exchange for the soul of
the sick individual.
After the birth of a child, its placenta (afterbirth) is buried under the dirt floor of the
house. The word used by Hmongs for the placenta can be translated as “jacket,” and it
is thought of as a piece of clothing. At death, the soul travels back to where the person’s
placenta was buried and puts on its placenta jacket. This allows the soul to travel on the
dangerous path to the place where the ancestors live. If the soul fails to locate its placenta
jacket, it will wander for eternity, never to be reunited with its ancestors.
The soul in Roman Catholicism
The doctrine of the Roman Catholic Church states that, after death, the destiny of each
soul is determined by God, based primarily on the person’s behavior during life. The main
issue is the presence of sin, defined as a “moral evil.” People who are free of sin and per-
fectly pure will go to Heaven, where they will be with God and will experience perfect
happiness. Although God is considered to be omnipresent, Heaven is considered to be
God’s home; in general, it is conceived of as being in the sky.
Souls that are in a state of grace but in need of purification go to Purgatory. The word
Purgatory comes from the Latin purgare, meaning to make clean or to purify. Purgatory
exists for those souls who die with lesser faults for which the person had not repented
or for which the penalty was not entirely paid during life. For example, venial sins are
considered a consequence of human frailty and are considered pardonable, requiring only
temporary punishment. These sins can be dealt with by time served in Purgatory. This is
necessary because nothing less than the perfectly pure can enter Heaven.
People who die in mortal sin or with original sin are relegated to eternal punishment in
Hell. A mortal sin is an act that is contrary to Divine law and separates the sinner from
God; original sin is the sin of Adam in Genesis (disobedience), which is washed away by

The soul in Hinduism and Buddhism
Hinduism holds a belief in an immortal, eternal soul that is born again and again in a
series of different bodies, a process called reincarnation. Although the bodies differ each
time, the self—its distinct personality—remains unchanged. Rebirth as a human is seen as
a precious and rare opportunity.
The Hindu idea of reincarnation is closely tied to the concept of karma. Karma con-
cerns an individual’s actions and the consequences of those actions. One’s life is what
one has made of it, and every action, thought, and desire—good or bad—will affect
one’s next life. The life one lives now is the consequence of past actions. This cycle of
birth, death, and rebirth in this world is known as samsara, and the ultimate goal is to
escape samsara and achieve moksha, or liberation from the limitations of space, time,
and matter. Because the achievement of salvation is difficult and complex, an individual
will require multiple lifetimes to achieve it. Although there is no mobility within a single
lifetime, how the individual accepts and lives during that life will determine the level for
the next reincarnation.
Buddhist concepts of the soul, reincarnation, and karma differ in important ways. Bud-
dhists do not believe in an immortal soul or a conscious personality that continues on.
What is referred to as a soul, Buddhism conceives of as a combination of five mental and
physical aggregates: the physical body, feelings, understandings, will, and consciousness.
These make up the human personality, which is caught up in the endless cycle of birth,
death, and rebirth.
The Four Noble Truths state that life is imperfect and inevitably involves suffering that
originates in our desires and will cease when desires end. The way to do this and achieve
release from the cycle is to follow the Eightfold Path, which consists of right understand-
ing, right thought, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindful-
ness, and right meditation. A person who does this can achieve Nirvana. The goal of
Buddhism is not to go to some blissful heaven but to extinguish desire and craving and
escape from the suffering of this life.
Like Hindus, Buddhists also have the concept of karma, the belief that all of one’s actions,
good or bad, help create one’s personality. This process continues even after death. Rebirth
is seen as the transmission of karma. Buddha compared this process to a flame that passes
from one candle to the next. However, it is only the flame of karma that is passed on, not
a continuous personality. The Wheel of Life (Figure 8.1) shows the 31 planes of existence
conceived of by some Buddhists as psychological metaphors and by others as reality. These
planes of existence include hells, hungry ghosts (beings tormented by unsatisfied desires),
animals, humans, and gods. Humans proceed around and around this wheel, repeatedly
experiencing suffering, unless we are freed into Nirvana. Nirvana means to extinguish or
put out the flame of the candle.

Tana Toraja ancestors and social death
The Tana Torajas are horticultural people living in small villages growing rice and raising
water buffalo and pigs in the mountains of Sulawesi, Indonesia. Ritually, their world is
divided into the smoke-ascending realm and the smoke-descending realm, a division that
exists in both the physical and supernatural worlds. The smoke-ascending realm is associ-
ated with the rising sun in the east, the deata, and health and fertility rituals. The deata are
gods and spirits found in mountains, rivers, trees, animals, and the roofs of houses; they
also exist in the stars, clouds, rain, and mist. The smoke-descending dominion includes the
nene, or ancestors, and the bombo, or souls of people who have recently died. This realm
is associated with the setting sun in the west, death rituals, and the progression of souls.
Through ritual, the bombo are transformed into nene, and the nene can be transformed
into deata.
The smoke-ascending and smoke-descending realms must be kept separate, a require-
ment that lies at the heart of much of Toraja ritual, which includes the offering of food,
such as rice, and the sacrifice of animals. This ritual activity is a way of “feeding the gods,”
and if it pleases the gods, they will, in turn, aid the people.
Torajas separate physical death from social death clearly. In Toraja society, physical
death is associated with the cessation of breathing and heartbeat, but social death extends
over a period of time—it is a cultural interpretation of death. When a person is physically
dead, the individual is not said to have died but is said to have a fever or to be sleeping.
This knowledge is important because, at this point, all smoke-ascending rituals are for-
bidden. To Tana Torajas, the act of dying is a lengthy process whereby the bombo (soul)
begins to separate from the physical body.
The social pronouncement of death does not take place for weeks and, in the case of
very important people, even years. The body is wrapped in a cloth or put in a wood coffin
and placed along the south wall of the house with the head to the west—the direction that
is associated with smoke-descending rituals. The corpse, which may or may not be par-
tially embalmed, is said to be asleep. During this time, the decaying body is still referred
to as a person with a fever. Offerings of food and drink are made to the dead person, and
people greet them and converse with them when they enter the house, keeping the bombo
up to date about the family and community. During this time, the bombo of the deceased

Bodies and souls
As we have seen, the concept of the soul is intimately connected with death. Although
the soul animates the living body, the soul also has a life beyond the physical. In this
section, we will look at cases in which the soul and body are disconnected, but one or
the other remains closely connected with the world of the living. These include ghosts,
vampires, and zombies.

The living dead: vampires and zombies
Stories of resurrection from death are in the mythology of many religious traditions. From
the Egyptian god Osiris, the Hebrew prophet Elijah, who could raise the dead and is him-
self expected to return, to Lazarus and Jesus, stories are told of those who die but later
return to life. In these examples, because the living person was seen as special, the resur-
rection has great religious significance. In other cases, the dead returning to life is more
ordinary, and it can be positive or negative. In this section, we will look at examples of
the latter, namely vampires and zombies. Both are familiar creatures in the United States,
where countless books, movies, television shows, and video games feature vampires and
zombies as their main characters. However, the folk beliefs that underlie these creatures
are often quite different from popular renditions.
Vampires
Much Western vampire lore is based on Bram Stoker’s novel Dracula, published in 1897.
Stoker based his book loosely on the historical figure of Vlad Tepes, or Vlad the Impaler,
but made numerous changes. Tepes was a Romanian prince, not a count, who ruled in
Walachia, not Transylvania, and was never viewed as a vampire by the local population.
In fact, Tepes is a local hero to many in Romania.
The vampire is a creature that was considered to be real throughout much of Europe, pri-
marily in parts of Eastern Europe, and they bear little resemblance to the depictions in these
novels. Vampires were more likely to be shabbily dressed peasants than elegant counts and
were certainly not seen as romantic in any way. So, who or what is a vampire? A vampire is
believed to be someone who had recently died but who has returned to bring death to others.
The interest in vampires and the documentation of cases of vampirism began in the
eighteenth century when parts of Serbia and Walachia were turned over to Austria. Aus-
trian patrol officials began recording the local custom of exhuming dead bodies and “kill-
ing” them. An important case comes from the Serbian village of Medvegia in the 1730s.

In reality, much of the evidence for the return of Paole and others as vampires can be eas-

villagers staked Paole and reported that he groaned, what they most likely heard was the
release of this gas. Although his corpse appeared not to have decomposed, this also is not
unusual. Corpses actually decay at varying rates, and the burial itself delays decomposi-
tion. In fact, all decomposing bodies would show these “vampire” features. However, only
the bodies of those suspected of vampirism were ever dug up and “killed.”
Paul Barber points out that the people who were most likely to be labeled vampires were
those considered difficult, unpopular, or great sinners during their lifetime.19 He suggests
that a belief in vampires provided an explanation for unexplained deaths, especially from
epidemic diseases and other unfortunate events. Even better than just an explanation, the
attribution of misfortune to vampirism also provided a course of action: the vampire could
be “killed.”
VAMPIRES IN NEW ENGLAND
Vampire beliefs were fairly widespread throughout Europe and came with early settlers to
North America. Folklorist Michael Bell, in his book Food for the Dead, documented sev-
eral cases of vampire beliefs in New England, dating from 1793 to as late as the 1890s.20
New England vampire beliefs primarily revolved around consumption, an earlier term for
tuberculosis.21 The death of a family member from tuberculosis was frequently followed by
further deaths within the family. The disease is readily transmitted among people living under
crowded conditions, such as was common in rural New England farms in the nineteenth cen-
tury. One of the first to die was thought to return to cause illness and death in family members.
One strategy to prevent contagion was to dig up the body of the supposed vampire and
destroy it, usually by burning. In 1854, in Connecticut, a man was dying following the
death of his father and two older brothers. Friends exhumed the bodies of the deceased
brothers and burned the bodies next to their graves. Sometimes, the smoke from the burn-
ing was used as medicine. In 1827, in Rhode Island, the remains of a young girl were
exhumed, and her body burned while members of the family gathered around and inhaled
the smoke to prevent further illness. Drinking a mixture of ashes from the burning and
water was sometimes seen as a cure.

Death rituals
Death rituals or funerals are rites of passage whereby an individ-
ual moves from the status of living to dead (or another post-death
state such as ancestor). The loss of a member of the community,
especially an important member, can be very traumatic. We can
think of a death as a disruption of the social fabric of the family
and community that needs to be mended. Death rituals provide
ways of channeling behavior in what can be a highly emotional
state, providing explanations for death and for what happens
after death. In these functions, funerals act very much like social
rites of intensification (see Chapter 4).

Funeral rituals
Funerals permit and channel expressions of grief. Cross-culturally, all people feel grief, but
it can be manifested in different ways. In some societies, there are specific times when it is
appropriate to express grief and times when it is not. Other societies, including British and U.S.
societies, emphasize control of one’s emotions. Grieving is often done privately, not in public,
even at the funeral service. The length of time set aside for grieving is often limited, and after a
period of time, the close relatives are expected to move on with their lives. Even when grieving
is publicly conducted, it is still limited in time, and its expression is culturally channeled.
Among the Murngins of Australia, funeral rituals actually begin before death. The fam-
ily and community gather around the dying person and begin to wail and sing songs to
comfort them. The songs also provide instructions to the soul and request that it not cause
difficulties for the family. Although some of the emotional energy of the men is directed
toward revenge for the death (death is usually believed to be caused by sorcery or fighting),
grief is more explicitly expressed by the women, who take sharp sticks or stone knives and
cut their heads so that they bleed.
Through the early twentieth century, mourning Kiowa women cut off fingertips or
gashed their arms, legs, and breasts with a knife before keening at the gravesite of a hus-
band or close relative. After several days passed, someone brought her back home. Women
in seventeenth-century Delaware society (Northeast culture area) scratched their faces and
wailed for long periods.
Across cultures, there are also customs associated with social grief, death, and other
forms of loss, where non-relatives are employed or appointed to express heightened public
emotion openly on behalf of the family or community. This ceremonial practice is referred
to as ritual wailing.
In a study of the Shokleng, Bororo, and Shavante tribes of southern and central Brazil
(Tropical Forest culture area), anthropologist Greg Urban found common signs of grieving
associated with ritual wailing. During ritual wailing, there is a musical line or tone with a
rhythmic structure of words and cries or wails. There are also various semiotic forms asso-
ciated with crying, such as the cry break, when individuals force air from the diaphragm
in a wail, breathing out sound; voicing while inhaling; the use of a “creaky” voice; and,
in some cases, the employment of a falsetto voice. He also found that in ritual wailing,
the general expression of grief has no particular or designated audience but is, instead, a
group’s need to express profound emotion associated with death or separation and loss.25
The way grief is expressed in ritual wailing is culturally learned, a product of enculturation.
Earlier, we described the funeral rituals of the Torajans of Indonesia. In Tana Toraja,
there is a strong cultural pressure not to show one’s emotions and to keep sadness and
anger hidden from others. Torajans believe such emotions are bad for one’s health and are
disruptive to interpersonal relationships. However, in the context of death, Torajans can
and are expected to express grief.
When the corpse is kept in the house between the time the person physically dies and the
beginning of the funeral, wailing does not occur, and the family remains calm. When the
funeral finally begins, which might be several months later, there are particular times when
it is appropriate to cry and wail. Villagers wail loudly and express both grief and sympathy

Funerals are for both the living and the dead. Frequently, the fate of the soul depends on
the proper rituals being performed correctly by the family. Often, the most important issues
are to protect the living by separating the living from the dead and to move the soul away
from the living community so that it cannot cause harm. There are many ways in which the

find its body, thereby willing it to leave. Other ways of moving the soul are to frighten it with

Disposal of the body
The focal point of most funeral rituals is the corpse, and one of the most important activi-
ties in a funeral is the process of disposing of it. In some cultures, it is truly a disposal, for
it is thought that as long as the body is intact, the soul will not leave. In other cultures,
burial may take place quickly, often within 24 hours, or the body may be kept for several
days or longer before burial. This is especially true if some type of preservation is practiced
and the body plays an integral part in the ritual.
Burial
Perhaps the most common means of disposing of a body is burial. In order to analyze
burials, there are many variables involved, such as where the body is buried. Often, there
is a sacred place for burials, such as a cemetery or cave, and they are sometimes restricted
to a particular ethnic group, social class, or people associated with a specific occupation
or cause of death. Many nation-state societies provide special cemeteries for members of
the military, for example, or for those who died in a specific war, such as World War II
cemeteries. Some cemeteries contain unmarked graves, and others, very elaborate tombs.
However, bodies are not always placed in special places. Some are buried near the house or
even under the floor of the house, as practiced by the ancient Mayas. Frequently, this occurs
when the soul becomes an ancestor, and the family wishes to keep the ancestor close by.
The body can be simply wrapped in a blanket or cloth, sometimes made with special
designs or prepared earlier in life for just this occasion. The body can be placed in a wood
coffin of various shapes and designs or a pottery vessel. The position of the body may be
influenced by the size and shape of the container. A body can be stretched out in a coffin
(most frequently on its back but sometimes on its side or stomach) but would be bound
in a flexed or fetal position if placed in a pottery vessel. Sometimes, the body is oriented
in a particular direction, with the head often positioned in the direction of a sacred place.

Secondary burials
Funeral rituals sometimes include two burials. The first takes place at death and involves
burial or some other disposition of the body. The secondary burial takes place at a later
time, perhaps months or years later. This second phase often marks the end of the mourn-
ing period and commonly involves digging up, processing, and reburying the body in some
way. This is sometimes related to conceptions of the soul and the idea that what happens to
the body mirrors or, in some way, affects what happens to the soul after death. Eighteenth-
century Choctaws (Southeast culture area) placed the dead on scaffolds outside the villages.
Several months later, bone pickers with long fingernails removed the decomposing flesh from
the skeleton and placed the bones in a community bone house. Likewise, Mandans of the
Northern Plains allowed the body of the deceased to decay on a scaffold before the bones
were collected, wrapped in animal hides, and buried in the village refuse area or on a river-
bank. The skull was placed among the crania of other clan members encircling the scaffold.
Among the Murngins of Australia, each kin group is associated with one or more sacred
water holes where totemic spirits live. The spirit comes out of the water hole, often in a
father’s dream, and asks the father to point out its mother; the spirit then enters its moth-
er’s womb. If a baby dies, its spirit returns to the water hole, becomes a spirit once again,
and waits to be reborn. When an adult dies, the spirit returns to the totemic well, where it
will always remain a spirit, never again to be reborn.
After death has occurred, totemic designs are painted on the body during a celebration and
it is then carried to the grave. The grave is a symbol of the sacred totemic well, and the body
is placed into the grave, laid out straight with the face down. Then, the grave is filled in.
After two or three months or more, the body is exhumed. Any remaining flesh is removed
from the bones, and the bones are washed. Some small bones, such as finger bones, may
be kept as relics by close relatives. The cleaned bones are placed on bark paper and made
into a bundle, which is tended for several months. Afterward, a coffin is made from a tree
trunk hollowed out by termites. The bones are broken up with a stone and placed into the
log. The log is left to rot, and the bones are left to decay.
Cremation
Cremation is not as common as burial, yet many cultures practice cremation for a variety
of reasons. Hindu cremation rituals are elaborate and involve a procession to the cremato-
rium, where prayers are offered on behalf of the deceased. It is customary to wear white at
these ceremonies, never black. Following cremation, Hindus customarily scatter the ashes
into a sacred body of water or a place important to the deceased. In modern industrial
societies, cremation is becoming more popular as land becomes more and more valuable
and crowded, and less is available for cemeteries. Also, cremation is more economical than
burial and more popular as the cost of burial increases.
Among the Yanomamös, after a person has died, the body is decorated. It is then brought to
a pile of firewood set up in the middle of the community, and the body is burned. The smoke

Mummification
In some cultures, such as those that practice cremation, it is important to destroy the body
to release the soul. Other cultures stress the importance of maintaining the integrity of the
body after death. The practice of embalming is not intended so much to preserve the body
for all time as to prevent decay during the funeral period and to permit the display of a
lifelike body. Some cultures, however, stress the need to prevent the decay of the flesh for
all time and may practice smoking the body or preserving it in salt or oil.
To the ancient Egyptians, death was the next step in the continuation of life. To partici-
pate in this new life, the body had to be preserved. The Egyptians developed a process of
mummification that, by the New Kingdom times (ca. 1570–1070 BCE), was able to pre-
serve the body thoroughly.
The process of mummification was complex and time-consuming and could be practiced
in its complete form only for the important and wealthy. In one of the most common tech-
niques, they would remove as much of the water in the body as possible by burying it in
the mineral natron for 70 days. The internal organs were removed and preserved in jars,
and the body cavity was filled with resin-soaked linen. The body itself was then wrapped
in additional linen, and hot resin was painted on the wrapping to form a hard layer.
Finally, the entire body was wrapped in a cast made of linen and plaster.
While mummification is usually associated with ancient Egypt, it appears that mummifica-
tion was widely practiced in Britain during the Bronze Age (2200 to 750 BCE). However, this
practice was quite crude when compared to the elaborate technology of Egypt. While this
kind of mummification delayed decomposition, eventually, the flesh decayed, leaving only the
skeleton. In a normal burial, bacteria from the gut create tiny holes in the bone. Microscopic
analysis of mummified bones lacks such holes since mummification prevented or slowed
down this process. However, analysis shows that only a small percentage of Bronze Age bod-
ies were processed this way. Techniques included removal of the internal organs, smoking the
body over a fire, or burial for a time in a peat bog, an environment very hostile to bacteria.

U.S. death rituals in the nineteenth century
In the United States in the early nineteenth century, a person would most likely die at
home, especially in rural areas, surrounded by family, friends, clergy, and perhaps a physi-
cian. Death was often a public affair, and the community would show concern about the
medical and religious condition of the dying person.
Once death occurred, the close members of the family took responsibility for the prepa-
ration of the body for burial. This was done primarily by female family members rather
than a professional undertaker. The body was ritually washed and groomed, a cloth or
shroud was wrapped around the body, and the corpse was placed in a coffin, most likely
made after death to the measurements of the person.
The body stayed in the home for one to three days in the parlor. Furniture was removed,
mirrors were covered, and black crepe was hung. If the weather was warm, ice was often
placed around the body to slow decay, and family members would keep a vigil over the
body while visitors came to the house to view the deceased, recite sections from the Bible,
socialize, and eat.
Finally, after a brief service, the family and friends formed a procession, and the coffin
was carried to the gravesite. Early in the century, especially in rural areas, the body would
be buried on family land; however, as communities grew, burials more frequently occurred
in cemeteries. If the distance between the home and the cemetery was short, the coffin
would be conveyed by the family; later, this was replaced by the hearse, a horse-drawn car-
riage specially built for this purpose that could be rented from a livery stable. Sometimes,
the procession stopped at a church for a public funeral service and perhaps a final viewing
of the body before continuing to the graveyard. The body would finally be buried in the
ground or placed in an aboveground tomb.
Things changed during the Civil War, during which more than 600,000 men died. (This is a
very large number, especially when compared with the 57,777 who died during the Vietnam

War and the 405,399 who died in World War II.) After a battle, there were so many corpses
that it was not possible to give them the respectful treatment expected during other times.
However, attempts were made to give the bodies a proper burial if at all possible and to mark

a process that did not occur at the local funeral when a person died in his or her own bed.
Embalming was first practiced in the U.S. in the 1840s to preserve medical cadavers. It
was not until the Civil War that this process became widely used by the general population
to preserve the bodies of those who had died in battle so that they could be shipped home
for proper burial. Undertakers who specialized in embalming set up shops near hospitals
and army camps and in tents next to battlefields. This was adapted to be used more widely
in U.S. society after the war.

Today, most people in the United States die in hospitals or nursing homes. When the indi-

Before the funeral ritual, there is often a viewing at which the body is displayed—the
corpse is usually dressed in meaningful clothing and “styled” so that the individual appears
as much as possible to be in a natural, “living” state. There is little formal ritual at a view-

graphs), people dressed in somber colors, and condolences shared with the family. People
often sit or stand around and tell stories of the deceased.
People in the United States have relatively little experience with death compared with
members of other societies and are often ill at ease in its presence. Commonly, a person
will not attend his or her first funeral until adulthood. In other societies, children would be
present throughout a person’s illness, death, and funeral. There is also a special vocabulary
for things associated with U.S. funerals that is thought to be more acceptable than more
traditional terms—funeral director for undertaker and casket for coffin, for example.

rituals are relatively short, usually lasting a half hour to an hour. Typically, friends, neighbors,
and coworkers will take a few hours out of a normal working day to attend the funeral before
returning home or back to work. These rituals are relatively quiet; there is little outward
expression of grief. The casket is then taken in procession to the gravesite, where there is usu-
ally a short graveside ritual. Often, the mourners leave the gravesite before the grave is filled
in. This is followed by an informal gathering of family and friends at a family member’s home
that includes informal feasting and conversation, usually with little or no ritual.
In recent times, some changes have been seen in U.S. practices around death. Crema-
tion has become a popular alternative to burial, and the growth of grief recovery therapy
shows a new recognition that funerals and U.S. culture do not always provide the best
means of coping with such events. Even modern technology has had an impact, with
announcements of deaths, condolences, and memorials taking place through social media.

Days of death
Festivals that emphasize death and frame it as a concept are found cross-culturally. The one
most familiar in the United States is probably Halloween, although few know much about the
origins and religious underpinnings of what has become a secular day of costumes and candy.

Halloween
In essence, the holiday of Halloween has its origins as a case of cultural contact, a
theme that will be discussed in more detail in Chapter 12. The basis of this holiday
is an ancient Celtic festival called Samhain. Samhain was New Year’s Day and was
celebrated on November 1. The Celts believed that during Samhain, the portal that
normally separates the worlds of the living and the dead opened, and the souls of those
who had died during the past year could then move into the otherworld. To celebrate
the day, special foods were prepared as offerings, and people dressed up as spirits and
wild animals.
With the conversion of Ireland to Christianity in 300–400 CE, many local religious
beliefs and practices were redefined. In a practice that continues to this day, Christian
missionaries were encouraged to reframe local customs in Christian terms. Novem-
ber 1 was soon declared All Saints Day, a day to honor Christian saints, particularly
those who did not otherwise have a feast day. The day before All Saints Day, October
31, became known as the Eve of All Saints, or the Even of All Hallows, which was
shortened to Hallow Even or Halloween. However, the meaning of All Saints Day
was not at all related to the original Celtic holiday, and it was not very successful in
replacing it.
Around 900 CE, the Christian Church added the holiday of All Souls Day on November 2.
This holiday honors all of the people who have died during the past year and is much
closer in meaning to Samhain. Many of the traditional beliefs and customs of Samhain
were preserved, including the idea that night was a time for the wandering dead, offering
food and drink to masked revelers, and lighting bonfires. Stylized representations of death,
including skeletons, are common.
Day of the Dead (Dia de los Muertos) in Mexico and the Andes
In Mexico, the Day of the Dead is associated with the Catholic holidays of All Saints Day
(November 1) and All Souls Day (November 2) and cultural contact between Aztec rituals
and Spanish Catholic traditions. The Aztecs set aside a month (corresponding to the end
of July and August) to honor the dead. The festivities were overseen by Mictecacihuatl, the
“Lady of the Dead.” Later, Spanish priests moved the celebration so that it would coin-
cide with All Saints Day and All Souls Day and be more easily integrated into Mexican
customs.
In what is often referred to as the “folk Catholicism” of Mexico, the dead are seen as
intermediaries between the living and God. The Day of the Dead is a time of family reun-
ion for all family members, living and dead, and is an expression of family continuity.
This is not seen as macabre or a solemn event but as a celebration. During the first week
in November, shops offer many special items for this celebration. Included are many rep-
resentations of skeletons, elaborate wreaths and crosses, and papal picado, or tissue paper
cutouts. Food items are also popular, including special bread called pan de muerto and
skulls and coffins made of sugar.

In the northwestern Andes of Argentina, an area also colonized by the Spanish in
the sixteenth century, All Souls Day (Day of the Dead) is called Día de Todos los
Muertos. As in Mexico, it is a time for families to gather to remember those they
have lost. In the highland Indigenous communities, many people believe that there
is a transition period for the soul once a person has died. These beliefs are syncretic
and involve Catholic teachings mixed with an Indigenous veneration of Pachamama
(Mother Earth).
When a person dies, the body is washed and dressed for burial but will be kept in the
home for eight days while people visit the departed, pray, and mourn. During that time,
they believe that the soul is trying to separate from the body but continues in and around
the household, so it is important to speak nicely of the departed. After eight days, the body
will be buried, and then, during the following three years, the soul will visit and check on
their family and household, specifically during the celebration of “Los Muertos.”
During this holiday, the family cooks or purchases the favorite foods of their recently
departed (candy is commonly purchased for children) and sets a place at the table for the

While conducting fieldwork in the Andes, anthropologist Marjorie M. Snipes was invited
to prepare a plate for a family member who had died three years previously in the United
States. While eating the celebration meal with one of the families in the community, a
moth came into the dining area, flying around the candles on the table and then circling
around Snipes’ head. Florentina, her host, motioned for her to be quiet and attentive, as
the moth was a sign of her grandmother’s soul coming to greet her and express gratitude
for the meal. It was a very solemn and reverent moment for everyone there, a sign of ritual
success and family connections.

deceased family members long beyond this period as a form of remembrance.
