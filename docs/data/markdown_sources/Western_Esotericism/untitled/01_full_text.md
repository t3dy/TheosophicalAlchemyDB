Esotericism and the Academy serves in my view two main purposes. The first is
evident enough: research on the historical origins and the genealogy of the
complex of concepts associated with what we call today ‘Western esotericism.’
The book basically tries to answer the following questions: Where does this
complex come from? Which authors defined it and discussed it as a historical
objectbefore therecent developmentofaspecificresearch fieldwithin academia?
Why do wefind itso often associated withprejudiceand scorn?
The second purpose is perhaps broader and less immediately evident, and
consists in making sense of the study of esotericism as an enterprise that has
value from a scholarly, but also more generally from a cultural, point of view. In
otherwords,thequestionis:WhyisitimportantatalltostudyWesternesotericism
as ahistorical cultural phenomenon?
The first purpose is important because the scholar who approaches the field of
Western esotericism is usually confronted with a thick forest of concepts whose
meaningandmutualrelationshipisfarfrombeingself-evidentorclear.Retracing
thehistoryoftheseconcepts,includingnotoriouslyresilientonessuchas‘magic’or
‘the occult,’and understanding how they became the complex that we now call
‘Western esotericism,’ is obviously essential for disentangling this conceptual
mess. In an ideal agenda for the study of Western esotericism this should be –
and in fact for some it has always been – a top priority. The second purpose is
important because the validity and the relative importance of this field is far
from being unanimously accepted even within academia, despite the relative
successthatthefieldhasencounteredsofarinitsprocessofacademicinstitutiona-
lization. This explains why this is not just a book about Western esotericism, but
also a book about how esotericism relates closely to the history of much larger
cultural complexes such as ‘religion’and ‘science’ inWestern culture.
