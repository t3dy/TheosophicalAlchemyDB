# FrÃ¼he Neuzeit : Emblematics in Hungary : A Study of the History of Symbolic Representation in Renaissance and Baroque Literature

## Section: Conclusions

Conclusions
Throughout Catholic Europe, the composition of emblematic biographies of
Jesuit saints was closely tied in with the needs and practical aims of the
Society. They constitute a particular episode in the history of emblematics.
The genre had its Golden Age in the mid and late seventeenth century; after
that a swift decline followed. The last canonization celebrated by the Society
with emblematic biographies was that of Jean-François Régis. No new series
was composed after the mid-eighteenth century, and only a few new editions
were published in the Central and East European printing shops of the Soci-
ety. One of the last Jesuit theoreticians to treat emblematics, Ignatius Weite-
nauer, makes no mention of emblems of the saints and does not refer to the
possibility of a distinct Jesuit emblematics. His works indicate that the spiri-
tual charge of the emblematic tradition had been exhausted and that its
vogue in the Society was at an end.100
Now we have identified the main features of these emblematic bio-
graphies of saints, the question remains of whether we can speak about a so-
called >paradigm of Jesuit emblematics<; in other words, whether there was
something we can call Jesuit emblematics<?101 Our answer, at least in the
limited field we have investigated, is that there was. The main features of
Jesuit emblematics can be summarized as follows:

(i) The Jesuits did not cultivate emblems of saints for their own sake but
consistently subordinated these to the interests of the Society, its canoniza-
tion strategy, and its belief in the importance of the diffusion of saintly
cults.102 The chief factors in the composition and dissemination of emblems
of saints were: the nature of the particular occasion, the Society's own em-
blem workshops, Jesuit authorship, and the role of the printing shops and
publishers working on the basis of commissions from the Society.
(ii) Until the mid-eighteenth century, emblematic biographies of saints usu-
ally conformed to the prescriptions and expectations of Jesuit emblem theory.
The emblematic practice we find in the prints did not depart from the
prescriptions issued by Jesuit theorists. It is difficult, if not impossible, to
interpret the theory and practice of Jesuit emblematics without considering
the theory of pictures, in which the ars memorativa holds a central place.103
(iii) The Jesuits made a concerted effort to make their emblems generally
understandable and recognizable and to consign any esoteric features to
the background.
(tv) Their overriding wish to achieve claritas led to an increased role for the
unambiguous similitudo. A system of links based on the notion of >similitudo
idonea< became fashionable and, with it, emphasis came to be placed on the
concept of dignitas: on the increased use of a small set of well-known exam-
ples whose function and meaning was not in doubt.
(v) The emphasis on practical utilitas led to a pedagogical expansion of the
tripartite structure of imago, lemma, and explicado. A prose title, separate
from the motto, became standard, identifying at the very beginning the sub-
ject matter of the scene depicted. The practice of repeating that explanation
in various forms (prose, epigram, elegy, ode, and so on) also became stand-
ard.
(vi) The Jesuit emblem of a saint reflects the process by which, by means of
an allegorical-symbolical transformation, an abstract expression can emerge
from a biography or from an individual biographical episode. The motto is
not as important a component as the pictorial motif but it does reinforce the
symbolic meaning and aids interpretation.

(vii) Verse and prose explicationes (commentaries) reflect the religious and
humanist knowledge acquired in the Jesuit schools, the consistent use of po-
etic and rhetorical norms, the influence of the theology of the Exercitia spiri-
tualia, and the Society's specific aims.
(viii) The Jesuits did not create a fundamentally new set of structures; they
drew mostly upon existing tradition - above all, upon classical and humanist
literature. A much larger proportion of their texts and pictures can be related
to the secular impresa and emblem tradition than has been suggested hith-
erto.104 Humanist emblems are modified; their motifs are associated and re-
combined, the original combination of figure and verse breaking up and
being modified by other influences. Transitional forms appeared in the mid-

proper.
(ix) In relation to much discussed principle of >utile et dulce< it can be estab-
lished that delectatio was not a primary goal.105 The Jesuits interpret delec-
tatio as an ancillary means of illustrating Christian beliefs, communicating
them effectively, and rendering them memorable.
(x) In the relationship between figure and text, the emphasis shifts over time
in favour of the text. The place of artistic representation is gradually assumed
by the role of intellectual and emotional effect.
(xi) The essence of the emblems is the choice of motif, the manner of con-
struction, and the context. As well as recurring motifs and observation of the
rules demanding a high degree of variation, we can also see artistic invention
and autonomy at work.
(xii) A final important feature is the creation of a spectacle for all and the
attainment of as great a degree of popular appeal as possible.

X Literary emblematics and the fine arts:
Rhetorical conception and iconographie programme
of the fresco cycle on the Grand Staircase of the
Jesuit College at Györ
At the outset, we suggested the emblem came to be important not only in
literature but also in the fine and applied arts. Partly as a result of emblem-
atic influence on printing and partly for reasons quite independent of that,
emblems, and even entire emblem series, came to be depicted, in Hungary
as elsewhere in Europe, in frescos and wall paintings, on both the inside and
outside of buildings and on a variety of objects in daily use. The compilations
of emblem material by Joachim Camerarius, Jacobus Boschius, and others
were often consulted at the design stage of emblem programmes for both
ecclesiastical and secular spaces. Most of the time, an eclectic use of source
material went hand in hand with new combinations and original composi-
tions. In the emblematic material employed in the fine arts, the third element,
the interpretation, is frequently omitted and we are left with a bipartite form,
consisting of motto and pictura.
The earliest Baroque cycle of Marian frescos anywhere in Hungary can
be found in the stairwell of the Jesuit college at Györ. This series, painted in
1697 by an unknown artist (or artists), probably of Italian origin, is in the
main stairwell of the south-west wing of the two-story building.1 The frescos,
each framed in a stucco cartouche, were, until restoration in 1991, believed
to be in oils. In the course of that restoration, however, it emerged the 1882
repainting in oils by János Mandausz in fact covered a layer of high-quality
frescos from the seventeenth century. The painting over in oils substantially
followed the original, both in iconography and textual inscriptions, but the
resulting aesthetic effect was uneven, which may help to explain why art
historians had paid these frescos scant attention.

The two-branch staircase, with a dividing wall between the branches, turns
back on itself through 180 degrees on reaching the landing half-way up. The
cycle of frescos is on the barrel vault ceiling and walls of the staircase, as
well as on the ceiling and side walls of the landing. The series is based on a
refined system of figurai representations, symbolic, allegorical, and emblem-
atic motifs, typological relationships, cross-references, and inscriptions. The
whole concept is designed for this particular space: the shape of the stairs,
the architectural arrangement of space, and the stucco frames for the frescos.
The frescos are disposed rhythmically in parallel groups of three; two
wall-frescos facing each other are surmounted and linked by a third image
on the vault (see Fig. 72). The ceiling pictures, mostly figurai and of material
associated with the Virgin Mary, are flanked by symbolic representations
open to interpretation in several different ways. A tripartite arrangement of
another sort can be seen on the symbolically painted ceiling on the landing:
six pictures are placed at the angles of two virtual isosceles triangles. It is
not difficult to recognize behind this arrangement the influence of one of the
first and most influential examples of emblematics in the visual arts: the Sala
del Tesoro in Loreto. There, the main pictures on the ceiling carrying the
narrative and the emblems to the side of them act as interpretations of each
other; that is, the central series of pictures provides a key to the interpreta-
tion of the emblematic pictures to either side of it, and these emblems then
in turn offer a new way of looking at the central picture. These individual
representations and meanings are then orchestrated so that they fit into a
particular sequence and are also linked with each other in any number of
ways.2
The artistic significance of the whole ensemble lies in the fact that the
series, thanks to its stylistic coherence, combines a motley of genres and
pictorial and linguistic elements in such a manner that the individual ele-
ments come together to form a comprehensive and satisfactory whole. The
frescos on the ceiling above both branches of the staircase and on the side
walls of the landing are linked by the stucco décor, which itself follows the
architectural design, giving the overall conception a visual unity and reinforc-
ing the total effect. From the point of view of content, one can see at a glance
that the whole fresco cycle revolves around the figure of Mary. In addition
to the standard types of Marian iconography portraits, there are historical
scenes and narrative sequences with images taken from Song of Songs, the
Lauretan Litany, and other prayers to the Virgin, as well as her various titles,

The main thrust of the series consists of typological references to préfigura-
tions of the Virgin in the Old Testament; these are set using all the allegorical
techniques of interpretation (allegatio, allusio, annominatio) favoured in the
décor of semi-sacred spaces at the time.3 There is a complete absence of ab-
stract constructions consisting merely of symbols and signs. Except for a stucco
picture, every representation comes complete with inscription, the inscriptions
assisting in the interpretation of the Marian allegories and, at the same time,
indicating that certain of the pictures belong together. The stucco picture show-
ing a Jesuit teacher and a student in the company of St Ignatius Loyola rein-
forces the message of the Jesuit motifs in the frescos on the side walls of the
landing that the other underlying principle of the series, alongside the Virgin
Mary, is the teaching of St Ignatius and the Jesuit tradition (1).
Emblem series on the Virgin Mary were most frequently used to decorate
churches, chapels, and the halls in which the congregations (or confraternit-
ies) of the Virgin Mary established by the Jesuits would hold their meetings.
Although the ceiling was a favourite location for substantial groupings of
allegories, the use of Marian emblematics in a stairwell is relatively rare; the
few known parallels are also from Jesuit colleges (Graz and Judenburg, for
example).4 The placing of the series and the system of interpretations in-
volved also indicate that these frescos were primarily intended for a rela-
tively small audience who would see them on a regular basis: for the Jesuits,
that is, living in the building. The main aim was to make the resident mem-
bers of the Society stop and think, and to spur them to take as the starting-
point for their own meditations this representative exhibition of the Society's
spiritual and meditational traditions.

Several of the frescos combine more than one pictorial motif which can
also be interpreted independently. Some of them were intended to be read
as a free-standing iconographie unit (5, 8), while others fit together in such
a way that the meaning of the whole is greater than the sum of the parts
(6,12,16).
From the point of view of the placing of picture and text, the frescos can
be grouped into several basic types. In twenty-four cases, the overwhelming
majority, the text appears as part of the picture. In one case, the inscription
belonging to a picture on one of the side walls of the landing was placed
above it, in a separate stucco frame on a dark blue background (11). The
picture immediately opposite this has its text both above the text in the man-
ner we have just described, and also in the picture itself (19).
Some pictures have several texts, chosen from different sources. For our
present purposes, we shall take as our basic unit of inscription a text referring
to one picture only and taken from a single source. By this criterion, one of
our pictures (19) has three such text units, five (2, 5, 8, 6, and 21) have
two, and the remaining twenty one each (see Fig. 73). The total number of
inscriptions (or text units) in the whole series is, thus, thirty-four. Of these,
one inscription appears on its own, in the centre of the landing ceiling, and
is clearly intended as the central text to the entire series of pictures (15). It
consists of a variant of the Marian antiphon Regina coeli, entreating protec-
tion against the three >plagues of Egypt<. Eight of the inscriptions, if they are
read in the proper order, give us the text of the Salve Regina (these are 4, 3,
2, 5, 8, 22, 21, and 20); the section chosen, beginning with the words >Sub
tuum praesidium^ was used also as an antiphon and is here linked to picture
11 in the series. If we take the inscription to picture 14, >Gratia plena< -
which may have been taken from any one of a number of sources - to be
part of the Ave Maria (used also as an antiphon on the Feast of the Assump-
tion), then there is a total of eleven text units on the wall pictures taken
from four different Marian antiphons.
These Marian antiphons apart, the fourteen inscriptions of biblical prove-
nance constitute the greatest number taken from a single source. A further
inscription (2) comes from the Lauretan Litany, and another (19) from the
Te Deum. Pictures 25 and 28 each have one line from the Marian hymn that
begins with the words >Ave maris stella<. The inscription to picture 19 also
includes the motto of the Society of Jesus.
In most instances, the pictorial motifs and the inscriptions only make
sense when taken together. The text often does not simply explain the pic-
ture, it also interprets it. This is particular true of those representations which
include the figure of the Virgin: 2, 3, 4,11, and 22. Elsewhere, the connection
between picture and text is more complex, and finding an interpretation that
links directly to Mary is not a simple matter. This is the case, above all, with
those compositions that include human figures alongside symbolic objects.

For example, the scene involving Jacob's ladder (10) is, according to the
traditional typological interpretation of it, a préfiguration of Christ's incarna-
tion or of his Ascension into Heaven. The inscription, however, refers to
sponsa Dei - that is, the Virgin Mary - and to the fact that the ladder can
also symbolize the road to eternal happiness. It is therefore difficult to de-
cide, at first glance, which motif we are dealing with and, therefore, what is
the correct interpretation we should give to the picture. A third category of
picture-text relationship in these frescos and associated texts is one in which
neither can be interpreted by itself because the key to a proper interpretation
lies in the series as a whole and depends on the extent of our knowledge of
matters and traditions not made explicit here. Those compositions that depict
only objects with a symbolic meaning fall into this category.
The links that are made between text and picture can be of different
kinds. Thematically, for example, the units in the Salve Regina sub-series
belong together, and nos 11 and 19 on the landing are iconographically con-
nected. A further example would be the depictions of the Ark of the Cove-
nant and the table of gold with the shewbread (24, 27), all of which appear
together in the Old Testament (3 Regum, 7: 48), and that of the candlestick
with seven lamps and the lighthouse on the sea (25, 28) which are linked by
the flame motif and refer back to each other. Not only are there connections
between facing pictures on the side walls but each picture is also linked with
the one next to it: nos 5, 6, and 7, for example, can also be interpreted as
expressing homage to God and a plea for military success. The inscriptions
on all three allude unambiguously to the Virgin Mary, but the connection
needs further interpretation: we need to follow the train of thought linking
her to the scene of the expulsion from Paradise (5), the motif of Joshua's
battle against the Amorites (6), and the idea of the Aula Dei (7).
Links like these all indicate that this series of frescos is an iconographie
ensemble, literary in inspiration, which has been designed with great care and
fitted together meticulously. The series as a whole can only be understood if
we first recognize the exact nature and implications of the overall framework
into which both the individual pictures and picture-text combinations have
been fitted.

No contemporary ensemble, designed as a series, is known to have existed
anywhere within the borders of Greater Hungary, and certainly there is noth-
ing comparable to the Gyor Grand Staircase in terms of artistic achievement.
The Catholic church at Vágújhely (Nové Mesto nad Váhom), constructed in
the seventeenth century around the Lauretan Litany, had a fresco series in
the choir but the design was far simpler, while the emblematic wall pictures

in the refectory at the Benedictine abbey at Pannonhalma and in the ceremo-

This becomes even more surprising when we consider that, in her survey
of emblematic decoration in the ecclesiastical buildings of southern Ger-
many, Cornelia Kemp identifies no fewer than 247 instances of such emblem-
atic material in churches and church buildings. A comparison of the entries
in Kemp's catalogue with the inscriptions in the Györ series, shows eleven
instances of identical inscriptions and nine where these are similar, or iden-
tical in part. There are also many parallels between pictorial motifs in south-
ern Germany and at Györ but these cannot form the basis of any firm conclu-
sions. If we take as our criterion for correspondence an inscription associated
with an identical picture and having an identical text, then only three of the
fresco decorations in southern German ecclesiastical buildings match exactly
those we find on the Györ Grand Staircase.6 On the other hand, we should
not look for models and parallels for the Györ ensemble only in buildings
with comparable architectural décor.
The focal point of the programmed series at Györ is the figure of the
Virgin Mary. With the exception of the stucco picture (1), all the components
in the series contain allusions to her and it is clear that there is no other idea
or figure central to the series.

The theme of the Virgin ties the picture-text combinations together per-
fectly. There is no sign anywhere in the series of any association between the
Virgin and the twin notions of pain and suffering. The only hint of the Cruci-
fixion comes in a single representation of the Glory of the Cross (2, 19, see
Figs. 74-75)), and the Virgin as >Mater dolorosa< does not occur at all. In
place of the suffering church, the pictures convey the joy, triumph, and
contentment of the Ecclesia triumphans. At the time when the series was
composed, the idea of >Maria de Victoria< was already an iconographie tradi-
tion familiar to the Györ Jesuits. They had a Maria de Victoria chapel in
their church, with an altar painting done in 1642 for the German citizens of
the town showing the figure of the Virgin Mary triumphant over the Turks,
and, in 1686, the bishop of Györ, Leopold Kollonich, had a column erected
to the victorious Virgin in the square in front of the church and the college,
to commemorate the liberation of Buda.
The pictures in the series, with their abstract meanings and subject-matter,
can, at times, be highly complex, and this indicates that the painter of the
frescos was not the same person as the designer of the series and its iconogra-
phy. Earlier research did not suggest there might have been more than one
person involved and simply assumed the painter of the series to have been
an unknown Italian. The pictorial motifs alluding to the occasion of the com-
position (1,11,19), the inscriptions taken from the liturgy and clearly educa-
tional in tone, as well as the very way the pictorial elements are arranged,
all make it clear that this is a work commissioned by the Jesuit college.7 It
was, in all probability, paid for out of donations from György Széchényi, the
bishop of Györ (1658-85) who also financed the building and decoration of
the college and the decoration of the church interior, and from Erzsébet
Esterházy.8

The Speculum went through seven editions before 1714. Masen was consid-

The significance of Masen's definition of the emblem for the pictorial
element in an emblematic series was recognized by Cornelia Kemp. For
Masen, the basis of the emblem is a pictorial representation the interpreta-
tion of which is already familiar; according to Kemp, this explains why, in
the material she examined, the pictura is generally of a scene which has an
explicit atmospheric quality which, even apart from its functional role of
inducing the reader/spectator to ponder on possible analogies, lends the em-

as a source of emblems.25 That she should not have done so is perfectly
understandable, since precise attributions to sources are, as we argued
earlier, rarely possible given the sheer quantity of material and the ways one
model influences the next; it is also true that Masen's own descriptions are
somewhat laconic and give few clues as to the actual pictorial representation
he had in mind. Identifying sources is even more problematic in the case of
Marian emblematics where most of the pictorial and textual exempla were
extremely well known and in common use.

The examples we have seen so far show that Masen's picture descriptions

train any number of variations and transitional forms of the emblematic

The Grand Staircase as a spur to meditation: Ways of reading
As we suggested earlier, when discussing the conceptual and iconographie
programme of the Györ stairwell frescos, as well as the structures of picture
and text, these frescos and stuccoes were also functional, and, while we can
interpret the basic meanings involved as part of a closed system, the cycle
carries within it a capacity for functioning as an open system, too. Recon-
structing this system in the context of the influences, fashions, and currents
of thought of the time, gives us a more profound understanding and a deeper
knowledge of the role played by >applied emblematics< in shaping public
opinion and taste. A grand staircase that was used on a daily basis by the
residents of a college fulfilled something more than a representative and
recreational role; it had other, primarily didactic and mnemonic functions.
From what we have said so far, it is apparent that the series was intended
to be instructive and educational, and that the kinds of emblematic structure
deployed at Gyôr carried many layers of meaning. The first was always asso-
ciated with the Virgin Mary. But beyond that, reading the pictures in a dif-
ferent order gave rise to different meanings: there was always the surface
Mariological meaning and then there were others that fell quite outside the
scope of Marian devotion. When devising a new way of reading the pictures,
there was no need to insist that the entire cycle should be amenable to the
new approach, since once could concentrate on a smaller or greater number
of pictures, as one chose, rearranging them in one's imagination. The ways
one read the cycle depended primarily upon the way had been taught to read.
The role of the Jesuit meditation techniques in shaping the ways allegori-
cal and symbolic painting were regarded is now a matter of common knowl-
edge. Teaching rhetoric and techniques of meditation, combined with regular
practice, enabled Jesuits of various ages, interests, and education, who came
across pictures like these to rearrange them continually in their imagination
and use them as a catalyst for meditation. The various ways the cycle could
be read, most of them rooted in Jesuit rhetoric, teaching methods, and em-
blematic practice, give us a pointer to a system of meditation largely forgot-
ten today but once very familiar, which grew out of the relationship between
image and text and was structured so as to perform a particular function.

[Page 249]
241
Coordinated structures of this kind were always open to fresh ideas and
shifts in emphasis and meaning. The structures that held groups of ideas
together permitted arbitrary shifts in focus but only rarely became subordi-
nate; the individual constituent pictures in a series remained dynamic and
autonomous. Because those who saw them were fully aware of the symbolic
ambiguities inherent in cycles such as this, the sequence in which the compo-
nent elements were read could continually be fragmented and rearranged,
thus stimulating the reader to made fresh, often idiosyncratic conceptual
links between them. The selection of topical ideas from this wealth of motifs
presented no difficulty to the contemporary spectator, but they do set a limit
on just how well we today can describe in words picture series of this kind.
