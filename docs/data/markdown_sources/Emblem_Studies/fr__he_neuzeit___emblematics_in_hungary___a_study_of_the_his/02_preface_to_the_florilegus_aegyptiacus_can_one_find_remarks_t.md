original conception of the emblem and, as József László Kovács has demon-
strated, it was heavily influenced by Valeriano's Hieroglyphica, from the pref-

symbolic motifs and interpretations it offers in works by earlier and contem-
porary emblematists, such as Alciati, Camerarius, Rollenhagen, Jacob von
Bruck-Angermundt and others. Lackner described emblematics as a method
for deciphering Egyptian texts. He uses the expressions aenigmata, parabola,
hieroglyphica, allegoria and emblemata as synonyms, and makes no attempt
to define them more narrowly or distinguish between them. Both the preface
and the alphabetical structure he gives his work indicate that what he had in
mind was a practical vade-mecum for architects and painters. His attitude,
which mirrors mannerist fondness for emblems, is functional: he holds that
the main purpose of emblematics is the creation of works of a moral charac-
ter to aid and abet the acquisition of virtue. Besides morality and didactics,
he stresses the importance of delectatio.

(¡7) Theories orientated towards reception, our other main approach, can first
be seen in the eighteenth century. Two typical representatives of this ap-

His separate mention of emblem and symbol reveals Vanossi's familiarity
with earlier ideas, although he does not go into details and his own emblems
cannot be separated from his symbols. He mentions some of his sources by
name, among them Saavedra Fajardo and John Barclay (whose Argenis was
also published with emblematic illustrations). Jesuit theorists apart, Vanossi
may also have known the ideas of the late humanists. For him, however,
emblematics is exclusively a useful, pleasantly instructive aid to reception,
and not an autonomous literary form. A further indication of this is his si-
lence about how emblems can be constructed.

So, Zsámboky's and Lackner's ideas (or rather those of Vanossi and of
Bod) can be seen as corresponding to the double view of the emblem found
in handbooks on literary theory. The concern of humanist and late humanist
authors was with the creation of emblems for their own sake: for the sake of
the content which can be better expressed by the emblem than by any other
literary form. For them, the emblem is accordingly an autonomous literary
category. The two eighteenth-century authors, on the other hand, saw the
emblem as a non-autonomous, secondary form of expression, which was mer-
ely an aid to instruction and to the understanding of philosophy and of the
Bible.

would have to admit that Hungarian emblem practice (which is far richer
than theory) can be understood only with difficulty or not at all. It is clear
that, in Hungary, practice cannot be viewed merely as something dependent
on theory. Theories may contain the foundations of a whole variety of practi-
cal applications, but the theories themselves never give us an accurate de-
scription of actual practice. What disquisitions we can find concerning prin-
ciples only deal with some of the questions that matter from the point of
view of actual practice, and the influence of many of those theories made
itself felt only indirectly, if at all. The Hungarian experience also illustrates
the mutual influence upon each other of independent emblem theories, of
remarks made about emblems in handbooks of rhetoric and poetics, and of
practice. The channels of reception of both theory and practice do not always
coincide: the real extent of any international influence can be assessed only
from the manuscripts and printed editions of European works of rhetoric,
poetics, and emblem theory that can be found in Hungarian archives and
libraries, from editions produced in Hungary of European writers on the
subject (some of them in Hungarian translation), and from the emblem
books which in use in Hungarian educational establishments.60

III Routes of transmission: Jesuit education
and emblematics
When we were looking at the history of literary emblematics in Hungary we
noted that, in contrast to the relative dearth of theoretical writing on the
subject, actual practice was surprisingly rich and varied.1 The first emblem
books associated with Hungary to come complete with theoretical glosses
appeared in the second half of the sixteenth century and the first half of the
seventeenth. Their impact upon both theory and practice was nugatory. By
the same token, the comments we find about the emblem in works of literary
theory had only an indirect impact on actual practice.2 Other characteristic
features of Hungarian emblematics include an increase in the number of
publications as the Jesuit college system became established; the practical
and educational nature of a significant proportion of the printed material,
much of it produced for special occasions; the use of bipartite, rather than
tripartite emblematic forms; the moral, didactic and pious intent of much of
the material; and the dominance of verbal elements. All this gives us a clear
indication of the importance of Jesuit education in importing into Hungary
European concerns and trends. A relatively rich practice of literary and ap-
plied emblematics emerges alongside very modest attempts at producing a
coherent emblem theory only where people actively import those traditions
and where the right conditions prevail to ensure that the imported fashion is
understood, disseminated, and imitated.
The slow evolution of emblematics in Hungary in comparison to its devel-
opment in Southern and Western Europe was due to the country's historical,
political, social, and economic situation during the sixteenth and seventeenth
centuries. It was a situation which afforded the Jesuits great scope. The
following facts are clearly relevant:
(a) at the turn of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, emblematic forms
of expression were not unknown in Hungary;

Sources
In Greater Hungary there were about fifty Jesuit houses, colleges, and mis-
sion stations between 1579 and 1773 (see Fig. I).3 These institutions offered
a strictly organized curriculum and students and novices attended courses
regularly. From the sixteenth century right through until the mid-eighteenth
the Jesuits founded houses on a continuous basis, the majority of them in
Northern and North-Western Hungary. There were fewer in the central re-
gions which had long been under Turkish occupation, and the majority of
the population in Eastern Hungary was Protestant. The six houses founded
in the sixteenth century did not all function without a break because of epi-
demics, war, and the anti-Jesuit attitude of some among the local population.
Twenty houses were established in the first half of the seventeenth century
and a further twenty in the second half, providing a solid base for secondary
education. A Jesuit university was opened at Nagyszombat (now Trnava, in
Slovakia) in 1635. Most of the five houses founded in the eighteenth century
were in Southern and Eastern Hungary.

regional distribution of our source material follows the demographic pattern

way the Society of Jesus developed in Hungary. The source material involved
in our analysis is varied, though it does not include any spectacular pieces

Emblematics in Jesuit education
The evidence of library holdings, then, suggests that emblematic material
constituted an important means of communication in the Jesuit cultural and
educational system. So: how did it do so and what were its practical conse-
quences? We have a number of surviving lists of recommended books which
allow us a glimpse of the process of emblematic education in the colleges.
After the publication in 1599 of the definitive edition of the Jesuit school
curriculum, the Ratio studiorum, book lists like these were often drawn up,
either at the Society's headquarters in Rome or, locally, for a particular prov-
ince or an individual college. The lists prepared for the North German prov-
ince between 1604 and 1608 recommended the Tabula Cebetis for study in
the second grammar class, and Giraldus's Syntagmata, Valeriano's Hiero-
glyphica, and Natale Conti's Mythologia as works suitable for extra-curricu-
lar study.30
