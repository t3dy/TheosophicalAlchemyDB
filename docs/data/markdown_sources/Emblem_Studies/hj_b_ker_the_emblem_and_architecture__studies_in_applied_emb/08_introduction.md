Introduction
Among the many rulers of France, François I (1515-1547) is famous for
having had a strong interest in architecture. While the monuments construc
ted during his reign had a functional necessity-serving the needs of the
court-architecture was also exploited as a means of defining territory and
conveying dominance. This is clearly evident in the number and scale of the
projects initiated during his rule. Along with the continuation of his
predecessor's building projects, the king financed the construction and
rebuilding of numerous ecclesiastic and secular monuments. In addition to
a number of smaller châteaux, major royal residences were built at
Chambord, Fontainebleau and Blois. The king also provided for the rebuil
ding of ecclesiastic monuments by supporting the campaigns mounted at the
cathedrals of Beauvais, Troyes, and Senlis. In Paris, François I contributed
to the construction of a new Hôtel de Ville and initiated the rebuilding of
several parish churches including St-Eustache (1532-1589), St.-Merri
(1515-c.1551) and St.-Gervais-et-St.-Protais (1494-1551). In all of these
campaigns particular attention was given to the form and appearance of each
monument. While there are considerable differences between individual
monuments, both secular and ecclesiastic buildings reflect the more direct
incorporation of royal symbolism that increasingly characterized the later
Middle Ages and distinguished the reign of François 1.
Royal Iconology
Prior to the establishment of the literary emblem in the works of
Andrea Alciato (1492-1550), pictorial symbols, impresa and devices played
a major role as signifiers in the material culture. By the end of the Middle
Ages, it was common for members of the royal family and certain nobles
to have their own device and specific motto. The emblem of Louis XII
(1462-1515), as taken from the arms of Blois, was the porcupine with the
motta "Cominus ac Eminus" or "De près et de loin" [From near and from

The impresa of the Prince
In 1504, Louise de Savoie (1476-1531) had a medal struck in com
memoration of her son François' s tenth birthday. One side of this medal
shows the prince in profile while the other has the salamander amidst flames
with the motto "Notrisco al buono, stingo el reo," [I feed on the good and
extinguish the bad]. According to time-honoured tradition1 the salamander
was believed to extinguish fire while also able to live in it. Once adopted as
a device, the attributes of the salamander, which included wisdom, sincerity
and endurance, were extended to its bearer. The animal became the most
recognizable device of François d'Angoulême, duke of Valois, and later
king of France. This device was not newly created, but inherited from his
father Charles, count of Angoulême. It is also thought to have been used
during festivities in 1461 by Jean d'Angoulême, François' grandfather. 2
After his ascendancy to the throne, the symbol appears on royal residences,
and churches throughout his domain. 3
The salamander was an easily recognized and readily understood
reference to the monarch seen in a variety of mediums. In la ter emblem
books, the crowned salamander amidst flames is combined with different
mottoes. ln Claude Paradin's Devises Heroiques (1557) the motto, "Nutrisco
& extinguo" [I nourish and 1 extinguish], appears above the salamander
while "Candide et Syncere" [Candid and sincere] is the motto used by J.
Camerarius in his Symbolorum et emblematum ex acquatilibus et repilibus
desumptorum centuria quarta (1604). The primacy of the salamander was
achieved largely through repeated employment. In architecture, it appears
as a sign denoting the royal buildings of François 1 on portals, gables, walls,
chimneys, and staircases. While often more profuse on royal residences, this
device also appears in ecclesiastic architecture. Furthermore, the profusion
of royal symbols is ubiquitous regardless of the architectural vocabulary of
a particular monument.
Increasingly, the device of the king and the first letter of his name
known as the royal initial-were accompanied by other symbols which create
a "text" that can be read like an emblem or pictorial construct. At Senlis,

The alternate coffers, which have the salamander as their central
motif, are equally emblematic. The salamander amidst flames can be read
as an impresa without its motto. The animal, furthermore, assumes the
characteristic shape of the letter "S," making its symbolism more emphatic.
The inclusion of the knots of Savoy in the adjacent coffers makes a double
interpretation of the salamander possible; the salamander is, at once the
device of the late Charles d'Angoulême ( 1460-1496) and of François 1.
While the crown above the body of the salamander implies the monarch, it
is possible to see multiple associations within the sculpted relief of the
ceiling, namely representation of the royal and implied "imperial" family of
France.
Emblematics and Architecture
Like the devise of a specific individual, during the sixteenth century
two distinct architectural languages were used for very different purposes.
The vast majority of noble and royal residences are described as Renais
sance, whereas in ecclesiastic architecture, there is a distinct revival of
Classical Gothie. A second major difference concerns the nature of construc
tion. The secular building campaigns under François 1 were often new struc
tures or major additions to existing buildings while ecclesiastic building

centered on redefining Gothie architecture through the rebuilding of major
monuments. The iconology of both secular and religious monuments of the
sixteenth century is, thus, rather complex. To convey meaning, a formal
system was devised using select prototypes, a modern architectural vocabu
lary and specific royal symbols. The incorporation of royal symbols-the
fleur-de-lis, royal initial and emblematic device of the sovereign-is the
most obvious manifestation of the politicization of architecture.
While a full discussion of the formal characteristics of sixteenth
century architecture is beyond the scope of this essay, it is revealing to chart
the appearance of applied emblematics. The most common symbol in French
architecture is the fleur-de-lis4 which appears in the ornamentation of
churches, cathedrals and royal residences. By the sixteenth century, it had
become an emphatic reference to the monarch. The fleur-de-lis is the princi
pal motif of the central balustrade of the western façade of Troyes Cathedral
(1502-1531), and the late Gothie façades of Senlis and Beauvais. The fleur
de-lis also became a common motif for tracery. It was used in the clerestory
windows of the nave of the royal parish church of Saint-Eustache at Paris,
begun by François I in 1532, and in the Cathedral at Troyes. In both, fleurs
de-lis appear in the nave and transept areas, but do not appear as tracery in
the choir which was reserved for use by the clergy.
Like the fleur-de-lis, the first letter of the king's name was also a
symbol of the sovereign. While used relatively discretely during the reign
of Charles VIII (1483-1498) and Louis XII (1498-1515), this visual reminder
of the monarch served increasingly to define royal domain. In addition to
appearing throughout the royal residences, the initial of the king can be seen
on cathedrals and in private chapels. Charles VIII, adopted the "K" denoting
"Karolus" and made frequent references to his namesake-Charlemagne
and patron saint, Saint Charles. Charlemagne's use of the "K" as his symbol
and signature is invoked by Charles to create a direct link to this important
"ancestor." The "K" of Charles VIII can be seen on the pillar supporting the
chapel of the Hôtel de Cluny in Paris.5 Under Charles VIII, the crowned
"K," individual fleur-de-lis, and the coat-of-arms of France all became part
of the iconography of the rose window of the Sainte-Chapelle at Paris. The
royal initial of Louis XII can be found at Blois and on the south façade of
Sainte-Chapelle at Paris.
This tradition of using the royal initial was greatly expanded during
the reign of François I, and the royal "F" appears on monuments throughout
northern France. It was employed extensively in each of the royal residen
ces. At Blois, Chambord and Fontainebleau, the "F" is one of the principle

The development of an emblematic vocabulary specific to François 1
is explicit in the modification and proliferation of a "new" Ortler of Saint
Michael and a further conflation of devices. During his reign, "Francis 1
replaced the collar of shells of the Ortler of Saint Michael [founded by Louis
IX in 1469] with a gold double cordelière, the symbol of Saint Francis and
a device already used for Anne of Brittany. "13 The newly espoused collar
of the Ortler is represented in the high vaults of the transept of Notre-Dame
at Senlis. In the first bay on the north side, the blazon is the coat-of-arms
of France surmounted by an imperial crown and encircled by the Ortler of
Saint Michael. The chain links again resemble the twisted knots of the
Hou se of Savoy. This emblematic motif or knot, commonly seen in the tails
of salamanders represented in the architecture built under the monarch, is
also a prominent feature in one of two large-scale portrait paintings of the
king. In the portrait of François 1 c.1530-1535, attributed to Jean Clouet and
found in the Louvre, there are several types of golden knots or cordelière
embroidered on the monarch's doublet. Similar cross referencing was found
at the château de Madrid in the Bois-de-Boulogne begun in 1528 (no longer
extant). Here, there was a simple compounding of emblematic devices. In
addition to the appearance of salamanders on the façade and in the vaults of
the gallery, there were salamanders with wings14 like those of the
phoenix-the device of Eleonor of Austria, second wife of François 1 and
queen of France (1530-1547).
As seen on the exteriors of Notre-Dame at Senlis and Villers
Cotterêts, the royal initial and device of François 1 are placed centrally or
as a crowning element to demarcate the king's domain. ln the transepts at
Beauvais and in the decoration of Chambord, more complex emblematic
programs include, or combine devices to create multiple references even
assertion of dynastie prolongation.

The emblematics found in the architecture of François 1 made manifest
ideas expressed in the visual and literary arts glorifying the monarchy. At
the start of his reign, François 1 commissioned two important treatises on
absolutism. Claude de Seyssel (1450-1520), royal councilor, diplomat and
member of the clergy, wrote La Monarchie de France (1515) and Guillaume
Budé (1467-1540) produced L'institution du Prince (1519) which emphatical
ly support the divine right of kings. The se propagandistic treatises are
comparable to the royal building campaigns; bath affirm the position of the
king as the supreme leader of an implied feudal structure.
