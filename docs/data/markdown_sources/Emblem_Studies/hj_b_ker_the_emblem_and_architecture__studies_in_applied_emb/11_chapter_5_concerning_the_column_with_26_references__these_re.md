## Section: Chapter 5 concerning the column with 26 references: these represent

Chapter 5 concerning the column with 26 references: these represent
respectively 14%, 14% and 10% of the book. Thus three of the 23 chapters
account for a third of all the references. Later on 1 shall have to speak about
the other motifs.
To begin with, we have to consider some methodological problems in
the statistical analysis. The statistical evaluation is problematical for two
reasons: first, Book 16 does not contain all emblematical carriers ofmeaning
with architectural motifs; second, Picinelli's encyclopaedia does not do
justice to those emblems in which architectural motifs are not the most
important carriers of meaning. 1 shall give some examples of the first point.

While lime [c alx] as a building material is mentioned in the book
about buildings (Chapter 2), the references to stone and marble appear in
Book 12 (Chapter 22: Lapis, Saxum, Marmor), which deals generally with
stones and precious stones. The bricklayer's trowel and the square, typical
tools for building, will be found in the 17th book (Chapter 35: Trulla
Caementarii; Chapter 22: Regula), which is dedicated to mechanical instru
ments [Instrumenta mechanica]. The altar, which is used in emblematics
quite often to present the main subject, finds its place in the 14th book
(Chapter 1), containing the ecclesiastical instruments [Instrumenta ecclesi
astica].
The second reservation concerning a statistical analysis leads us to
discuss the possible uses of architectural motifs in emblems. Our first
example cornes from the Livre d'armoiries en signe de fraternité, written by
Georgette de Montenay (t 1581) and was first published in 1571. The 23rd
emblem in this book features the Tower of Babel to demonstrate that God
will humiliate all human pride (Fig. 1).5 The architectural motif is the main
subject in this emblem. Another function is assigned to architecture in the
41st emblem in the collection Emblematum Tyrocinia (1581) of Mathias
Holtzwart. Under the motta "Clauus clauo tunditur" [One nail hammers out
the nail] i.e. Evil is repaid by evil, a scorpion symbolizes a man taking
revenge for an injury. The animal all but disappears in the picture, because
the architectural motifs in the background attract all of our attention (Fig.
2).6 But these elements do not carry any further meaning; they derive from
the horror vacui of the artist who invented the pictures. Like landscape
motifs in other emblems, they provide a context for the emblematic bearer
of meaning, a context without any significance. This context may be arbit
rary or determinate. Among the arbitrary ones are certain architectural
motifs which, as in Holtzwart's scorpion emblem, could be replaced by a
landscape background without any Joss of meaning. Determinate architect
ural motifs can be seen as appropriate and in some way "natural" to the
emblematic bearer of significance. An example of this is the architectural
interior in Holtzwart's organ emblem (Fig. 3),7 because an organ is more
usually found in the interior of a building than in the open air. For the third
use of architectural elements in emblems examples may be found in Gabriel
Rollenhagen's Nucleus emblematum (1611/13). The motif of burning logs
with the motta "Dum nutrio consumor" [ln feeding 1 destroy myself] symbo
lizes dutiful sacrifice (Fig. 4). 8 The main motif is placed on a plinth that
looks like an altar and seems to have, at first glance, the sole function to
dis play the bearer of meaning in a striking way. But this does not exclude

the possibility that the motif of the plinth may also have a deeper sense. In
his 65th emblem Rollenhagen shows under the motta "Sacrificium deo cor
contribulatim" [The troubled heart is a sacrifice for God] a burning heart on
an altar (Fig. 5). 9 The altar is an appropriate context because it is the usual
place for sacrifices destined for God, and indicates that the troubled heart
is a sacrifice pleasing to God, and this sacrifice deserves a special emphasis.
From this point of view the plinth supporting burning logs in 1, 15 may also
indicate that the dutiful sacrifice is pleasing to God and deserves to be
praised.
Another method by which the main significance may be slightly modi
fied will be encountered in Rollenhagen's emblem 1,69. Under the motto
"Constante fiducia" [ln constant hope] stands a woman on a plinth (Fig.
6). 10 She is identified as a personification of Ecclesia by ber attributes of
a crown, chalice, and cross. Rollenhagen does not explain in detail the
meaning of the emblem in bis epigram, but he offers the reader a space in
which to interpret the emblem.11 The conclusion is not difficult to grasp
that the plinth indicates the virtue of constancy. 12 This interpretation bas
parallels in other emblematists who are more detailed in their explanations
than Rollenhagen. Holtzwart leaves us in no doubt as to the meaning of the
different emblematical subjects in bis 63rd emblem (Fig. 7) .13 In the
German subscriptio he explains the motif of the altar as follows: "Der Altar
deut/ daB man mit grund/ i Vnd bstand all ding soll handlen rund" [The
altar means that one should do everything deliberately, thoughtfully and with
constancy]. From this point of view, the altar in Rollenhagen's emblem of
the burning logs also points to constancy as a condition for dutiful sacrifice.
Rollenhagen's parallel emblem (11,31), which praises the ruler's readiness
for self-sacrifice on behalf of his people, shows the emblematical main
subject on a roughly hewn stone (Fig. 8). 14 By way of contrast, the flames
of the logs symbolizing the fire of love (Fig. 9)15 have no such basis: love
is not constant.
Sometimes the altar may also be a blind motif, an iconographically
inherited burden without any meaning. In ber sixteenth emblem Georgette
de Montenay depicts a raven which drops a nut onto an altar (Fig. 10).16
This motif symbolizes the idea that God temporarily raises the unjust only
to destroy them later all the more effectively. The epigram does not mention
an altar, but only a stone, which itself is not described in detail. God is the
cause of the destruction of pride but nevertheless it is unnecessary to inter
pret the altar in this emblem as a pointer to the actions of God. If a detailed
interpretation of the emblematic pictura were undertaken, it would be logical

to equate God with the raven and not with the altar, because just as the
raven raises the nut to great height so God raises the sons of injustice before
their fall. The assumption that the altar may be explained by reference to an
iconographical tradition in this case seems to be more plausible than a
sophisticated interpretation.
The analysis of these few examples has shown that there are three
possible uses of architectural motifs in emblems. Firstly, they may appear
as main bearer of meaning, and whether they appear alone or together with
other motifs they communicate the emblematical message. Secondly, archi
tectural motifs may provide a well founded or arbitrary background to the
emblematic bearer of meaning which derives from another field. Thirdly,
architectural motifs may support the emblematical message by providing
additional notions or specific accentuations; in this case it is not always
necessary to interpret them as allegorical motifs and they do not have to be
interpreted in one way only.
But what is encompassed by "architecture" in the emblem? Which
motifs appear in Picinelli? A glance at the index to the sixteenth book
clearly shows a tendency: the separate parts of a building appear less
frequently than whole architectural structures that form the main subjects.
Beside the pyramid, obelisk, and tower, the following are dealt with in
separate chapters: castle (Chapter. 1: arx, castellum), bouse (Chapter 6:
domus, aedificium), labyrinth (Chapter 11), mill (Chapter 12), bridge
(Chapter 14), temple (Chapter 19), theatre (Chapter 20), hut (Chapter 22:
tugurinum) and fountain (Chapter 3: cisterna). We may also regard as
individual architectural structures the statue (Chapter 18), tomb (Chapter 17:
sepulchrum) and column, for emblem pictures frequently depict the column
and tower, mostly in isolation like the pyramid rather than as a part of a
larger building. Thus Holtzwart symbolizes a true friend by a column placed
on a plinth; this column stands firm amid stormy waves and tempest (Fig.
11).17 Two Italian imprese show in one case a tower (Fig. 12)18 and in the
other a pyramid (Fig. 13)19 instead of the column to represent the con
stancy in the face of changing circumstances. Also the portico often looks
likes a monument than the entrance to a building. Thus Rollenhagen places
his bust of the god Janus in a portico standing against a landscape back
ground (Fig. 14).20 As architectural units larger than a single building
Picinelli mentions city (Chapter 4: civitas) and market-place (Chapter 9:
forum). The workshop (Chapter 13: officina) is dealt with as a single inner
room. Furnishings such as the bed (Chapter 20: thalamus), stove (Chapter
8: fornax) and distillery (Chapter 10: furnulus) are all found in the sixteenth

Of the different building materials he includes only lime in a separate

Picinelli does not mention the builder himself. Georgette de Montenay

the tools of her trade. The base of a pillar indicates the virtuousness of the

woman builds her house], a biblical quotation22 which recalls other biblical
metaphors of the house,23 conveys the thought that the queen is be praised
not only for the conduct of a good life; Georgette de Montenay alludes to
the support that Jeanne d'Albret provided to the Calvinist cause. By way of
contrast, Rollenhagen uses the figure of a builder (Fig. 16)24 to illustrate
the maxim that like any trade poetry has its own distinct rules.
The question of how architectural motifs are used in emblematics is
not easily answered. To begin with, we must understand that the architec
tural emblem as any emblem can fulfil a variety of functions. We can say
in brief that like other emblems those with architectural motifs can be used
as bearers of meaning for the purposes of moralistic teaching or can be
placed within the genre of panegyric. Georgette de Montenay presents the
motif of the builder in order to praise the queen of N avarra; she pursues her
panegyrical intentions while Rollenhagen expresses through the same motif
his ideas about poetry and to a certain extent appeals to his readers to regard
the rules of poetry in their own efforts.
The horizon of meaning in architectural emblems is not narrowly
confined since the emblematic subject can be presented in different
manifestations. Thus Georgette de Montenay symbolizes by two columns the
supremacy of faith in God over confidence in human strength (Fig. 17).25
One column with the inscription "Non confundit" [lt will not be destroyed]
is supported by the hand of God emerging from a cloud and resists the
tempest; the other bearing the inscription "Nolite confidere" [Do not trust]
seems to be held up by a human hand26 and breaks into pieces. The majori
ty of architectural emblems evoke the idea of firmness and constancy, but
this interpretation is in no way so prescribed as to be irreversible. Indeed,
architectural motifs can also be used to express the opposing notion.
However, some different tendencies can be observed. As we have noted
earlier, while column, pyramid and tower are interchangeable as emblematic

subjects, they represent some fundamental meanings. If the column is used
as emblematic main subject or as allegorical attribute and if it does not only
present the main subject it symbolizes above all constancy because of its
quality and not because of its function. This fundamental meaning is also
valuable for the tower, but which also can point to the dangers threatening
the great since the tower more often than other parts of buildings is hit by
lightening.27 Pyramid and obelisk show principally honorable distinction
and are determined in their meaning less by their quality than by their
representational function.
The motto usually determines the meaning of an emblem in a simple
and straightforward manner; by appropriate commentaries the meaning can,
however, be extended further. In this case the method of allegorization is
applied as a point by point exposition of a subject. In order to the honour
of the Saxon Elector Johann Georg III, Johann Franz Griendl von Ach
invented in 1680 a "Pyramis oder Ehren=Saule" [Pyramid or column of
honour]. But this pyramid is not constructed of breakable masonry but rather
of meaningful hieroglyphs. He takes each letter of the prince's name as an
emblematic picture as might be seen on the frontispiece of the work (Fig.
18).28 In the course of the work each letter is accompanied by a motto and
an epigram, which are explained in a commentary. Under the motto
"Nunqvam flexa, nunqvam fracta" [Never bent still less broken] there
appears the "I" of JOAN as a column, which is entwined by a scroll with
the name and title of the ruler, presenting the Saxon coats-of-arms (Fig.
19). 29 The epigram explains the column merely as a sign of exceptional
strength. In the commentary, however, Griendl von Ach identifies the cir
cumstances that moved him to choose the column as subject; since the
middle ages the electoral princes of the Holy Roman Empire were associated
with the metaphor of the pillars of the Empire.30 He reaches back to
Vitruvius's five orders of columns and identifies his subject as a composite
column. Just as this column excels all other orders of columns unifying in
itself the best qualities of the others so does the Saxon prince unite all the
virtues of his ancestors in himself. The different qualities of the column
parallel different virtues: the stability of the column announces the stead
fastness, its strength represents fortitude, its height bespeaks dignity, and so
on. Whereas the emblem itself symbolizes only one quality of the column,
the commentary develops nine different meanings. This example shows that
even the architectural motifs have many qualities which can be copiously
interpreted. Only a few of these qualities have been used in emblematics. A

To answer the question about the use of architectural motifs in
frontispieces I wish fo draw upon an example from English literature, Sir
Walter Raleigh's work The History of the World, the first edition of which
was published anonymously in 1614 (Fig. 20).32 The frontispiece is accom
panied by a sonnet by Ben Jonson, and it has been discussed in detail by
Corbett and Lightbown. 33 Its basic structure is shaped by architectural
motifs which provide the platform and frame for different personifications.
The building, which resembles a temple or memorial, consists basically of
four columns carrying a platform above a plinth. In the frieze of the
platform is engraved the book title, and in front of the plinth there is another
step bearing the imprint and date. On this step are lying the personifications
of death and oblivion, holding above them the central figure of the page, the
personification of History, called "Magistra vitae" [the mistress of life] in
the legend. She holds above her head the globe, and it seems that two wing
ed figures "Fama bona" [good fame] and "Fama mala" [ill fame] support the
globe. From centre top, the eye of providence looks down on the globe. In
the side niches, framed by the four columns, experience ["Experentia"] and
truth ["Veritas"] stand, which like all the other figures, are provided with
appropriate attributes and labeled with Latin legends. The columns are also
decorated and labeled. Looking from left to right, the first column is
decorated with books and labeled "Testis temporum" [witness of time], the
second, "Nuncia vetustatis" [messenger of antiquity], is replete with
hieroglyphs, the column of the light of truth is covered with flames, and the
fourth one is adorned with laure! branches and is called "VIT A
MEMORIAE" [life of memory]. Without doubt, this frontispiece presents
many allegorical motifs which I cannot consider here in any detail. On the
basis of Jonson's sonnet one would have to conclude that architectural motifs
have no referential allegorical meaning. Of the various parts of a building
Jonson mentions in his sonnet only the columns, which are, however, not
very important structurally-their load-bearing function is limited to sup
porting the upper platform-they are significant only as the bearers of the
four different titles that are assigned to History and also as the bearers of the
decorative motifs, which can be understood as allegorical attributes of these
titles. The architectural elements do not help us to understand this frontis
piece. They form only a decorative frame in which to exhibit the personifi
cations. While the y display the bearers of meaning and mark them as
decisive elements of the picture, they do not themselves convey any specific

meaning. At times it may be otherwise, as in the frontispiece to Francis
Bacon's Sylva Sylvarum, where one is able to identify the columns as the
columns of Hercules,34 but mostly the use of architectural motifs on
frontispieces does nothing more than provide a decorative framework. In
emblems, however, the reader can frequently assign allegorical qualities to
those motifs, if their function extends beyond that of filling the background.
There are, of course, further differences between emblems and allegorical
frontispieces, such as, for instance, the more complex structure of picture
and meaning in the frontispiece, but such an analysis would have to take into
account all kinds of images and not only architectural motifs. Such a consi
deration would fall outside the scope of the present reflections.

Respiciendo et Prospiciendo:
Political Allegories of Architecture and Sculpture on
the Frontispieces of Leoni's Editions of Palladio and Alberti
HANSJ.BÔKER
McGill University
In 1715, Giacomo Leoni (1686-1746), previously the court architect of the
Elector Palatine at Düsseldorf, and-as he proudly states to enhance his
authority in this matter-a Venetian by birth, published in English trans
lation the first of the Four Books on Architecture by Andrea Palladio.
Dedicated to his new sovereign, King George I (1714-27), it was to become
the starting point for a renewed interest in the architecture of Palladio that
dominated the entire Hanoverian period in English architectural history.
Subsequently, Palladian architecture became synonymous with the Georgian,
or Hanoverian, Style in England and her colonies, and was thus closely
related to the ruling dynasty. Architectural historians, however, tend to
disclaim any impact that the new sovereign from Hanover might have had
on architectural politics and rather attribute the introduction, or at least the
dissemination, of the new architectural idiom to the persona! taste of Richard
Boyle, 3rd Lord Burlington, who commissioned Isaac Ware to produce a
historically more correct, if not dogmatic, edition of Palladio's works in
1738.1
That the reception of Palladianism through Leoni's work was intended
to promote a decidedly new view of Palladio is best illustrated by the
portrait that precedes the first volume (Fig. 1). Alledgedly based on a
portrait of Palladio attributed to Paolo Veronese, it depicts the artist as a
rather young, clean shaven, self-confident man, not the bearded and bold
one known from all earlier publications. 2 Palladio thus becomes transformed
into a member of the modern, courtly society, and the whole publication
project is marked, not by the sort of antiquarian interest that characterized
the edition subsequently published by Lord Burlington (who corrected the
"error" by publishing the original portrait), but rather by an attempt to
modernize the Palladian idiom for its new court use and to shape it into a
style acceptable in early eigteenth-century architecture.3
The differences of intent are made very clear in the frontispieces of
the two editions. While Isaac W are copied for his publication-as others had

[Page 256]
232 Hans J. Baker
done before him-the original title page of 1570, Leoni's edition of Palladio
opens instead with an allegorical image that is programmatic in its allusion
to contemporary politics (Fig. 2). In this image, designed by Sebastiano
Ricci and engraved by B. Picart, the personification of Time-Kronos
pushes aside a heavy velvet curtain to reveal the bust of Palladio dressed
like a Roman statesman in a toga. Radiating from the royal order of St
George, which is elevated by a putto, a beam of light strikes the bust. To
the right, seated on a cloud, Britannia as ruler of the world points to the
coat of arms containing the heraldic representations of England / Scotland,
France, Ireland and the German territories of the Hanoverian house. At her
feet, Fama is sitting in a rather pensive mood, reminiscent of Dürer's
Melencholia. She rests her arm upon a book-clearly the works of Palladio
himself-and scattered around her are the tools of architecture, while she
holds in her hands a trumpet and a laurel wreath. The pattern of representa
tion follows the iconographical convention of allegorical depictions of rulers
in the Baroque period, and it is not surprising to see an immediate model for
it in the allegorical portrait of Ernest Augustus, the father of George 1. Far
from being a mere homage to the celebrated architect, the allegory has also
a concise political meaning: George I, as identified by the English order of
St George and British-Hanoverian heraldry, sheds a new light on the
authority of architecture, Palladio, who thus-in his turn-enlightens Great
Britain, or, as Wittkower phrased it, "the message is clear: it is in England
under royal patronage that Palladio's light shines once again. "4 In this
interpretation, the first Hanoverian ruler on the British throne becomes
identified as the initiator of a reception of Palladianism.
The concept propagated in this frontispiece stands in a marked
contrast to the traditionally accepted view of George's lack of interest in
artistic and architectural patronage, and especially in the Palladian style as
such; in this traditional view it would be seen as the visual language of the
English nobility opposing with its democratic or aristocratie tendencies the
inclinations of the Hanoverians towards continental absolutism, and thus to
the French Baroque style in architecture.
The recourse to the rôle of the sovereign as expressed in the frontis
piece, however, is intentional. That for Leoni and his contemporaries
Palladianism clearly had a decidedly princely connotation is illustrated by
the dedicatory preface which precedes three of the four books. Although the
intended audience was, as the list of subscribers proves, mainly the English
nobility-including, interestingly, "Sir Christopher Wren, Knight, surveyor
General of His Majesty's Work" (1632-1723)-the dedications are directed

In the first of these prefaces, dedicated to King George, Leoni states

This pronounced princely connotation of the architectural idiom is
clearly more than a mere attempt to flatter the new British sovereign. In
these prefaces, the authority of German princes-including the newly elected
English king-is used to propagate Palladian architecture amongst the
English aristocracy by creating the impression that the style had been firmly
established at German courts. This was to give, at the same time, a very
concise connotation to this style. By turning the Hamage à Palladio of the
frontspiece into a showpiece of political propaganda for the prince, the
architectural style represented by this architect, therefore, acquires a
decidedly political meaning, especially if one considers the fact that the
"classical language of architecture" served predominantly as the expression
of political authority. 9

Palliadian architecture. The isolation of a central motif as an abbreviation

gives this main motif a rather emblematic character denoting architectural
as well as socio-political order. As a sign for "House," sanctified by its
allusion to the Classical temple and equiped with the insignia of order and
power, the architectural motif refers as much to the ruling "House of

actually inhabited one of the villas built by Palladio, namely the Villa

while the garden façade had been remodeled previously in Palladian terms.

period, the erection of the catholic church St Clement. This church, situated
opposite to, and in view of the electoral palace on the west bank of the river

frontispiece. Alberti's own description of a building as including, among

THE INTELLECTUAL AND CULTURAL CONTEXT OF
ARCIDTECTURE AND EMBLEMATICS
Whereas the term architecture needs no clarification, "emblem" does.
In the study of art and literature "emblem" has corne to denote the illustrated
genre which combines symbolic visual image with a motto and epigram. If
understood broadly to include imprese, iconographs and allegorical personi
fications, the emblem may be regarded as a language of symbolic signs. As
such it informed the print and material cultures of the sixteenth to eighteenth
centuries. It is hardi y surprising that architects and patrons should have
made extensive use of emblematic forms to provide both decoration and
additional significance to their buildings.
Visual signs that represent social, religious, political and economic
institutions have been called insignia or emblems, although the emblem is,
properly speaking, a combination of symbolic visual sign and brief texts.
Such signs may be objects themselves-a crown, palm-tree or eagle-or a
picture or a word denoting the object. These signs evoke directly the thing
itself, and thereby the institution or idea or person associated symbolically
with that thing. Such signs include the emblems of secular and ecclesiastical
rank-crowns, hats, sceptres, and chains of office; aristocratie insignia such
as heraldic coats-of-arms, imprese, and monograms; symbols indicative of
social status-ritual apparel, head-dresses, and costumes; the insignia of the
army, guilds and trades; and, perhaps more important in terms of distribu
tion, the numberless emblems and iconographie personifications that convey
abstract notions. Thus the emblem in the material culture frequently defines
or marks cultural institutions, as well as being an expression of the social,
moral and religious order.
On the persona! level, wearing a crown, or bearing a shield may be
regarded as an artificial extension of that body, the very ornamentation
revealing the qualities of the person. As cultural constructions they may
refer to a larger and more abstract body such as a terri tory. The crown of
a prince may reveal his persona! status and power but it refers simultaneous
ly to his realm and rule. Thus emblems point beyond themselves in different
ways. Institutions dedicated to the virtues and qualities of their objectives or
missions are distinguished by boundaries, and they frequently receive special
expression through their buildings. There is an anthropomorphic and
symbolic dimension to architectural discourse of the period which frequently
also embraces a cosmological significance.

[Page 273]
The Emblem and Architecture 249
Architectural design and the creation of imprese and personal emblems
are acts of self-representation that may also incorporate symbolic structures
involving semiotic extension and closure. A building may extend the actual
realm or image of the patron by incorporating symbolically a larger, perhaps
more idealized order. Such an extension occurs under the patronage, or
under the aegis, of the patron. The phrases are telling: the aegis is protective
armour denoting impregnable defense. Thus placing the patron's shield or
arms over the main portal of a residence recalls the defensive or protective
purpose of the threshold, which allows movement between institutions.
Social closure could be reflected in architectural closure. As Biagioli notes:
"After 1550 court culture tended to close itself off (both culturally and
geographically from surrounding society with the focus increasingly on
itself, the prince or the culture of other courts. "1 The prince also became
the perfect map of this new culture; he was "the heart while the court
represented the limbs of civilized living. "2
Other institutions, such churches, libraries and townhalls, show in
varying degrees a similar interrelationship between architectural expression
and symbolic self-revelation.
PART 1
ORIGIN AND IDSTORY
Emblematic architecture depends for its creation and interpretation on
symbolic modes of thinking and expression. Thus the first sections deal
generally with allegory and in greater detail with the emblem.
As was noted in the Introduction, the emblem is an early modern form
of symbolic representation combining symbolic graphies with brief texts,
which also enriched the material culture into the twentieth century.
This section addresses two issues. First, the architectural and emble
matic representation of an individual or an institution usually involves an
idealized image based on earlier sources. The origin or source identifies the
model for such an image. Thus a present representation-a building and its

establish both claim and connection through the inherited virtues and

emulate the "truth" or to claim the virtues of a great ancestor or political

extension of origin, bath architectural and emblematic art may be regarded
as legitimizing the ties with a past that has an exemplary value. This applies
to heraldic signs, but also in a similar way to emblematic signs, which may
lack heritary power, but can lay claim to truth as signs.

MONUMENTAL ARCHITECTURE
In the material culture emblematic designs were incorporated into
almost every kind of art and artefact: they are encountered in painting and
portraiture, in wall and ceiling decoration, in carving, jewelry, tapestry and
embroidery, on household utensils and coins, on the arms and accoutrements
of an army. It cornes as no suprise to find them on public and private buil
dings. In fact, veritable emblem programs may be found adorning the walls
of many private residences, civic and ecclesiastical buildings.
Heraldic insignia and imprese were commonly incorporated into monu
ments. Family and communal insignia appear on palaces, altars, sarcophagi,
as well as on vaults, along the walls of rooms, façades of civil buildings, in
the halls of cloisters and universities, on portais and on city gates, aque
ducts, triumphal arches and streets. Monumental buildings, commemorative
structures and triumphal arches frequently have an emblematic function.
Renaissance and baroque symbology as expressed in the applied
emblematics of architecture was a language governed in part by the hierar
chy and etiquette of palaces, churches, and institutions. The various orders
of society, indeed differences in social status, were distinguished by a
person's dress and dwelling. Such enclosures reflect one's position within
a social hierarchy. The connection between "decoration" and "decorum"
informs the construction of an image, where image and enclosure are
frequently mutually defining.
The city could also be made over in the simulacrum of an idealized
order. Through architecture and emblematic decoration the city becomes a
body, like the body politic, vested in ritual garb, a referent of an ideal social
order. The body thus expresses and defines the limits of institutions. But it
also defines the "other," the allegorical order.
In the interiors of buildings an ostentatious display of coats-of-arms
could be used to support the daims of new families to pedigree. Elements
of the interior could become emblems of status. Ceilings and façades were
the frequently used for emblematic decoration and display. For example, the
Ufizzi façade was a genealogical table of Cosimo Medici, and it was ordered
as a governmental structure. Architectural proportion and !anguage create
an order that is expressive of Cosimo's cultural aspirations.
Interior structures often unfold a complex network of relationships.
From floor to floor, and from room to room, the histories of families are

created. In the centre or at the very top, in positions of distinction, there is
often a room that celebrates the head, the ruler or the founder of an institu
tion, or family. From this emblematic centre, the surrounding programs
unfold according to a patron's claims or aspirations. Often in this position
family shields are constructed in an iconographie program of apotheosis.
The surrounding allegorical figures legitimize the claims. In a building,
places of transition, such as stairs, can become the sites for the emblematic
elaboration of inter-connectedness or separateness. Stairs, portals, comices
can become elegant links expressing the language of mediation. In the cases
of powerful families it is possible to find connections between programmes
of various institutions set up under their patronage. Thus within a city entire
territ ories may be claimed as emblems of the ruling bouse or order. Again
the degree to which these idealized intentions are woven into the fabric of
the city is related to the strength and grandeur of their position within a
governing order.
