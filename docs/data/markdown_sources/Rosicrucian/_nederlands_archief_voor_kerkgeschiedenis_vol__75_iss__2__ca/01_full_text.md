The rehabilitation of Adam Haslmayr is a recent phenomenon. In the late
eighteenth century even his existence was doubted, and for the best part of
the nineteenth century scholars tended to deny that he was either of
importance or of interest. This changed with the bibliographical discoveries
of the late 1890s, but the process of appreciating and understanding Haslmayr
only really got under weigh after the Second World War with the studies of
Anton Ðörrer and, above all, of Walter Senn.
In Adam Haslmayr: Der erste Verkiinder der Manifeste der Rosenkreuzer Carlos
Gilly has made good use of the research of his predecessors and has added
an astonishing amount of material which he discovered himself. The result
is a book likely to remain the last word on Haslmayr for many years to
come, a study of exemplary scholarship containing new information and
placing Haslmayr in the broader context of the European Paracelsian
movement and of its corollary, Rosicrucianism.
Adam Haslmayr was born in Bolzano in the Tirol. It was there that he
studied music and became an organist before being employed as a Latin
teacher in 1588. Subsequently he was appointed imperial notary and, in 1592,
received a patent of nobility. The Tirol, an area whose mines and minerals
had fascinated Paracelsus at the beginning of the century, was filled with
followers of the great doctor. In 1594 Haslmayr himself fell under the
influence of his writings. Although officially a Catholic Haslmayr became
increasingly lax in his observances. With an ill-advised but no less constant
faith in the authorities, he dispatched a Paracelsian treatise he had written to
the emperor's brother, Archduke Maxmilian, governor of the Tirol. The
pamphlet was examined by a committee of Jesuits and Haslmayr, sum-
moned to Innsbruck, was warned to desist from his Paracelsianism.
After attempting to obtain work in Innsbruck and elsewhere, Haslmayr
settled in the nearby town of Schwaz, where he formed part of a large group
of Paracelsian physicians and alchemists and where he seems briefly to
have enjoyed the favour of the archduke, who was by no means opposed to
alchemy. Again, however, he fell foul of the authorities. He quarrelled with
the municipal doctor of Hall, the influential Hippolyt Guarinoni. He failed to
confess and communicate at Easter at a time when this compulsory
formality was rigorously observed. Worst of all, he travelled to Vienna in
1612 with a petition for the archduke whom he urged to protect the Rosi-
crucian movement. Maximilian, appalled by the discovery of a new heresy,
reacted to Haslmayr's letter by sentencing him to the galleys in the port of
Genoa.
Despite the gravity of his punishment Haslmayr remained unrepentant.
During the four and a half years which he spent chained to the oar he
extended his circle of friends and admirers and managed to do an amazing
amount of writing. Thanks to his friend Karl Widemann in Augsburg he
was already supported by Prince August von Anhalt who vainly petitioned
Maximilian for his release. He was in correspondence with Antonio de'
Medici in Florence, himself a keen alchemist, and he dedicated a work to
the grand duke of Tuscany, Cosimo II. But his greatest patron, to whom he

Once he was free Haslmayr avoided the Tirol and moved to Augsburg
where he could count on the friendship of Widemann. The Bavarian city
seems to have remained his base until his death in about 1630. He continued
to write prolifically on the subjects of alchemy and prophecy, defending the
Rosicrucians and opposing the "new prophets" of the Thirty Years War. The
one lesson he had learned from his experience in the galleys, however, was
that it was advisable to publish anonymously.
Besides telling the fascinating story of Haslmayr's life in a remarkably
readable manner, Carlos Gilly has provided a number of hitherto unknown
or misinterpreted documents which he has reproduced in full. First there is
the text of Haslmayr's reply to the Rosicrucians, the Antwort An die lobu>iirdige
Briiderschafft der Theosophen vom RosenCreutz which appeared in 1612, two
years before the publication of the Fama Fraternitatis. It is therefore the first
known reaction to the movement in Tübingen. Gilly discovered the work at
the Anna-Amalia-Bibliothek in Weimar, and the ten pages of the text are
reproduced in facsimile. The same treatment is accorded to the far longer
Nuncius Olympicus, which Gilly has identified as Widemann's catalogue of
Haslmayr's library, a text running to almost 50 pages and held at the Herzog-

numerous letters to Widemann, August von Anhalt and other admirers.
Haslmayr's significance has thus at last received full credit. The fact that
he was a Catholic gives an added dimension to Rosicrucianism, a movement
whose sympathisers have hitherto mainly been sought in the Protestant
camp. Gilly also provides an admirably lucid commentary to Haslmayr's
somewhat nebulous religious ideas. One of the main features of Paracel-
sianism, he points out, was an emphasis on the Bible as the sole source of
piety with an insistence on the role of the physician by no means limited to
the study of the body: he should, rather, also be an astronomer and a
theologian, capable of reading the divine signs in the heavens and of
warning humanity of the punishments in store. The new religion inspired
by Paracelsus was to supersede all existing confessions. It was to unite
Christians in a tolerant faith which would reveal the errors both of traditional
medicine and of traditional theology.

From the early sympathisers the catalogue passes on to the activists, the
little group of men at Tübingen responsible for the Rosicrucian manifestoes:
the doctor and jurist Tobias Hess, who inspired the movement; Johann
Valentin Andreae, mathematician, inventor and divine, who was the actual
author of the manifestoes; and the younger polymath Christoph Besold.
Besides a miscellany of works and letters by the three men, the exhibition
has an impressive selection of manuscripts and printed editions of the
manifestoes themselves, the Fama first printed (much against the wishes of
its author, as Gilly points out) in 1614, and the Confessiof irst published in the
following year.
The description of the 83 main items is followed by a list, with illustrated
frontispieces, of another 264 exhibits. These are some of the immense mass of
publications which Rosicrucianism provoked-translations, replies, defences,
discussions, attacks, produced throughout Europe and dating from the second
decade of the seventeenth century until the 1690s. Not only does this section
show how wide were the repercussions of the movement organised by the
three men in Tübingen, but it also gives an idea of the dimensions of the
Rosicrucian bibliography in which Carlos Gilly is at present engaged.
