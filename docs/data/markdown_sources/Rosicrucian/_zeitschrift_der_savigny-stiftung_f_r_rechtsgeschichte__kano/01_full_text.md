Industrious editors and publishers of the late fifteenth and sixteenth
centuries made great contributions to the history of law. Our knowledge
of medieval law would be impoverished without them. But, paradoxi-
cally, their work has also subtly impeded the study of the late medieval
Roman and canon lawyers. Historians have relied on their editions too
much and have only rarely compared the different printed editions
systematically or checked the printed texts against the manuscripts.
Recent studies have shown that the results can be surprising and im-
portant1). One may, perhaps, generalize and say that we should no

The most important works of Johannes Andreae have been printed
since the sixteenth century. Nonetheless, as Stephan Kuttner wrote
over twenty years ago, "it is not possible to draw up a reliable catalogue
of Johannes Andreae's writings ... Of his minor treatises ... our infor-
mation is haphazard and incomplete. Also for the major works a full
inventory and study of the manuscripts is needed"2). Cyprian Rosen
underlined Kuttner's point in a recent study on Johannes's Quaestiones
mecuriales, in which he presented evidence that in an earlier compilation
of his quaestiones, Johannes had arranged them under titles3). Indeed,
many of Johannes's quaestiones are separately transmitted in manu-
scripts and circulated independently of the final form he gave them
when he arranged them under the chapters of De regulis iuris. The
rubrics attached to some of these questions confirm Rosen's conjecture
that in an earlier work called Mercuriales, they were placed under
titles, not under the rules of law4). The rubrics also provide valuable in-
formation about the date and place where Johannes disputed a parti-
cular question5).

Philipps was the first to call attention to Johannes's Additiones17).
He had seen Munich manuscript, Clm 6351, and concluded that it con-
sisted of additions to the Novella. Gillmann also examined the manu-
script but argued that the work was a series of excerpts from Johannes's
Novella, compiled by a student18). He based his conclusions primarily on
a few instances in the manuscripts in which Johannes was referred to in
the third person. However, in works of this type it is not unusual to
find an author's name cited in the third person. This may only indicate
that the origins of the work lay in a reportatio. And even if it began as a
reportatio the versions we have are without a doubt from Johannes's
hand. He frequently employs the first person in the text (third person
references are very rare) and the material is Johannes's own. Further,
Gillmann did not notice that there are no references to the Clementines
in both recensions of the Additiones, which means they must have been
composed before ca. 1317. And all the references to events after 1317
which Gillmann used as evidence to date the Novella are missing. As we
shall see, the Additiones are neither additional glosses to the Novella nor
excerpts from them. Rather they are drafts of a gloss and commentary
on the Decretals that he wrote early in his career.

The priority of the earlier to the later recension of Additiones, and the
priority of the Additiones to the Novella, can be shown in two ways. In
most cases one can see a clear development of Johannes's ideas from
their first formulation in recension of Clm 6351 (henceforward Addi-
tiones I), their elaboration in that of Clm 14026 and 15703 (Addi-
tiones II), and their final form in the Novella25). Repetitiones that
Johannes included in both recensions also establish a guide to the
chronological and textual evolution of his commentaries.

Johannes's Additiones, repetitiones, and Novella offer us a rare view
of a canonist's maturation over a long period of time. The Additiones
are also important witnesses of a literary genre that has not received
much attention from legal historians. They also can provide an oppor-
tunity to study the development of Johannes's interest in the history
of canon law and in earlier glossators. Every folio of his work reveals his
engagement with the thought of his predecessors and the texts of earlier
collections. He must have owned glossed manuscripts of the Compi-
lationes antiquae, as he frequently made comments on Raymond of
Pennafort's editing of the Gregoriana and on the wording of the de-
cretals in the Antiqua (see above 337). He pursued and cited the
opinions of the early decretalists. The sigla of Johannes Teutonicus,
Tancred, Laurentius Hispanus, and Vincentius dot the pages of his work.
He probably possessed a respectable library; some of the works he
cites no longer exist. He referred, for example, to the opinions of Philip-
pus, a contemporary of Vincentius, whose commentary is no longer
extant. He had an interest in the history of his discipline and in the
history of canonistic thought. If he lived today, he would have made a
first-rate historian of medieval canon law.

Schulte noted that Johannes wrote Additiones to the Sext45), but
his Additiones and Apostillae to the Clementines are less well known. As
with the Additiones to the Decretals of Gregory IX, Johannes seems to
have written several different versions of them. Stephan Kuttner
noted a layer of additional glosses to the Clementines in Vatican MS
Arch. S. Petri A. 38 and concluded that Johannes had written Apostillae
to that collection46). A manuscript in Bamberg, written in Italy during
the fourteenth century, contains his Additiones to both the Sext and
Clementines47). The Bamberg commentary to the Clementines has much
of the same material (although it also differs considerably) that Kuttner
printed from the Vatican manuscript. A Munich manuscript contains
another work attributed to Johannes, Apostille Clementinarum domini
Johannis Andree doctoris decretorumK). This work has many references
to Ste., Stephanus Hugoneti49), and may be the work Zabarella saw
when he wrote that scripsit etiam Stephanus Prouincialis ; cuius dicta
uidit Johannes Andree post perfectum suurn commentum et hinc forte
motus nonnullas apostillas adiecit super hoc toto uolumine50). The attri-
bution of the work to Johannes in the rubric of the manuscript is also
supported by the methodology of the canonist. As in the Additiones to
the Decretals, Johannes glossed the decretal, then wrote additions to
the Ordinary Gloss, which he referred to as in glo. epy. (epilogues)51).
