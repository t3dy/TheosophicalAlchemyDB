The Rosicrucian Enlightenment. By FRANCES A. YATES. London: Routledge &
Kegan Paul, 1972. xv + 260 pp. £4-50.
Dr Yates loves a mystery. Following her highly original and enthralling work on
occult traditions during the Renaissance it was only logical that she should come
at length to one of the most obscure intellectual episodes in the next period: the
strange furore over the Rosicrucian literature which appeared in Germany just before
the outbreak of the Thirty Years War. Her new book offers a first serious treatment
of the subject in English and she makes full amends for the neglect. Without wasting
time over the outworn question whether a ' Rosicrucian brotherhood ' can ever have
had any physical existence, she sets out to investigate the mental climate from which
the manifestos proceeded and the response which they evoked. From a summary
of the three original texts — the Varna Fratemitatis (1614), Confessio Vraternitatis
(1615), and Andreae's Chymische Hochzeit Christiani Rosencreutz (1616) — she moves;
in ever wider circles to analyze the possible impact of their strange occult-alchemical
message. On the way we gain insight into some major debates: the origins of free-
masonry and the Royal Society; the philosophical world of Bacon and Descartes; the
Paracelsan revival and its consequences; the role of publishers and artists. The meat
of the problem, however, and the novelty of this approach lie in a more limited area:
the Holy Roman Empire on the brink of religious conflict. For Miss Yates the Rosicru-
cian writings are not just an upsurge of timeless mysticism, a youthful extravaganza
or an academic joke. They were, she argues, in main part conceived as propaganda for \
the cause of the Palatinate, especially for Frederick V's escapade in Bohemia. That
cause, welcomed by Protestants in Central Europe, and buttressed by the links with
England following on the Palsgrave's marriage to James I's daughter, is seen as not
only a campaign against Habsburg power, but a call for spiritual regeneration, intellec-'
tual advancement, and confessional harmony. We are introduced to its advocates at
the court of Heidelberg: the earnest young elector and his lively, intelligent wife;
his adviser Christian van Anhalt; and curious figures like Salomon de Caus and
ZinegrerL The failure of the Winter King, resulting largely from his father-in-law's
unwillingness to help, and the occupation of his electorate by Habsburg troops, cut
short the enthusiasm, obliterated its traces, and condemned it to centuries of •
unhistorical misunderstanding.
The theory is an attractive one, yet it presents grave obstacles. The Rosicrucians
have gained a historical background, but it is a sketchy one, based on well-known •
sources, like the backdrop to a contemporary court masque. Can we really treat the
Rhincland Calvinists so cavalierly? Although no adequate study exists, we do know
something about the climate of thought in the Palatinate before 1618. It was one of
open scholarship, tolerant and accommodating, but distinctly critical in its standards,
with Gruter, Parcus, and Lingelsheim as leading representatives. Curiosity certainly
existed, and irrational or unorthodox tastes could perhaps be indulged more freely
than elsewhere, but much further proof would be necessary to see there the centre
of a strong occultist movement. How anyway can we suppose that the Palatine
government commanded the loyalty of progressive spirits elsewhere in Germany, in

those Protestant areas which so notably stood aside in 1620? The first Rosicrucian
tracts appeared in Hesse-Cassel (which with its learned Landgrave Maurice certainly
merits research), and were taken up in other parts of the empire, especially Swabia.
Their support came primarily from convinced Lutherans like J. V. Andreae, and
drew on a mystic tradition which had unbroken antecedents back into the Middle
Ages. As Peuckert showed, some of the Weigelians and late sixteenth-century chiliasts
are distinctly Rosicrucian avant la lettre, while we can follow the same kind of pious
reconciliation theology (as Neveux has done) through the next 100 years to Leibniz
and Spener. Indeed the whole chronology of the Rosicrucian excitement: 1614 to 1620,
which lends striking prima facie support to Dr Yates's view, becomes rather less per-
suasive on closer inspection. Specialists have long recognized the links between the
fraternity of the Rosy Cross and the strange learned societies which subsequently
: sprouted up in Germany around August of Brunswick and Ludwig of Anhalt; they
have much in common, especially when the latter are divested of any anachronistic
' nationalist' layer of interpretation. More important, the Rosicrucians may plausibly
be argued to have themselves arisen only when real options became closed. As the
Empire moved ever nearer open conflict, so this esoteric movement brought a desperate
alternative to serious political involvement like that of Anhalt or Ludwig Camerarius.
Of course the Rosicrucians were dragged down by the outbreak of war and a general
hardening of official positions; like the Illuminati of Bavaria in the 1780s they were
fair game for the suspicion of conspiracy, and political enemies did their utmost to
associate them with international subversion, in this case with the militant Calvinist
camp. But such scares only act on existing fears, and do not reveal the true character of
the movements attacked. Moreover indirect testimony, whether from adversaries or
well-wishers, can rarely be decisive, especially when so much of it is given only
through allusion and symbol.
The author treads firmer ground when she concentrates on the two main Rosicru-
cian thinkers, Robert Fludd and Michael Maier, who epitomize for her the great
foreign influences on the movement and the whole Palatine position: England and
Bohemia. She rightly stresses the crucial part played by Britain in the politics of
Protestant Germany, the great expectations which men had of James I. There are
illuminating passages about England's cultural influence, above all on the imaginative
Andreae, who may well have been thus stimulated to some of his most powerful work.
But the anglomania can be exaggerated, and certainly not all of it flowed in occult
channels - perhaps the most powerful stimulus to the Rhineland was the intellectually
restrictive purkanism of Perkins and Whitaker. The problem emerges most clearly
with Miss Yates's central witness, John Dee. Thanks chiefly to her own work, that
brilliant and extraordinary Elizabethan is now rehabilitated in England where, as she
observes, his influence only declined when the Anglican scientific orthodoxy of the
1660s disowned him. Until then he remained an acknowledged authority on all kinds
of magic, mathematics, mechanics, and many arcane speculations. Was he as much
heeded on the Continent? Dr Yates has discovered his philosophy behind one of the
early Rosicrucian manifestos, and concludes that Dee inspired the whole movement.
The hypothesis is a bold one. His Monas Hieroglyphica indeed had several continental
printings — in fact all its editions were foreign (1564, 1591, then in the Theatra
Chymica of 1602 and 1613) — but it was a small book and highly obscure. Nothing else
by Dee appeared abroad, except a little posthumous edition of Roger Bacon's Epistola
de secretis operibus artis et naturae (Hamburg, 1616 and 1618), which Dr Yates

curiously does not mention, though it bears a dedication to the Rosicrucian Brethren.
This evidence suggests no great craze for Dee-literature; had there been one, writers
like Morhof, with his proclivity for the arcane, would surely have mentioned it. Dee's
spiritual successor Fludd achieved greated fame abroad, but he was anyway more
firmly schooled in the continental tradition; Dr Yates rightly points to the opposite •
current of the 1640s and 1650s when European occultism, as mediated by Fludd and
others, enjoyed a remarkable revival in Puritan England.
Consideration of Dee leads us to the other crux: Bohemia. Dee lived there in the
1580s and clearly believed that he had an occultizing mission, but again existing
evidence will not bear the weight of Miss Yates's interpretation. Nor can her stress
on the domestic legacy of the mysterious Emperor Rudolf II be allowed as it stands.
Much more remains to be known about irrationality in Bohemia and its influence on
Protestant leadership; the subject is exciting and important. Yet a gap will always
exist between the obscurities of this mentality and the active political contest for the
country in 1618-20, just as the habit of symbolism and emblematic expression, being
part of a whole late-Renaissance world, could have profound or trivial connotations
depending on the standpoint of the observer. It is significant that Anhalt and Rozm-
berk pursue alchemical or Paracelsan studies — deeply revealing of their personality
and age; it does not mean they are political agitators for a Hermetic reformation.
While we are still ignorant of much that went on in Prague during 1620, its leading
figures were assuredly men like Scultetus - destroyers of old images, not creators of
new. The point can be made with Budovec (not mentioned by Dr Yates), whose
deep spirituality allowed some sympathy for Rosicrucian enthusiasts, even for Dee,v
but who led the revolt because he associated true religion with the defence of estates'
rights. Above all we see it with Comenius. The Bohemian emigres were deeply
inclined to mysticism, and Comenius drew much from the Rosicrucian mood (Dr
Yates might have used the vast new biography by Milena Blekastad). But its role is
surely that of a refuge, a release from disaster, while the legacy of the Bohemian
Brethren represented far more positive Biblical and educational values; we should
beware of reading even too much chiliasm, let alone Hermetism or Cabalism, into the
sect before 1618, when it had its great practical opportunity.
This book's historical case therefore needs to be examined very carefully. The
author wisely disclaims anything more than the presentation of a hypothesis. Some
of her speculation simply goes beyond the evidence - like 'the suggestion that Vaclav
Hollar or the mature Descartes may have entertained secret sympathy for Rosicrucian
ideals; some reconsiders it without fully convincing — for example the analysis of the
word ' ludibrium ', especially in Andreae's later disavowals. Nevertheless we can
allow the general significance of a movement which Miss Yates's labour and inventive-
ness have recovered from obscurity for an English audience. Wherein then lies that
significance? Does the writer really sustain her defence of the so-called Rosicrucians,
her emphasis on their progressive features? She certainly plays down aspects of the
movement which contemporaries always noted, its secretiveness and irrationality.
Modern research has made clear that a true secret society never existed, thus leaving
room for a fairer appraisal (whereas the nineteenth century remained merely scathing).
But this can go too far: Dr Yates has paradoxically brought her occult movement so
far into the open as almost to rationalize it out of existence. Its supporters were after
all fanatics in an age of fanaticism, seeking initiation and hidden wisdom. The
evidence that they had advanced views on mathematics or mechanics, even on chemi-

cal processes, still looks thin; it is not to be outflanked by applying the term ' Rosicru-
cian ' to broader currents and more distant personalities simply because they held some
positions in common. The reaction of the 1620s represents much rather a step in the
direction of empirical science — witness the attitudes of Descartes and Mersenne — than
a witchhunt against an intellectual dawn which had been about to break. Dr Yates's
book has two virtues: the first is to have drawn out the Rosicrucians from the shadows
of unprofessional writing. The second is to reiterate her major though debatable pro-
position that the Hermetic-alchemical philosophy played a central role in the initial
stages of the scientific revolution. Both arc matters of great complexity; the attempt to
link them together is revealing above all for its limitations.

Anyone who knows anything about Scottish thought in the eighteenth century -
that ' Scottish Enlightenment' that has now become an academic development area,
thesis-prone and almost ripe for exploitation by contributors to slightly-less-than-
learned journals — knows that Henry Home, Lord Kames, is a central figure, who
had his finger in every pie, encouraged and promoted the work of Hume, Smith,
Millar and others and was for a time a kind of father-figure to them as well as to
Boswell. They may also know or suspect that to write a large book about him is not
a very enviable task. There is not the incentive or intellectual excitement as with, say,
Hume or Montesquieu or Voltaire. Kames is comparatively a secondary figure;
although he was a forceful personality and a man of Franklin-lie energy and applica-
tion, there is little drama, if any, in his life of law and letters and ' improvement';
his books cover a vast range of subjects, including agriculture, education, the minutiae
of Scots law, ethics, aesthetics, natural theology and what we would call sociology; he
does not write well and his thought tends to be sprawling and diffuse, not of the kind
that readily generates interesting lines of interpretation, but which gives rise to the
difficult and not very rewarding sort of questions as to who influenced whom and
how precisely x differs from y, questions which demand a very detailed knowledge
of a great many texts. He has for the most part attracted the attention of modern
scholars interested primarily in literature, criticism and aesthetic theory; it is his
Elements of Criticism that is best known, recent interest in him has been generated
in faculties of English, and that is Professor Ross's provenance. (W. C. Lehmann,
whose Henry Home, Lord Kames, and the Scottish Enlightenment was published in
1971, was a professor of sociology.) Professor Ross has exploited to the full the material
available, has made a very good job of mastering the Scottish background, especially
the background of Scots law, and has produced the next biggest biography, after
Mossner's ' Life of Hume', of any figure of the Scottish Enlightenment of the
eighteenth century. It is thorough, full, well-written and readable.
