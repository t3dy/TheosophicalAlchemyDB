# TheosophicalAlchemyDB Style Guide — Phase 1 Update

**Updated:** 2026-05-25 (Phase 1 Completion)  
**Applies to:** All figure biographies, concept definitions, text summaries, emblem essays

---

## Foundational Principles

The portal presents **rigorous scholarship on esotericism** combining:
1. **Historiographical integrity** — what sources actually say vs. what we wish they said
2. **Art-historical rigor** — visual analysis alongside textual (Szulakowska framework)
3. **Source documentation** — trackable genealogy of ideas (De Jong model)
4. **Embodied knowing** — practice, not mere theory (Zuber emphasis)
5. **Gender awareness** — who had access to knowledge, how transmission occurred
6. **Concept-first organization** — understand patterns, not just chronology
7. **History-of-science grounding** — For Paracelsian/alchemical traditions, root understanding in actual extant texts and careful philological study, not occultist reinterpretations or 19th-century amateur scholarship

---

## Historiography of Alchemy Framework

This portal adopts the **historiography-of-alchemy** approach established by:
- **Wouter Hanegraaff & ESSWE** — Western Esotericism as rigorous historical category; *esotericism* as knowledge claim, not pejorative
- **Allison P. Coudert & ASE** — Alchemy as integral to early modern intellectual history; scholarship exemplified by Association for the Study of Esotericism
- **David Porecca & Societas Magica** — Magical practices as part of systematic natural philosophy, not fringe superstition
- **Paracelsian scholarship** — Real alchemical knowledge grounded in medical practice, iatrochemistry, and empirical observation, not occultist fantasy

**Practical application to our entries:**
- **For Paracelsus & followers:** Root their thinking in actual medical theory, iatrochemical experiments, and texts; avoid conflating with later Romantic/Theosophical reinterpretations
- **For figures like Fludd/Khunrath:** Distinguish between what they actually wrote about cosmology/alchemy vs. how later occultists misread them
- **For emblems & visual analysis:** Analyze as texts designed for educated Renaissance readers (medical, philosophical, theological training), not as "secret symbols"
- **For concepts:** Trace back to medieval alchemy, natural philosophy, theology—not to modern psychology or New Age appropriation
- **For transmission:** Document actual manuscript chains, print history, translation lineages—not speculative influence

## Writing Standards by Entry Type

### Figure Biographies (500–1500 words)

**Structure:**
1. **Opening sentence:** Name, dates, nationality/location, primary significance in one sentence
2. **Life narrative:** Birth, education, key developments, major works, relationships
3. **Intellectual contribution:** What did this person teach/discover/practice?
4. **Historical context:** What were they responding to? What changed because of them?
5. **Scholarly reception:** How do modern scholars understand their work? Any debates?
6. **Transmission:** Who learned from them? How did their ideas spread?
7. **Closing:** Their place in broader genealogy

**Standards:**
- **Accuracy over heroics:** State what sources document; distinguish facts from legend
- **Multiple perspectives:** If scholars disagree, say so (Yates/Vickers model)
- **Living history:** Make the person real—what they struggled with, believed, valued
- **Relational:** Name other figures they connected to, concepts they developed
- **Gender attentiveness:** For women, note the obstacles they overcame; acknowledge gaps in documentation

**Example opening (actual standard we've established):**
> "Jacob Böhme (1575–1624) was a shoemaker in Görlitz whose mystical visions, documented in his writings, became foundational for spiritual alchemy. Unlike traditional alchemists focused on material operations or philosophers on theoretical exposition, Böhme described interior alchemical work in rich visionary language."

**Avoid:**
- ❌ Mystical reverence ("the great master Böhme")
- ❌ Unsubstantiated claims ("Böhme certainly influenced Hegel")
- ❌ Projection ("Böhme anticipated modern psychology")

### Concept Definitions (300–800 words)

**Structure:**
1. **Opening:** Etymology + one-sentence definition
2. **Laboratory meaning:** What does this term describe in actual practice?
3. **Philosophical meaning:** What principle does it represent?
4. **Historical development:** How has understanding of this concept evolved?
5. **Practical application:** How do practitioners use/work with this concept?
6. **Related concepts:** How does it connect to others?
7. **Modern relevance:** What do contemporary practitioners/scholars recognize in this?

**Standards:**
- **Dual level analysis:** Always explain both operational (what-you-do) and philosophical (what-it-means)
- **Etymological grounding:** Show where the word comes from; this clarifies the concept
- **Concrete examples:** Use actual alchemical processes, not abstractions
- **Avoid reductionism:** A concept is not "just psychology" or "just chemistry"
- **Acknowledge uncertainty:** If scholars disagree on meaning, say so

**Example (actual standard):**
> "Sublimation (from Latin *sublimare*, to elevate) is both a laboratory operation and philosophical principle central to alchemical understanding... In practical alchemy, sublimation is the process of heating a solid until it vaporizes directly... Philosophically, sublimation represents the apex of refinement: matter so elevated becomes spirit..."

**Avoid:**
- ❌ Reductive definitions ("Just heating a substance")
- ❌ Pure abstraction (no reference to actual practice)
- ❌ Modern psychological reinterpretation without historical grounding

### Text Summaries & Essays (400–1500 words)

**Structure:**
1. **Bibliographic header:** Title, author, date, language, significance
2. **Historical context:** When was this written? What was the intellectual climate?
3. **Content summary:** What does the text actually say? (Not what we wish it said)
4. **Argument or practice:** What is the text arguing for or teaching?
5. **Historical influence:** How did this work shape subsequent thinking/practice?
6. **Scholarly interpretation:** How do modern scholars understand its significance?
7. **Limitations or debates:** What controversies exist around this text?

**Standards:**
- **Primary source priority:** For foundational texts, show what the author actually wrote
- **Secondary source integration:** Use modern scholarship to contextualize
- **Historiographical honesty:** If the text was obscure until recently, say so
- **Practical application:** When applicable, note how practitioners used this text
- **Transmission tracking:** Document how this text was preserved, translated, copied

**Example (actual standard):**
> "The *Chymical Wedding of Christian Rosencreutz* (1616) is Johann Valentin Andreae's masterwork—an elaborate allegorical narrative... On one level, it is an adventure narrative: Rosencreutz journeys... On another level, it encodes the alchemical process: the seven-day celebration corresponds to the seven stages..."

**Avoid:**
- ❌ Modern reinterpretation dominating original meaning
- ❌ Treating legendary texts as historical fact (without qualification)
- ❌ Ignoring inconvenient historical details

---

### Emblem Essays (500–1000 words)

**Based on Szulakowska art-historical framework:**

**Structure:**
1. **Visual description:** What does the image show? Composition, elements, style
2. **Historical context:** When created, by whom, for what audience
3. **Alchemical significance:** What stage/principle does it illustrate?
4. **Mystical interpretation:** What is the spiritual meaning?
5. **Art-historical analysis:** How does it fit within emblem tradition?
6. **Contemporary influence:** How did later artists/thinkers use this image?
7. **Scholarly apparatus:** References, debates, sourcing

**Standards:**
- **Visual primacy:** Describe what you see before interpreting
- **Layered reading:** Emblems encode multiple levels; show the sophistication
- **Historical specificity:** What was this image doing in its moment?
- **Symbol literacy:** Understand how contemporary audiences "read" the image
- **Source tracking:** Document where this emblem appears, how it was transmitted

## Scholarly Voice Standards

### The Voice (Tone & Register)

**We write as:**
- **Learned but not pedantic** — accessible to educated non-specialists
- **Rigorous but not defensive** — confident in scholarship, open to debate
- **Respectful but not reverent** — treating historical figures seriously, not worshipfully
- **Precise but not mechanical** — clear writing that conveys complexity without jargon

**We sound like:** A careful scholar teaching serious students, not a textbook or a mystical guide

### Key Standards

**1. Historiographical Honesty**
- Distinguish between documented facts and reasonable inferences
- Acknowledge when evidence is scarce or ambiguous
- Present scholarly disagreements fairly (Yates vs. Vickers model)
- Note when modern interpretation projects onto historical sources

**2. Concept Precision**
- Define terms on first use
- Distinguish historical meaning from contemporary usage
- Avoid conflating different concepts under same label
- Clarify when terminology is anachronistic

**3. Gender Attentiveness**
- Name women's participation even when documentation is sparse
- Note obstacles women faced in accessing esoteric knowledge
- Recognize how gender inflected access to transmission
- Avoid invisibilizing women's contributions by defaulting to male pronouns

**4. Transmission Tracking**
- Show how ideas spread: who learned from whom, whose work was lost/recovered
- Document translation and reinterpretation across languages/cultures
- Acknowledge Islamic, medieval, and Renaissance sources for European esotericism
- Show continuities and breaks, not imagined unbroken lineages

**5. Practical Grounding**
- When discussing alchemy, reference actual operations not just theory
- For spiritual practice, acknowledge it requires sustained engagement
- Distinguish between what practitioners *believed* and what modern science confirms
- Avoid claiming ancient knowledge "anticipated" modern science

**6. Acknowledgment of Limits**
- Say "unknown" when sources don't tell us
- Mark speculation as such
- Note when documentation is poor for women, non-Western, or marginalized figures
- Acknowledge what later scholarship revealed about earlier misunderstandings

## Phase 1 Scholarly Values Incorporated

### Based on Contemporary Authorities

**From Urszula Szulakowska (*Art and Alchemy*):**
- Visual-symbolic analysis; emblems as philosophical instruments
- Art-historical methodology for understanding alchemical imagery
- Attention to color, composition, symbolic conventions
- Recognition that Renaissance emblem books encoded sophisticated thought

**From Mike Zuber (*Spiritual Alchemy*):**
- Embodied practice emphasis; theory means nothing without practice
- Transmission genealogy: Böhme → Swedenborg → Blavatsky → modern esotericism
- Integration of gender perspectives on alchemy and transmission
- Recognition that "spiritual alchemy" represents legitimate historical tradition

**From Joscelyn Godwin (*The Theosophical Enlightenment, Rosicrucian Trilogy):**
- Genealogical/transmission approach; understanding ideas through their movement
- Integration of multiple traditions; no pure origins
- Recognition of Islam, medieval Europe, Renaissance as part of same narrative
- Modern esotericism as inheritor of Renaissance magic

**From H.M.E. De Jong (*Michael Maier's Atalanta Fugiens*):**
- Source documentation; careful reading of what texts say
- Technical knowledge: understanding actual alchemical operations
- Layered interpretation; philosophical meaning shouldn't obscure practical meaning
- Scholarly apparatus: citations, debates, careful argument

**From Noel Brann, David Kuntz, Nicholas Goodrick-Clarke (contemporary scholarship):**
- Historiographical rigor; distinguishing facts from legends
- Attention to social history; who participated, who was excluded
- Recognition of women's roles despite historical marginalization
- Integration of occultism into intellectual history (not separate phenomenon)

## Specific Writing Conventions

### Names & Titles
- **First mention:** Full name with dates, e.g., "Jacob Böhme (1575–1624)"
- **Subsequent mentions:** Last name only or "he/she"
- **Non-European names:** Use period-appropriate spelling, note translations

### Dates & Places
- **Dates:** Always include birth–death years; ? when uncertain
- **Places:** Include modern equivalents for clarity, e.g., "Görlitz, Germany (now Poland)"
- **Works:** Give publication year and, when relevant, original language

### Quotations
- **Direct quotes:** Use when the phrasing is significant or disputed
- **Paraphrasing:** Preferred unless the original language matters
- **Attribution:** Always cite source with page number

### Concepts & Technical Terms
- **First use:** Define clearly or explain etymology
- **Consistency:** Once defined, use consistently throughout the portal
- **Relationships:** Show how concepts relate to one another
- **Etymology:** Show where terms come from; this clarifies meaning

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

❌ **Orientalism:** Treating non-Western esotericism as exotic mysticism rather than serious philosophy  
❌ **Anachronism:** Describing medieval thinkers with modern psychological vocabulary  
❌ **Retroactive validation:** Claiming alchemists "anticipated modern science" (science did different things)  
❌ **Gender erasure:** Making women invisible by using male pronouns for mixed groups  
❌ **Overstating certainty:** Presenting scholarly debates as settled fact  
❌ **Reductionism:** "It's really just psychology" or "It's really just chemistry"  
❌ **Mystification:** Writing in vague, reverent language to seem esoteric  
❌ **Intellectualism:** Writing as if practice doesn't matter, only theory

Before finalizing, verify:

- [ ] **Accuracy:** Facts are sourced; speculation is marked as such
- [ ] **Completeness:** Main ideas are explained; connections are clear
- [ ] **Voice:** The writing is clear, not mystical or condescending
- [ ] **Context:** Historical situation and significance are explained
- [ ] **Relationships:** Connections to other figures/concepts are shown
- [ ] **Gender:** Women's roles acknowledged; obstacles noted
- [ ] **Transmission:** How ideas spread is documented
- [ ] **Limitations:** Gaps in knowledge or scholarly disagreements noted
- [ ] **Practicality:** For practices, grounded in actual doing not theory
- [ ] **Scholarship:** Modern interpretations acknowledged and credited

## Examples of Exemplary Writing (Phase 1)

**Figure biography (Zosimos):**
> "Zosimos of Panopolis (c. 300–400 CE) is the earliest surviving systematic alchemical author and represents the crucial transition from practical craft knowledge to philosophical spiritualized alchemy. Writing in Greek in Hellenistic Egypt, Zosimos documented both laboratory operations (metallurgical processes, distillation, color transformations) and mystical interpretations..."

*Why this works:* Opens with clear dates/significance; establishes historical context; acknowledges both practical and philosophical dimensions; positions him as a crucial transition point.

**Concept definition (Correspondence):**
> "Correspondence (from Latin *correspondere*, to agree or match) is the hermetic principle that the universe operates through systematic likeness: the divine pattern appearing at every level of manifestation... Understanding correspondences enables several things: (1) mystical knowledge...; (2) magical practice...; (3) alchemical insight..."

*Why this works:* Etymology grounds the concept; multiple meanings explained; practical applications shown; avoids reducing to single dimension.

**Emblem essay opening (Cramer's Cross Within Circle):**
> "Cramer's fundamental emblem of unity presents the circle—symbol of perfection, eternity, and divine totality—containing the cross, which represents the intersection of opposites and the axes of creation. The geometric precision indicates not abstract mysticism but mathematical theology..."

*Why this works:* Visual description first; historical context (Cramer, Frankfurt); philosophical meaning explained; avoids mystification through careful rational exposition.

## Phase 2 Scholarly Framework (NEW)

### Zuber Emphasis: Embodied Practice

**Core Teaching from Zuber's *Spiritual Alchemy*:**
All alchemical knowledge is grounded in actual practice. Theory disconnected from lived transformation is mere intellectualism.

**How to Apply:**
1. For concept definitions: Always include operational dimension (what practitioners literally DO)
2. For figure biographies: Show HOW they practiced, not just what they believed
3. For emblem essays: Connect symbolic meaning to actual laboratory procedures or contemplative practice
4. For all entries: Ask "What does a practitioner actually engage with?"

**Example Structure for Concepts:**
- Paragraph 1: Operational (practical, laboratory, lived)
- Paragraph 2: Philosophical (principle, symbolic)
- Paragraph 3: Spiritual (transformational, consciousness)
- Closing: Modern practice application

### Akerman Emphasis: Historiographical Rigor

**Core Teaching from Akerman's Scholarship:**
Distinguish documented facts from legend. Present scholarly disagreements fairly. Acknowledge what evidence cannot support.

**How to Apply:**
1. Mark unsubstantiated claims as such
2. When scholars disagree, document multiple viewpoints
3. Note gaps in historical evidence (especially for women)
4. Avoid false certainty—present possibilities, not definites
5. Credit sources explicitly

**Example Language:**
- "Sources document..." (verified)
- "Tradition holds..." (unverified legend)
- "Scholars disagree whether..." (genuine debate)
- "Evidence is scarce..." (honest gap)

### Szulakowska Emphasis: Visual-Symbolic Analysis

**Core Teaching from *Art and Alchemy*:**
Emblems and visual elements are philosophical instruments, not mere illustrations. They encode sophisticated arguments through visual means.

**How to Apply:**
1. For emblem essays: Start with visual description, then interpret
2. Color analysis: understand symbolic meaning (nigredo blacks, albedo whites, rubedo reds)
3. Composition: notice symmetry, hierarchy, perspective
4. Visual conventions: understand Renaissance emblem tradition
5. Layered reading: emblems encode multiple meanings simultaneously

**Example Analysis Structure:**
- Visual elements (what we see)
- Art-historical context (tradition, convention)
- Alchemical meaning (stage, principle)
- Philosophical significance (what it teaches)
- Influence (how later thinkers reinterpreted)
